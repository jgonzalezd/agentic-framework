## Ladder arithmetic

**Two rungs, one of them free, and no annual option verified — so most checks are unrunnable and the page is thin on arithmetic by design.**

**What can be computed:**

**Annualised, Pro is $239.88/year** — derived, not displayed. Abby never annualises, and there is no annual plan to compare against. **In a neighbourhood where every peer divides the annual number down to a monthly one, Abby simply has no big number to divide.**

**Price positioning against the verified bands:**
- **$19.99/month sits at the top of the AI-advice cluster of $8.99–$19.99**, level with Talkspace's "Tee AI Plan" and above `rosebud.md` at $12.99 monthly / $8.99 annual-equivalent.
- Against `rosebud.md`'s annual rate, Abby is **2.22× more expensive per month** — and offers no annual discount to close the gap.
- **Against the one-off artefact band ($9.95–$36), a single month of Abby costs roughly what a one-off report costs.** A buyer weighing "one hard conversation" against "a report about myself" faces near-identical prices with completely different commitment shapes.
- Against therapy: a single market-rate session is **$100–$200** (Psychology Today), so **five months of Abby ≈ one session.** The page never makes this comparison — **in fact it makes the opposite move, disclaiming the substitution outright.**

**The free-tier fence is a capability fence, not a volume fence.** "Text mode, basic support, and guided self-reflection exercises" describes what the free user *gets*, and by omission what they do not — voice, presumably, and deeper support. **Nothing is counted anywhere on the page.** No credits, no message caps, no session limits, no multipliers. In a category where every AI product publishes *some* unit (`gamma.md`'s credits, `claude-anthropic.md`'s session multiples, `perplexity.md`'s "4,000 bonus credits"), **Abby publishes none — and it is the only one whose page is not worse for it**, because the fence is a feature list the buyer can read.

## Social proof, trust and objection handling

**No user counts, testimonials, ratings or logos observed.** This is unusual even for the AI cohort, and it leaves the disclaimers carrying the entire trust load — which, unexpectedly, works.

**The three disclaimers are the trust mechanism:**

> **"Abby is not a licensed therapist, psychologist, or psychiatrist"**
> **"Abby cannot diagnose mental health conditions"**
> **"not a replacement for professional therapy or clinical care"**

**Stating what the product is *not* makes what it *is* more credible.** Compare `rosebud.md`, which hedges in the same direction but does so quietly while letting testimonials assert the substitution. Abby's version is louder, first-party and unhedged — and it costs nothing, because a buyer looking for a licensed therapist was never going to convert anyway. **The disclaimer disqualifies a buyer the product cannot serve and reassures the one it can.**

## What they do that most pages don't

**They write the value proposition as a list of the user's situations, not the product's features.**

> "Whether you're **overthinking a relationship**, **trying to make a decision**, or **just need to get something out of your head**, Abby helps you talk it through."

**It enumerates the user's states and only then names what the product does.** Every clause before the verb is something the buyer has actually experienced and can recognise; the product appears last, as the resolution. Compare the tier-descriptor slot in `notion.md` ("For growing businesses to streamline teamwork") — one is a mirror, the other is a taxonomy.

**They disclaim aggressively and gain from it.** Three plain-English statements of what the product is not, on the homepage, above the fold of the trust section.

**They publish no unit and lose nothing.** Because the product is one action — a conversation — repeated indefinitely, there is nothing to count, and Abby resists inventing something. **Compare `perplexity.md`, which borrowed credit vocabulary without a credit-shaped problem and produced the least legible unit communication in the corpus.**

## What transfers, and what doesn't

**Almost everything on this page transfers, because there is almost nothing on it that requires scale.** No logo wall, no science page, no enterprise tier, no credential — a two-rung freemium page with one very good sentence.

**Category-driven:** the safety disclaimers are specific to operating adjacent to mental health. Their *form*, though, is general — see below.

**Genuinely universal:**
1. **Write the value proposition as a list of the buyer's situations.** "Whether you're X, Y, or just Z, [product] helps you…" is a reusable structure, and the clauses should be things the buyer has said to themselves, not things you say about your software. **This is the single most copyable sentence in the decision-help neighbourhood.**
2. **Say what you are not.** Explicit disclaimers read as candour, disqualify buyers you cannot serve, and make the remaining claims more believable. This generalises well beyond wellness: "this is not accounting advice", "this will not replace a lawyer", "this does not do X" all buy the same credibility.
3. **Do not invent a unit you do not need.** If your product is one repeated action with near-zero marginal cost, a feature fence is cleaner than a credit system — and it imports none of the rollover, expiry, top-up and forfeiture questions that follow a meter. (See `tinywow.md` for the same choice made deliberately at $20/month.)
4. **Describe the free tier by what it includes.** "Includes core features including text mode, basic support, and guided self-reflection exercises" is a more generous-sounding sentence than any cap, and it costs nothing to write that way.
5. **The gap this page shares with its whole cohort is the opportunity.** **Not one verified AI advice product in this corpus sells a one-off** — while the human marketplaces have proven the transaction at $39–$500 (`mentorcruise.md`, `intro-co.md`). Two readings are possible: an open gap, or an idea the category has already tested privately and rejected. **The evidence available here cannot distinguish them**, and the answer materially changes how a decision-help product should be priced.
