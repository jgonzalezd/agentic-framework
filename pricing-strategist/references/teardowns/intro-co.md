## Ladder arithmetic

**There is no ladder, and that is the finding.** The price range is **$69–$2,500 — a 36× spread for what is nominally the same unit: one 1-on-1 video session.** No volume discount exists, no package exists, no per-minute rate is published, and no add-on can be stacked.

**The distribution matters more than the range.** The observed prices cluster at **$100–$500**, with the tails ($69 at the bottom, $2,500 at the top) thinly populated. **The working band for a named-human single session is $100–$500**, and the extremes are positioning rather than market.

**What the 36× spread actually prices.** An identical thirty minutes costs $69 or $2,500 **purely by whose face is on it.** There is no capability difference on the page, no deliverable specification, no duration difference recorded in the research. **The credential is the entire price justification** — which is precisely why the price can sit in the open, before any commitment.

**Does any add-on invert the ladder?** No add-ons exist.

**Does a lower tier plus add-ons reach a higher tier?** N/A — there is nothing to stack, and stacking cheap sessions does not produce an expensive one, because the expensive one is a different person.

**No conversion-to-recurring arithmetic exists**, because Intro does not attempt it. **The business accepts the one-off as terminal and prices for it.**

**Counterfactual arithmetic, done by the testimonials rather than by the page.** Users cite **"$14,000"** saved on interior design and **"$600+"** on fashion advice. Against a session in the $100–$500 band, those are **28× to 140×** and **1.2× to 6×** returns respectively — computed by the buyer, from the customer's own numbers, without the company making a claim.

## Social proof, trust and objection handling

**Trust is entirely borrowed from the expert's public identity** — named individuals, photographs, existing fame. There is no platform-level credential claim, no vetting badge, no satisfaction percentage. Intro does not need one; the buyer already knows who Nate Berkus is.

**Social proof is framed as counterfactual savings rather than satisfaction.** Testimonials cite **"$14,000"** saved on interior design and **"$600+"** on fashion advice, with **named locations** (Los Angeles CA, Chicago IL, Houston TX, New York City, Newport Beach CA).

This is a materially different rhetorical move from "it helped me". **"I saved $14,000" makes the session pay for itself inside the testimonial**, and it does so with a number the company never has to assert.

- **Price-forward, no gating.** The price is printed on the expert's card **in browse view, before clicking, signing up or disclosing anything.** The exact opposite of `justanswer.md` and `betterhelp.md`.
- **ROI framing over outcome framing** in testimonials.
- **Named-human premium** — a 36× spread on an identical unit, justified entirely by identity.
- **No conversion attempt to recurring.** The one-off is treated as terminal and the business is priced to survive it.
- **Life-domain category taxonomy** ("Career & Business", "Home Decor", "Wellness, Astrology & more") rather than product-feature taxonomy — the buyer navigates by their situation.

## What they do that most pages don't

**They put the price first, before anything is invested.** The number sits on the browse card ahead of the click. **This is viable because the price is attached to a named person whose face and reputation justify it** — the credential does the price-justification work, so the price does not need hiding. Where a company has no such credential, hiding the price is a temptation; where it has one, hiding the price is a waste.

**They let the customer state the ROI.** "I saved $14,000" is a claim the company could never make in its own voice about a 30-minute call. Placed in a testimonial with a name and a city, it is unfalsifiable and unobjectionable.

**They accept that the one-off does not convert.** No upsell, no membership, no "book 5 and save". A business that has decided its transaction is terminal can price it properly instead of pricing it as a loss-leader for a subscription that will never arrive.

## What transfers, and what doesn't

**Fame-driven, and this is the hard limit.** Intro's entire structure rests on **borrowed credibility from named individuals.** Take away the recognisable face and the $450 card becomes an unexplained number attached to a stranger. A product with no famous name attached **cannot** simply copy the price-forward posture and expect the price to justify itself — it must supply a different justification. The two available substitutes are visible elsewhere in this cohort: **quantified platform-level proof** (`mentorcruise.md`: "97% satisfaction rate", "42,000+ Matches made") and **obsessive deliverable specification** (`one-off-report-market.md`: "over 2,500 words each", "available in a PDF format").

**Genuinely universal:**
1. **Put the price on the landing page.** The gating strategies in this neighbourhood (`betterhelp.md`, `noom.md`, `calm.md`) are measurably resented — 39% of surveyed BetterHelp users "wanted more immediate information about pricing" — and transparency is a live, occupied differentiation position available at zero cost.
2. **Let the customer make the ROI claim you cannot.** A counterfactual saving quoted by a named buyer is the strongest price justification available to a product that cannot cite a credential, and it is the one rhetorical move nobody else in this neighbourhood makes.
3. **If the one-off is terminal, price it as terminal.** Designing a $29 artefact as a loss-leader into a subscription that the market has shown will not arrive (see the cross-cutting one-off analysis) produces a product that is bad at both jobs.
4. **Navigate by the buyer's situation, not your feature set.** "Career & Business", "Home Decor", "Wellness" are the categories the buyer already uses to describe their own problem.

**Market ceiling, for calibration:** a **named-expert single session bears $69–$2,500, clustering $100–$500.** That is the top of the verified band for a single piece of decision help. The bottom — a **no-human, self-serve, structured artefact — bears $9.95–$36** (see `one-off-report-market.md`). **The middle, roughly $36–$39, is structurally empty**, and it is where the buyer's mental comparison flips from "a report" to "a person".
