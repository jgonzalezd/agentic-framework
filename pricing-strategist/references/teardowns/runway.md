## Ladder arithmetic

**Price per credit at each rung.**

| Tier | Monthly | Annual/mo | Credits/mo | **$/credit (monthly)** | **$/credit (annual)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | $15 | $12 | 625 | **$0.02400** | **$0.01920** |
| Pro | $35 | $28 | 2,250 | **$0.01556** | **$0.01244** |
| Max | $95 | $76 | 9,500 | **$0.01000** | **$0.00800** |

**Discount, entry to top: 58.3%** ($0.02400 → $0.01000) across a **15.2× credit range** (625 → 9,500).

**The per-step discount is unusually even.** Standard → Pro is **35.2%** and Pro → Max is **35.7%**. Almost every other ladder in the corpus front-loads its discount into the first step and then flattens or inverts — `gamma.md` gives 50% at Plus → Pro and exactly 0% at Pro → Ultra, `midjourney.md` gives 34.0% at Basic → Standard and exactly 0% above it, `bannerbear.md` gives 69.6% then goes 0.34% backwards. **Runway is the only ladder captured in this corpus that discounts at a near-constant rate at every step**, which means a buyer who computes the per-unit price at any rung gets the same answer about whether climbing is worth it.

**Annual discount, checked at every rung.** $12 ÷ $15 = 0.80. $28 ÷ $35 = 0.80. $76 ÷ $95 = 0.80. **Exactly 20.0% at all three rungs, uniform and unhedged.** Note that no savings expression was captured, so Runway is discounting 20% without a captured claim attached to it. Annual totals are **$144, $336 and $912** — derived, not displayed.

**Buyer-frame conversion, using the published rates.** This is the section most credit pages make impossible, and Runway makes it easy.

| Tier | Credits/mo | **Gen-4.5 video at 60 credits/5s** | **Gen-4 Images at 8 credits each** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free | 125 (once) | **2.1 clips ≈ 10 seconds** | **15 images** |
| Standard | 625 | **10.4 clips ≈ 52 seconds** | **78 images** |
| Pro | 2,250 | **37.5 clips ≈ 187 seconds** | **281 images** |
| Max | 9,500 | **158.3 clips ≈ 13.2 minutes** | **1,187 images** |

**Cost per second of generated video**, from the same peg (60 credits per 5 seconds = 12 credits/second): Standard **$0.288/second**, Pro **$0.187/second**, Max **$0.120/second**. A buyer who knows how long their piece is can price it before subscribing, which is exactly what `perplexity.md`'s "4,000 bonus credits" and `gamma.md`'s explicitly variable rate deny their buyers.

**The free tier, computed.** 125 one-time credits at Standard's $0.02400 is **$3.00 of value**, given once and never refilled. In video terms it is **two five-second clips**. That is enough to see the output quality and nowhere near enough to finish anything, which is the correct calibration for a product whose marginal cost is real.

**Does any add-on invert the ladder?** **Not computable — the top-up rate is not published.** Runway surfaces the existence of top-ups and withholds the price, the same choice `descript.md` and `gamma.md` make. `midjourney.md` and `talkspace.md` are the only pages in the corpus that publish both halves.

**Does a lower tier plus top-ups reach a higher tier?** **Not computable for the same reason, but the page installs a guard that works regardless of the rate.** The **minimum purchase is 1,000 credits**, which is **1.6× the entire 625-credit monthly allowance of the Standard plan**. A Standard customer who runs out cannot make a small top-up; the smallest purchase available to them is larger than everything their plan includes for the month. Against Pro's 2,250 the same minimum is 44.4% of a month, and against Max's 9,500 it is 10.5%. **The minimum-purchase rule is therefore a tier-graduated upgrade nudge written as a single number**, punitive at the bottom of the ladder and irrelevant at the top, with no per-tier rule anywhere.

**The three-way expiry asymmetry, and it is the most transferable idea on this page.** Standard and Pro plan credits **"reset within 24 hours of your billing date"**. Max plan credits **"roll over to the following month"**. Purchased credits **"never expire"**. Read those three sentences together and two separate pricing decisions fall out of them:

1. **Rollover is sold rather than given.** It is a Max-only privilege, so a Standard customer wanting their unused credits to survive the month has an upgrade path that costs $95 and has nothing to do with volume. `elevenlabs.md` gives rollover to every paid tier and caps it; Runway charges for it.
2. **Purchased credits are property; plan credits are a lease.** Permanence is a non-price justification for charging above the plan rate on a top-up, and it lets the seller do so without the word "penalty" appearing anywhere. `midjourney.md` reaches the same structure independently — "extra fast hours cost $4 each and do not expire" — which is the strongest evidence in the corpus that this is a real pattern rather than a quirk.

## What they do that most pages don't

**They publish what a credit buys, in three different units, on the pricing page.** Most credit sellers in this corpus either refuse to peg the rate (`gamma.md` states outright that consumption "can vary based on what you're doing, which AI model is used, and the length or complexity of the generation") or peg it and leave the buyer to do the conversion (`elevenlabs.md`'s "1 text character equals 1 credit"). Runway publishes three separate rates for three separate output types, which is the only honest way to describe a meter whose consumption genuinely varies by workload.

**They charge for rollover.** Every other credit product in the corpus treats rollover as a customer-friendly concession. Runway treats it as a feature with a price, and puts it on the rung it wants to sell.

**They set the top-up minimum above the entry plan's monthly allowance.** This is a one-line rule that produces graduated upgrade pressure without a per-tier table, and it works whether or not the top-up rate is ever published.

## What transfers, and what doesn't

**Scale-driven:** the 15.2× volume range and the $95 top rung belong to a company selling to production teams. A small product will not have three rungs that far apart.

**Category-driven:** the per-output-type conversion table only makes sense when the outputs genuinely differ in cost by an order of magnitude, as video and text-to-speech do.

**Genuinely universal, and there are three:**

1. **If your meter's consumption rate varies by workload, publish a rate for each workload rather than refusing to publish any.** The alternative that most pages choose is a sentence explaining that the rate varies, which tells the buyer nothing and prevents them from sizing the plan before they buy.
2. **Make purchased units permanent and plan units expire.** It justifies a top-up priced above the plan rate as a trade for permanence rather than a fine for overuse, and it stops the two credit types being directly comparable at all.
3. **Set the minimum top-up at or above your entry plan's whole monthly allowance.** One number, no per-tier rules, and it makes the top-up structurally unable to function as a cheap permanent substitute for upgrading.
