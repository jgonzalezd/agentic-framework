## Ladder arithmetic

**The discount is exactly 20% on both tiers, and it checks out.** $35.00 ÷ $174.99 = **20.00%**. $20.00 ÷ $99.99 = **20.00%**. Both match the banner's "20% discount" and the coupon name `blink20`. **The savings badges are honest arithmetic on a stated list price** — unusually, the anchor and the claim reconcile.

**Effective monthly rates.** PRO $139.99 ÷ 12 = **$11.67/month**; PREMIUM $79.99 ÷ 12 = **$6.67/month**. At renewal: PRO $174.99 ÷ 12 = **$14.58/month**; PREMIUM $99.99 ÷ 12 = **$8.33/month**.

**The tier ratio is constant.** PRO ÷ PREMIUM = $174.99 ÷ $99.99 = **1.75×** at list, and $139.99 ÷ $79.99 = **1.75×** at promo. The discount is applied uniformly, so the relative positioning of the two tiers is preserved regardless of which price the buyer sees. **A 75% premium is being charged entirely for AI capability** — summarising PDFs, YouTube and podcasts — on a product where the core library is identical.

**Against the unshown monthly** `[secondary, "roughly $15.99/month" = $191.88/yr]`:
- PREMIUM at $79.99 saves **58.3%**; the monthly-to-annual-equivalent ratio is **2.40×**.
- PRO at $139.99 saves **27.0%**; the ratio is only **1.34×**.

**So the annual case is overwhelming for PREMIUM and merely good for PRO** — which is precisely why the monthly option is not shown. Displaying it would make PRO's annual discount look thin while making PREMIUM look like the obvious buy, and PREMIUM is the cheaper product. **Deleting monthly protects the expensive tier, not the annual commitment.**

**Year-two arithmetic, disclosed rather than hidden.** A PRO buyer pays $139.99 in year one and **$174.99 in year two — a 25.0% increase**, stated in the same sentence as the discount. A PREMIUM buyer goes $79.99 → $99.99, also **+25.0%**. The intro discount is a one-year instrument and the page says so.

**Does any add-on invert the ladder?** No add-ons exist.

**Does a lower tier plus add-ons reach a higher tier?** No stacking path. The AI fence is binary — you have it or you do not.

**Anchor inversion, and it is the structural signature of the page.** The more expensive plan is **listed first and badged "Most Popular"**, so **$79.99 reads as the sensible restraint rather than as the expensive option.** Combined with the tier names — "Reach your goals faster" versus "For casual learners" — the page makes the cheap choice available while making it slightly unflattering.

**Refund arithmetic:** none on the page. `[secondary and conflicting]` — 14 days per makeheadway, 30 days per another 2026 aggregator, both website-purchases-only. **Not verified.**

## Social proof, trust and objection handling

**"Become a member of our community of 43 million people"** — a single aggregate number, framed as membership rather than as a count of users. Plus **App Store 4.76 (96k)** and **Google Play 4.5 (73k)** — note the precision (4.76, not 4.8), which reads as a live figure rather than a rounded marketing claim.

**The objection handling is the fine print, and it is the best sentence on the page:** **"By proceeding you accept that $139.99 is processed as an intro offer, then $174.99 will be billed every 12 months automatically. You can cancel anytime in your Settings."** It names the renewal price — the *higher* one — in the same sentence as the discount, and tells the buyer where the cancel button is.

**This is empirically validated.** Growth.Design's Blinkist case study measured a variant **whose only goal was to address "the customer's fear of getting charged after the trial"** at **+23% trial signups, push-notification opt-in from 6% to 74% (a 1,200% increase), and customer complaints down 55%.** Blinkist evidently learned it and shipped it into the billing disclosure. **The push-opt-in figure is the more remarkable one: users who trust you about billing will grant you permissions they otherwise refuse. Trust compounds across unrelated asks.**

## Mechanisms in use

- **Delete the monthly plan.** The strongest possible version of the annual push: you cannot choose what you are not shown.
- **Live countdown timer on a digital good**, with a named coupon (`blink20`) that makes the discount feel earned rather than universal.
- **Strikethrough anchoring on both tiers at once**, so neither reads as full price.

- **Dollar-denominated savings badges** ("Save $35.00") rather than percentages.

## What they do that most pages don't

**Three things.**

**They delete the monthly plan** from the page. An annual discount concedes that monthly is a legitimate choice; removing monthly makes annual simply the price.

**They name the renewal price in the disclosure**, where most companies obscure it behind "then standard rates apply".

**They sell the tier as an identity.** Choosing between "Reach your goals faster" and "For casual learners" is not a feature comparison — it is a question about what kind of person you are.

## What transfers, and what doesn't

**Do not adopt — and this is a firm recommendation, not a stylistic preference:**

**The countdown timer and the "reserved" discount.** They work on impulse purchases of a cheap information product. Applied to a buyer in a heightened emotional state — someone deciding whether to leave a job, end a relationship, move countries — **a ticking clock is coercive, it will be recognised as coercive, and it poisons the trust the purchase depends on.** Blinkist can afford the technique because nobody is emotionally exposed while buying book summaries.

**Adopt with care:** deleting the monthly plan. Blinkist can remove monthly because its buyer is committing to an ongoing practice. **If your buyer may be done in six weeks, a user who resolves their problem and finds themselves locked into eleven more months will feel cheated** — and cheated users in an emotionally sensitive category write the reviews that define you. Offer monthly, price it in the observed 2.2×–2.9× band (stay at the low end), and let annual win on merit.

**Genuinely universal, and the first item is the most valuable in the consumer lane:**

1. **State the renewal price inside the discount disclosure.** Copy the structure directly: *"$X is processed as an intro offer, then $Y will be billed every 12 months automatically. You can cancel anytime in your Settings."* This is the one technique in the lane with a **measured result** — +23% trial starts, complaints −55%, push opt-ins 6% → 74%. Transparency measurably outperformed obscurity.
2. **Let the tier name make a claim about the buyer, not the features.**
3. **Quote whichever of dollars-or-percent is the larger-sounding number.** Blinkist quotes "$35.00" because 20% of $174.99 sounds smaller; Strava quotes "Save 44%" because the percentage beats the ~$50. Compute both, publish the bigger.
4. **If you run an intro price, disclose the step-up.** A 25% year-two increase discovered at the charge is a refund request; disclosed at purchase, it is a term.
