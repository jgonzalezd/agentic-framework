# Layer 07 — Risk reversal

**Layer slug:** `risk-reversal` · **Loaded at:** Phase 6

This layer governs everything that answers *what happens to me if this is a mistake*: the entry mechanic (hard paywall, free trial, freemium, reverse trial, free-tier-forever, guarantee-only), whether the trial takes a card and how long it runs, the guarantee and its exact wording, the refund fence, and the cancellation path. The thing that goes wrong most often is treating these as independent additions — stacking a free tier, a trial, a money-back guarantee and a cancel-anytime line on one page, on the theory that risk reversal is additive. It is not. **The entry mechanic is a single choice that determines the shape of the whole funnel**, and once it is made, most of the remaining devices have no conversion work left to do and are doing signalling work instead. The second failure, specific to AI and metered products, is promising a refund on value the buyer has already consumed: a 30-day money-back guarantee on a product whose output was generated on day one refunds the heavy user who extracted everything and denies the light user who was merely slow to try it.

> ### ⚠️ The mobile-vs-web transfer warning — read before quoting any number in this layer
>
> **Roughly 95% of the trial and paywall benchmarks below are App Store / Google Play in-app-purchase artifacts** (RevenueCat 115k apps / $16B / 2026; Adapty 16k apps / $3B / 2026). They do not describe a Stripe web checkout, and the gap is mechanical, not cosmetic:
>
> - **Trial-to-paid rates of 25–45% assume zero-friction card-on-file.** In IAP, starting a trial is one Face ID tap against a credential Apple already holds. There is **no card-entry step**. On web, a card-required trial adds a full form and a card-free trial is a structurally different product. Every RevenueCat trial figure is implicitly **100% card-on-file**.
> - **Cancellation-timing figures are partly an Apple UX artifact.** "55.4% of 3-day trials cancel on Day 0" (RevenueCat, 115k apps, 2026) is measured in an ecosystem with an OS-level cancel flow the user knows how to reach and Apple-sent reminder emails. Web cancellation is app-specific and higher-friction, so the same number would mean something different.
> - **Involuntary-churn and grace-period figures do not exist on Stripe at all.** The 32.3% Google Play / 15.2% App Store involuntary-churn rates are store billing-rail failure rates, and account-hold/grace-period lifts are Google Play platform features. **Never quote them for a Stripe product.**
> - **Price points ($5.99 weekly / $34.80 yearly medians) are shaped by Apple's price-tier grid and a 30% take rate.** Weekly billing in particular is a mobile-native pattern that is rare and often distrusted on web.
> - **"Revenue per install" has no web analogue.**
>
> **What transfers as direction, never as magnitude:** longer trials convert better than shorter ones; gating beats freemium on revenue per acquired user without a retention penalty; annual retains better than monthly. **What transfers as mechanism:** the retention-parity finding (P-182) and the consumed-value fence (P-196).
>
> Source for the warning itself: L07 lane synthesis of RevenueCat *State of Subscription Apps 2026*, Adapty 2026 and Superwall, plus RevenueCat's own Dipsea IAP-vs-web experiment (US only, ~12,500 users, May 2025, n=1 app).

## How to use this layer

Read **P-181 first and treat it as a gate**: it is the only pattern here that forecloses others, and everything from P-182 to P-189 is downstream of the mechanic it selects. **P-190 → P-195 are the guarantee set** and are largely optional for any product that already has a trial or a free tier — read P-194 before you write a guarantee at all, because for most subscription products the honest answer is that the guarantee is a trust badge, not a conversion device. **P-196 → P-199 are the consumed-value set** and are load-bearing for any product that generates output on demand (AI, credits, metered analysis); skip them entirely for a flat-access product. **P-200 and P-201 are cancellation** and P-201 is a blocking compliance check, not a recommendation.

In a **COLD** regime — a product at ~2,000 monthly visitors — none of this can be tested. Prefer the patterns whose mechanism is a legal or arithmetic fact (P-192, P-196, P-201) over the ones whose mechanism is a conversion claim from someone else's cohort (P-182, P-183, P-185). The single highest-value COLD move in this layer is P-195: read your own refund reasons and write their inverse onto the page.

**P-301 → P-311 were added by the L18b gap-closure lane on 2026-08-12** and they change how three earlier patterns should be read. **P-301** replaces the habit of quoting a published trial-conversion benchmark, because on Stripe a peer-matched percentile is available to any product with five active subscriptions and no public benchmark of the right shape exists at all. **P-302 → P-305 are the trial-length set** and they carry the only randomised evidence in this layer, which points the opposite way from P-185's mobile cross-section; read the Interactions note before choosing between them. **P-306 → P-309 are the compliance set**, and they supply the dated statuses that P-201 exists to demand — P-201 remains the gate, and these are the last reading through it. **P-310 and P-311 are the Stripe platform constraints**, and they are prerequisites rather than options for anything P-196 or P-199 recommend, because a refund policy cannot promise a behaviour the billing system does not perform. None of the eleven has been applied to a real product, so all of them enter `Pricing/Ledger.md` as `our-result: untested` the first time they are cited.

## Patterns

### P-181 — Choose one entry mechanic before you write a word of the page

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The entry mechanic decides how many people enter the funnel, how qualified they are, and whether the paid conversion is a *decision* the buyer must actively make or a *default* that happens if they do nothing. Those three properties determine everything downstream — the copy, the CTA, the guarantee, the cancellation flow — so choosing the mechanic late means rewriting the page. The six live options are: hard paywall, card-required free trial, card-free free trial, freemium, reverse trial, and pay-immediately with a money-back guarantee.
- **works-when**: You can name the growth loop (if any) that free users feed, and you can state your product's honest time-to-first-value in days. Those two answers pick the mechanic almost by themselves.
- **fails-when**: You are choosing by convention or by what a competitor does. Also fails when you try to run two mechanics at once — a free tier *and* a trial of the paid tier — which produces a page where the buyer cannot tell what they are being offered, and which none of the benchmark cohorts below measured.
- **observed-effect**: Per 1,000 website visitors (**ChartMogul / ProductLed / Growth Unhinged, n=200 B2B software products, Jan 2026**): freemium → 90 signups → **5.0 paying customers**; ungated freemium → 70 → **5.6**; card-free trial → 45 → **3.6**; **card-required trial → 35 signups → 10.5 paying customers**. Primary-entry-model adoption in the same sample: free trial **57%**, freemium **26%**, reverse trial **7%**, interactive demo **7%**, paid trial **4%**. Separately, mobile IAP (**RevenueCat, 115k+ apps, $16B, 2026**): D35 download-to-paid **10.7% hard paywall vs 2.1% freemium**.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(two large, independent, non-randomised cross-sections that agree on ordering; neither is an experiment, and neither cohort is a low-priced consumer web product)*
- **sources**: https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/ · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/free-to-paid-conversion-report · https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps
- **how-to**: Answer two questions in writing before choosing. First: *what loop do free users feed?* If the answer is SEO surface, virality, collaboration invites, or marketplace supply, you need a free tier and the choice is freemium or reverse trial. If the answer is "nothing, it just feels friendly", you do not need one. Second: *how many days until a new user experiences the thing they would pay for?* If it is minutes, a hard paywall or pay-immediately is available; if it is a week or more, you need a trial long enough to span it (P-185). Write the chosen mechanic at the top of the page brief and do not add a second one.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's free tier at 5 receipts/month feeds no loop — receipts are private, there is no invite, no public artefact, no SEO surface. Its time-to-value is one receipt, about ninety seconds. Both answers point away from freemium. Applying the n=200 ordering to 2,000 monthly visitors gives roughly **10 new customers/month at freemium rates (5.0/1,000) against ~21 at card-required-trial rates (10.5/1,000)** — but that survey is B2B software at $50–$249 monthly ARPU, so read the *ordering* and discard the magnitudes. The discarding is not a formality: Ledgerly actually acquires about **5 new paying customers a month**, or 2.5 per 1,000 visitors, which sits below the survey's *worst* entry model. Both projections above are therefore ceilings drawn from someone else's cohort, and neither is a forecast. The defensible Ledgerly move is a card-required 30-day trial (P-185) with the free tier cut to a demo of 3 receipts that does not create an account.

### P-182 — Gate the product when the free tier feeds no loop, and read the retention parity as the finding

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A hard paywall forces the purchase decision while intent is at its peak and pre-qualifies everyone who passes it. Freemium defers the decision indefinitely, and most users never revisit it. The standard objection — "you will lose the users who would have converted later" — is the claim the data actually addresses.
- **works-when**: The value proposition is legible *before* use, so a stranger can tell from the page whether it will help them; acquisition is intent-driven (search, referral, a named problem); and no growth loop depends on the free population.
- **fails-when**: Value only becomes apparent through use; you need free users for content, collaboration or supply; or you are quoting the magnitude rather than the direction. **These are mobile IAP figures** — download-to-paid has no web analogue, and the comparison is between app *populations* (hard-paywall apps are in different categories, with different products and teams) rather than a measured effect of switching. Note also the counter-reading: Adapty (16k apps, 2026) finds soft paywalls out-convert hard ones at the paywall-view stage (**4.85% vs 3.34%**, a gap of **+45%**) while carrying a much lower median LTV — **$20.00 against hard's $41.90, so the hard-paywall median is 2.1× the soft one.** This pattern read "21% lower LTV" until 2026-08-13, which is arithmetically impossible against the two published medians and understated the gap by roughly fivefold. Whichever funnel stage you measure decides which model "wins".
- **observed-effect**: **RevenueCat, 115,000+ apps, $16B revenue, 1B+ transactions, 2026, mobile IAP:** D35 download-to-paid **10.7% (hard) vs 2.1% (freemium)** — 5×; D60 revenue per install **$3.09 vs $0.38** — ~8×; and **Year-1 payer retention 27% (hard) vs 28% (freemium)** — statistically indistinguishable. The retention parity is the load-bearing number: the 5× conversion advantage is *not* bought with worse cohort quality. Year-over-year, hard-paywall conversion fell 12.11% → 10.7% while freemium stayed flat (2025 report, 75k apps, vs 2026, 115k apps — different samples).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(very large N and a clean mechanism, but cross-sectional, uncontrolled, mobile-only, and RevenueCat states it sees ~20% of global subscription-app revenue, with the largest apps running their own billing and therefore absent)*
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps · https://saastr.com/the-top-10-learnings-from-revenuecats-state-of-subscription-apps-how-115000-mobile-apps-deliver-16b-in-revenue-whats-working-whats-quietly-killing-growth · https://adapty.io/blog/high-performing-paywall-2026/
- **how-to**: Before switching, confirm in writing that your free tier is not doing structural work. Then gate **new traffic only** and measure to **D60 revenue per acquired visitor**, never to signup — the freemium funnel wins every early metric by construction. Keep the old cohort on the old terms; changing terms under existing free users is a separate, more expensive decision. If you cannot run the comparison at your traffic, prefer the gate for the reason the parity finding gives: the users you "lose" were not converting later anyway.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's 5-receipts-a-month free tier is almost exactly the amount of bookkeeping a very light freelancer does, so it is not a sample — it is the product, for a slice of the market. Replacing it with a 3-receipt no-account demo keeps the "see it work before paying" job and removes the permanent free landing pad. Falsifier: if new-visitor-to-paid over 60 days does not exceed the current rate within two months of the change, the gate is wrong for this product.

### P-183 — Require the card on the trial when the goal is revenue, and read the conversion rate as selection

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The card requirement moves qualification to the front of the funnel. You get far fewer signups, but everyone who starts has already cleared the highest-friction step, and the paid conversion becomes a *default* (do nothing → billed) rather than an active decision. The 30% figure is therefore partly a property of who starts, not a lift you can apply to your current signup base.
- **works-when**: Revenue efficiency matters more than user volume; no growth loop needs free users; and you pair it with unambiguous reversal — a visible trial end date, one-click cancel, and an email before the first charge (P-188).
- **fails-when**: The brand is unknown and the purchase is low-trust — the card requirement then reads as a trap, and the conversion advantage evaporates. Also fails when quoted as a target: only **20% of trial products in the n=200 sample require a card**, so the surveyed 30% describes a self-selected minority of products in a B2B sample whose typical respondent is $1–10M ARR at $50–$249 monthly ARPU. Note also that IAP trial benchmarks are **not** a proxy for a card-required web trial: in IAP the card is already on file, so there is no friction step to select anybody.
- **observed-effect**: **ChartMogul / ProductLed / Growth Unhinged, n=200 B2B software products, Jan 2026** (conversion defined as free signups converting to paying within six months): card-required trials convert at **30%**, ">5× ones that don't"; good band **25–35%**, great **50–60%**; card-free trials good **4–6%**, great **10–15%**. Per 1,000 visitors: **35 signups → 10.5 customers** (card-required) vs **45 → 3.6** (card-free). An earlier, conflicting cut from the same orbit (**n=1,000+, Aug 2023**) put "good" free-trial conversion at 8–12% under a different definition; **do not average the two**.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(large, well-documented survey; not randomised, B2B, and the two vintages of the same study disagree)*
- **sources**: https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/ · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/free-to-paid-conversion-report
- **how-to**: Default to requiring the card unless you can name the loop free users feed. Then spend the friction you just bought: show the exact date of the first charge on the signup screen, send the reminder email two days before it, and make cancelling one click from the account page. Disclose the requirement honestly at the button rather than after the form — Typeform ships "Credit card required" as visible microcopy on its Growth Flow trial, trading click-rate for qualified starts.
- **example-copy**: "$0 payment today" and "Email reminder 2 days before trial ends" — Grammarly, both under the CTA, 2026-08-12. Honest negative disclosure: "Credit card required" — Typeform, 2026-08-12.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly runs a 30-day card-required trial on Solo. The signup screen reads: "$0 today. Your card is charged $9 on 14 September unless you cancel — we'll email you on the 12th." A freelancer who books a reminder is exactly the customer profile; one who balks at the card at $9/mo was unlikely to survive month two.

### P-184 — Use a reverse trial when you need conversion pressure *and* a populated free tier — and do not believe the published lift

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: New users get the top self-serve tier for a fixed window with no card, then **downgrade to free rather than to nothing**. Loss aversion over features they have been using does the conversion work, and the free landing pad keeps the user inside the growth loop instead of ejecting them.
- **works-when**: Users reach real value self-serve inside the window, the paid tier is genuinely differentiated, and you have a loop that needs a populated free tier (which is the only reason to prefer this to P-183).
- **fails-when**: The paid features are not compelling — Airtable's Lauryn Isford: without strong packaging *"it's hard to make double-digit improvements in conversion, even with something as foundational as a free trial experience."* Also fails when time-to-value exceeds the window, and when a large fraction of users never touch a paid feature during it (fix activation first).
- **observed-effect**: **The published figures are in open contradiction and this pattern exists partly to record that.** The n=200 survey (ChartMogul et al., Jan 2026) puts reverse-trial conversion *between* its parents — good **4–6%**, great **8–12%** — which is not obviously better than freemium (good 3–5%), and finds only **7%** of products use it, the least-adopted model in the set. Elena Verna's Amplitude table reports free trials converting at **15% without a card and 70–80% with one**, and claims a **~20% lift** from reverse trials. The same survey measures card-required trials at **30%**. **These cannot both be right.** The L05 lane's recommendation is to prefer the surveyed number, because it has a stated sample, a stated definition and a stated window, while Verna's figures have a single operator behind them and no counterfactual. **Do not average them; report the disagreement.**
- **evidence**: `medium` for the mechanism, `anecdote` for every published lift
- **sources**: https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/your-guide-to-reverse-trials · https://amplitude.com/blog/reverse-trial · https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/
- **how-to**: Grant full paid access at signup for a window sized to your observed time-to-value. Instrument which paid features actually get touched. At expiry, downgrade explicitly and name what was lost, feature by feature, rather than degrading silently. Then measure your own number, because the two published estimates of this mechanism differ by more than 2× and neither cohort is yours.
- **example-copy**: "Your 14 days of Books ended. You're on Free now — that means 5 receipts a month, and CSV export is switched off. Your 340 processed receipts are still here."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly has no growth loop that a free tier feeds, so the reverse trial is the wrong instrument here and P-183 wins. It would become the right instrument the day Ledgerly ships shared client folders, because then the free population is supply for someone else's paid workspace.

### P-185 — Set trial length from your own time-to-value, and know the benchmark is mobile and cross-sectional

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A longer trial gives the product time to deliver a real result before the charge, so more of the cohort converts on evidence rather than on hope. A short trial mostly harvests users who had already decided, and it adds a Day-0 cancellation cliff. The market is drifting the *other* way, toward 3-day trials, because a short window minimises the time in which a user remembers to cancel — which is a passive-churn optimisation that reappears later as refunds and one-cycle churn.
- **works-when**: Time-to-value is measured in days or weeks (habit products, learning, anything on a monthly cycle), and you can afford the delayed revenue recognition.
- **fails-when**: Value is instantaneous and single-use — the user extracts everything and cancels, which is the consumed-value problem (P-196). Also fails when cash flow cannot absorb a 30-day float. **On the benchmark itself:** these are mobile IAP medians from a cross-section, not an experiment; the comparison is confounded by product type, since products that can afford long trials are products with long time-to-value. On web, the direction is credible and **the magnitude is not**.
- **observed-effect**: **RevenueCat, 115k+ apps, 2026, mobile IAP, card-on-file:** trial-to-paid **42.5%** median for 17–32-day trials, **37.4%** for 5–9 days, **25.5%** for ≤4 days — **+66.7% long-vs-short** (42.5 ÷ 25.5 = 1.667; this pattern read "+70%" until 2026-08-13). Meanwhile the share of apps using ≤4-day trials **rose 42.1% → 46.5% YoY**, and 17–32-day trials fell 6.1% → 5.0%: **the market is moving against its own data.** Cancellation timing by length: **55.4%** of 3-day trials cancel on Day 0 (84% cumulative by Day 1), 39.8% of 7-day, 35.7% of 14-day, 31.1% of 30-day. On web, the only figure available is prevalence: **14 days = 62%** of trial products, 7 days and 30 days tied at **14%** (n=200, Jan 2026); **no source publishes a conversion-by-trial-length cut for web.**
- **evidence**: `medium` on the correlation (very large N, published methodology); **`anecdote`-level on causality** — there is no randomisation anywhere in the dataset
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps · https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/ · https://amplitude.com/blog/reverse-trial
- **how-to**: Find the p50 and p75 time-to-first-value among users who *converted*, and set the trial modestly above p75. If a large share of trials expire with no meaningful usage, the problem is onboarding and shortening the trial will make it worse. Treat length as a randomisable variable in your own product rather than adopting anyone's benchmark, and measure realised revenue over a horizon that includes the delayed first charge.
- **example-copy**: "Try it free for 30 days. No charge until 14 September."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's value is not "a receipt got categorised" — it is "the monthly reconciliation took twenty minutes instead of an evening." That moment happens once a month. A 7-day trial cannot reach it and a 14-day trial reaches it only for users who happen to sign up mid-cycle. **30 days is the only length that guarantees the trial spans one full bookkeeping cycle**, and the fact that only 14% of surveyed products use 30 days is irrelevant to a product whose value metric is monthly.

### P-186 — Vary the *risk* across plans, not just the price

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: Almost every competitor offers one trial length and varies only the price between plans. Varying the trial length instead puts the concession on the dimension the hesitant buyer is actually weighing — exposure, not cost. A longer trial on the annual plan reads as generosity while functioning as a habit-formation window twice as long on the plan carrying twelve months of commitment.
- **works-when**: You have both a monthly and an annual (or a cheap and an expensive) plan, and you want the longer-commitment one chosen for reasons other than a bigger discount.
- **fails-when**: The asymmetry is large enough to look like a bribe for lock-in, or the longer trial exceeds your ability to deliver value in it. It also fails to be *readable* if the two plans are not side by side — the whole effect is comparative.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Observed in two independent live products: **Headspace** (2026-08-12) shows **"Annual - 14 days free"** against **"Monthly - 7 days free - $12.99 USD/month"**; **Notion** (2026-08-12) applies the same asymmetry to its refund window — *"We'll be happy to refund you the full amount (not pro-rated) if you downgrade your subscription within three days of signing up for monthly billing, or within 30 days of signing up for annual billing."* Across the seven consumer-subscription pages torn down in L13, Headspace is **1 of 7** using an asymmetric trial. No A/B test of the technique exists in the public record.
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: https://www.headspace.com/subscriptions (observed 2026-08-12) · Notion pricing page (observed 2026-08-12) · L13 consumer-subscription teardowns
- **how-to**: Put the longer trial or the longer refund window on the plan you want chosen, and show the two lengths adjacent so the difference is visible without arithmetic. State both in the same sentence structure. Do not simultaneously deepen the discount on that plan — pick one concession, or you cannot tell later which one moved anything.
- **example-copy**: "Annual — 14 days free" / "Monthly - 7 days free - $12.99 USD/month" — Headspace, 2026-08-12.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's annual Solo is $90/yr against $9/mo. Rather than deepening the annual discount, give annual a **30-day** trial and monthly a **14-day** one, stated side by side. The annual buyer gets a full bookkeeping cycle plus a second look; the monthly buyer gets a half-cycle. The falsifier: if 30-day-annual trials cancel at a higher rate than 14-day-monthly ones, the extra window is showing people the product's limits rather than its value, and that is a product finding, not a pricing one.

### P-187 — Answer the two distinct trial fears separately, in the two lines under the button

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: Trial hesitation is not one fear, it is two: *what leaves my account right now* and *will I be silently charged later*. Most pages answer only the first, with "no credit card required" or "free trial", which leaves the second — the one that actually blocks a card-required trial — unaddressed. Answering them as two separate lines is what makes the reassurance land as specific rather than as boilerplate.
- **works-when**: There is a trial and a future charge. The second line must be a promise you keep automatically, not a policy — the email must actually send.
- **fails-when**: The list grows past three lines and starts reading as protesting too much; or the reassurances restate benefits instead of neutralising objections. Also fails if the reminder is opt-in — a reminder the buyer has to ask for answers nothing.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the pairing specifically. The nearest evidence is directional: GoodUI Pattern **#15 "Bulleted Reassurances"** aggregates **18 tests** (12 measuring sales, 4 revenue, 9 progression) across checkout, product, landing and cart screens with 80%+ documented power — medians are `[paywalled — subscription required]`. GoodUI's own datastory #20 "Six Reassurances For Higher Sales" reports **+17% paid accounts** (Covenanteyes.com; sample, duration and significance all paywalled). **Cohort label: GoodUI's corpus is predominantly e-commerce and general web, and the screens named above are cart and checkout screens, not SaaS pricing pages.** The transfer to a subscription pricing page is this library's, and the mechanism carries better than the magnitudes, which are paywalled in any case. In the 33-page live census (2026-08-12), the microcopy slot under the CTA is the densest risk-reversal position on the page and is used consistently.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(pattern-level aggregation with paywalled medians plus consistent live prevalence; no isolated test of the two-line pairing)*
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/patterns/15/ · 33-page pricing-page census, 2026-08-12 (L17 copy bank)
- **how-to**: Write exactly two lines under the primary CTA. Line one states what leaves the account today, in dollars. Line two states the mechanism that prevents a surprise — a dated reminder, not a reassuring adjective. Then build the reminder before you ship the line. Grammarly's pair is the best in the live census and is directly copyable.
- **example-copy**: "$0 payment today" / "Email reminder 2 days before trial ends" — Grammarly, 2026-08-12. Alternatives from the same census: "No credit card required. Cancel anytime." — Squarespace · "14-day free trial. No card required. Free migrations." — Kit · "There are no contracts, meaning you can cancel anytime (with a complete data export)." — Fathom Analytics.
- **worked-example**: Under Ledgerly's Solo CTA: **"$0 today — first charge 14 September."** / **"We'll email you on the 12th, two days before."** Fathom's data-export clause is the one worth adding below it, because a bookkeeping product holds a year of the buyer's financial records and "can I get my data out" is a live lock-in fear: "Cancel any time, and export every receipt and category as CSV on the way out."

### P-188 — Put the renewal price and date inside the offer sentence, not after it

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The fear a trial or intro offer creates is not about today's price, it is about the charge the buyer cannot see. Naming the post-trial or post-intro price *in the same sentence as the discount* removes the fear at the exact moment it fires, and it converts the disclosure from something that reads as fine print into something that reads as candour. Blinkist's paywall test is the clearest published instance of the mechanism paying for itself.
- **works-when**: There is a step-up — trial to paid, intro rate to standard rate, discounted first year to renewal. The higher number must appear in full, not as "then standard rates apply".
- **fails-when**: The step-up is large enough that stating it plainly kills the sale — in which case the problem is the offer, not the disclosure. Also fails as a substitute for the reminder in P-187: a sentence at purchase and an email before the charge do different jobs at different times.
- **observed-effect**: **Growth.Design's Blinkist case study** (references 2021 research; page carries no explicit date) reports an A/B test in which Variant B's *only* goal was to address "the customer's fear of getting charged after the trial": **+23% trial signups, push-notification opt-in from 6% to 74%, and customer complaints down 55%.** ⚠️ The case study describes the variants but **does not reproduce the paywall copy**, and it is not clearly labelled as a controlled A/B test — L09 classifies the +23% as "a claim" with no sample, duration or significance published. The live operationalisation on Blinkist's own pricing page (2026-08-12) is verbatim: *"By proceeding you accept that $139.99 is processed as an intro offer, then $174.99 will be billed every 12 months automatically. You can cancel anytime in your Settings."* Across the seven consumer pages torn down in L13, **1 of 7** states the renewal price inside the discount disclosure.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` *(one vendor-published case study with no methodology, plus one live instance)*
- **sources**: https://growth.design/case-studies/blinkist-trial-conversion · https://www.blinkist.com/en/pricing (observed 2026-08-12)
- **how-to**: Write one sentence containing: what is charged today, what is charged next, when, and how to stop it. Put it directly above the button, at readable size — the FTC's JustAnswer complaint (P-201) turns on disclosures that were technically present but "difficult to see in comparison with the much larger text on the payment form". If your renewal price is higher than the intro price, say the higher number.
- **example-copy**: "By proceeding you accept that $139.99 is processed as an intro offer, then $174.99 will be billed every 12 months automatically. You can cancel anytime in your Settings." — Blinkist, 2026-08-12.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's annual Solo at $90 with a first-year founder rate of $60: **"You'll be charged $60 today for your first year. On 14 September 2027 it renews at $90 a year, and we'll email you a month before. Cancel from Settings any time."** Note that this sentence forecloses the temptation to quietly renew founders at the founder rate forever or at full price without warning — which is the point.

### P-189 — Turn the trial into dated calendar events the buyer can see, and let them move the reminder

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: "7 days free" is a promise with no shape. A dated timeline — today, day 5, day 7 — converts the trial from something happening *to* the buyer into a schedule the company has committed to. Letting the buyer choose when the reminder fires is the strongest available proof that the trial is not a trap, because a company running a passive-churn play would never hand over that control.
- **works-when**: You control the trial UI (web, or an in-app screen before the store sheet) and can actually send the reminder.
- **fails-when**: On mobile IAP, where the store owns the charge and the reminder; and where the timeline is displayed but the reminder is not implemented, which is worse than not showing it.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Observed in one live product: **Duolingo's in-app trial timeline** renders as "Today / Day 5 / Day 7" with **"Day 5: Get reminded when your trial is about to end"**; a September 2025 sighting reports Duolingo testing a **user-chosen reminder date**. L13 rates this **1 of 7** in its cohort and the best trial UI in the lane. No published effect for either version.
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: L13 consumer-subscription teardowns (Duolingo in-app offers, tasu.ai June 2026; Le Pennec sighting September 2025)
- **how-to**: Render the trial as three dated rows with real dates, not day-numbers. Make the reminder row a commitment ("we'll email you on 12 September"), and if you can, make the date editable. Then instrument the reminder's send rate as a system-health metric — a reminder that silently fails is a dark pattern you did not intend to ship.
- **example-copy**: "Day 5: Get reminded when your trial is about to end" — Duolingo, via L13 teardown.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's trial screen: **"Today — full Solo access. 12 Sept — we email you. 14 Sept — $9 charged unless you've cancelled."** With an edit control on the middle row: "Remind me earlier". A freelancer who moves the reminder to day 20 has just told you their real evaluation window, which is a better trial-length signal than any benchmark in P-185.

### P-190 — Name the guarantee; never call it "satisfaction"

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A generic guarantee is invisible because it is universal — every competitor has one, so it carries no information. A named guarantee is a proprietary asset that can be repeated by a customer to a friend, and the act of naming forces you to specify what is actually being promised.
- **works-when**: The name states the real promise vividly, and the register matches the brand.
- **fails-when**: The name outruns what you will honour, or clashes with the voice. Hormozi's shark-infested-waters formulation is memorable and would be corrosive in a quiet brand — the technique transfers, the register does not.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for naming specifically. The nearest adjacent figure tests *presence*, not naming: a self-reported single-operator test on a **$197 digital product** found a visible 30-day money-back guarantee increased sales **21%** with **12%** requesting refunds (≈6.5% net revenue lift, $19,700 → $20,977/mo) — no sample size, no significance testing, author states results are situation-specific.
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: https://www.quicksprout.com/what-converts-better-free-trial-versus-money-back-guarantee/ · https://www.gregfaxon.com/blog/100m-offers-summary · https://wisewords.blog/book-summaries/100m-offers-book-summary/
- **how-to**: Write the buyer's biggest fear as one sentence. Convert it into a promise. Give the promise a two-to-four-word name a customer could say out loud. Put the name in bold near the CTA and expand it in one sentence beneath. If you cannot name it without embarrassment, the promise is too weak to be worth the pixels.
- **example-copy**: High-energy register (Hormozi): "In 30 days, if you wouldn't jump into shark infested waters to get our product back, we will return every dollar you paid." Calmer client version he cites: "Love it or leave it guarantee." Classic long-form (Abraham lineage): "…I will gladly refund your entire purchase price on the spot, no questions asked and no hard feelings either." Live SaaS instances, 2026-08-12: "100% Money-back Guarantee" — ClickUp · "Risk-free, cancel anytime, no long-term lock-in" — Basecamp.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyer fear is not "it won't work", it is "I'll spend an evening feeding it receipts and it will mis-file half of them and I'll have to redo it." The named guarantee is **"The Evening-Back Guarantee"**: "If your first month's reconciliation isn't faster than doing it by hand, write to me and I'll refund the month. No form."

### P-191 — Pick the guarantee type from the taxonomy, not from the first example you read

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: All guarantees decompose into four types with different economics. **Unconditional** (time-bound, no questions) makes the purchase functionally a paid trial and is strongest where an individual refund is small and adjudication costs more than the refund. **Conditional** (gated on the buyer doing their part) suppresses claims mechanically and is the adversarial instrument — the conditions that protect margin are the same conditions that generate the "they wriggled out of it" complaint. **Anti-guarantee** ("all sales are final" with a credible reason-why) filters buyers and is honest only where value transfers irreversibly at purchase. **Implied/performance** (revenue share, success fee) applies to services with attributable financial outcomes and essentially never to a flat consumer subscription.
- **works-when**: You match the type to the shape of the value transfer and to who controls the outcome. Hormozi's own stated split: broad unconditional for low-ticket B2C, specific conditional for high-ticket B2B.
- **fails-when**: You pick conditional for a self-serve software product, where delivery has near-zero marginal cost, so "we keep working with you free until you get the result" means nothing and degrades into extended access. Also fails when conditions push refund requests into disputes and chargebacks rather than clean transactions — the cost moves, it does not vanish.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for type selection. Hormozi's illustrative model — 130 sales × $1,000 = $130,000 minus 13 refunds = $117,000 net, described as a 23% profit increase over the no-guarantee case — **is a worked illustration in a book, not a measured result.** The only semi-independent operator datapoint is the $197-product test in P-190 (21% more sales, 12% refunds, self-reported, no N).
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: https://www.gregfaxon.com/blog/100m-offers-summary · https://wisewords.blog/book-summaries/100m-offers-book-summary/ · https://theprocesshacker.com/blog/100m-offers-alex-hormozi-summary
- **how-to**: Ask one question: *at the moment of refund, what have I already spent that I cannot recover?* If the answer is "nothing" (software, flat access), use unconditional and keep the sentence short. If it is "my time" (service, coaching), conditional is defensible and the remedy should exceed the price — "we work with you free until you reach the outcome" — because the buyer also spent time. If it is "the deliverable itself, irreversibly", you are in anti-guarantee or consumed-value territory (P-196), not in guarantee territory at all.
- **example-copy**: Conditional template: "If you do not achieve [X result] in [Y timeframe], we will [Z]." Instances: "Show up 3x/week and log meals; if you don't lose 10 lbs, full refund." · "Double your money back if you take all required steps and fail." · "We will pay your hourly wage for the time spent on our call if you find it useless." Anti-guarantee: "If you're the type of customer who needs a guarantee before taking a jump, then you are not the type of person we want to work with." Non-refund reversal, live: "If you're not happy with one of our testers, you'll get a new one at no extra cost." — Userbrain.
- **worked-example**: What Ledgerly cannot recover at the moment of a refund is the cost-to-serve already spent, which at the canonical **$0.0186 a receipt** is about **$1.86** on a fully-consumed Solo month of 100 receipts, and about $6.00 at the $0.060 pre-lever rate. Unconditional still wins on arithmetic alone: the unrecoverable cost is a fraction of the $9 being returned, and adjudicating a condition costs more in email than either figure. The conditional variant — "process at least 50 receipts and if it doesn't save you time, full refund" — would suppress claims and generate exactly one kind of message: an angry one from someone at 47 receipts.

### P-192 — Never port the anti-guarantee to a subscription with a card on file

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: An anti-guarantee assumes you control the refund decision. On a recurring charge against a stored card, you do not: the customer's real refund mechanism is the card issuer. A refused refund becomes a chargeback, which costs a fee, arrives with no negotiation, and accumulates against your processor standing. "All sales are final" on a subscription is therefore not a stronger position than a refund policy — it is the same money leaving, plus a penalty, plus a dispute record.
- **works-when**: Essentially never for consumer subscriptions. The pattern is included because it is the most tempting posture for a founder who resents refunds, and the most expensive to get wrong.
- **fails-when**: Always, on a card-on-file recurring product. The one context where a hard non-refund line survives is category dominance with a free tier doing the risk reversal — Figma publishes *"Payments for Figma subscriptions are non-refundable."* and can, because it is Figma. Framer discloses only the refund it is legally compelled to give: *"If you live in the EU or Turkey, you are legally eligible for a refund if your subscription was purchased within the last 14 days."* Neither is a model for an unknown product.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Prevalence from the 10-page prosumer-SaaS census (2026-08-12): **nobody offers a meaningful money-back guarantee**; Figma is explicitly non-refundable, Framer is jurisdiction-scoped, Linear/Raycast/Vercel are silent, and only **Notion** publishes a discretionary window. In consumer subscription (L13, 7 pages, 2026-08-12), **6 of 7 carry no money-back language at all** and the free trial has entirely displaced the guarantee.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the chargeback mechanism is a fact about card networks; the prevalence is a census)*
- **sources**: L11 prosumer-SaaS teardowns (observed 2026-08-12) · L13 consumer-subscription teardowns (observed 2026-08-12)
- **how-to**: Write a refund policy you will actually honour, and honour it on the first email rather than the third. If you are tempted by "no refunds", price the alternative: one chargeback costs the transaction plus the fee plus the dispute, and enough of them cost you the processor. The only defensible version of "final" for a software product is a **consumption fence** (P-196), which refuses the refund on a fact rather than on a policy.
- **example-copy**: n/a — this pattern is about removal.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly at $9/mo cannot afford a chargeback: a single disputed $9 charge plus a typical dispute fee exceeds two months of that customer's revenue. The policy that costs least is the one that refunds on request, immediately, and asks the reason afterwards (P-195).

### P-193 — Place the guarantee where the price is, at the price's visual weight

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A money-back guarantee is read as a *quality signal* — information about the seller's confidence — and is consumed by buyers who will never claim it. That signalling only happens if it is visible at the decision point. Buried in Terms, hedged with conditions, or placed after the CTA, it is doing nothing, because the decision it was meant to influence has already been made.
- **works-when**: The guarantee is stated in one sentence with no conditions *in that sentence*, adjacent to the price, at comparable visual weight.
- **fails-when**: The sentence carries its own conditions, which converts a signal into a contract the buyer now has to evaluate. Also note the ordering constraint from Layer 09 (P-222): if the guarantee block makes the CTA no longer the loudest element, it is costing you.
- **observed-effect**: Academic framing (**Akcay, Melbourne Business School, and Boyaci, ESMT Berlin, 2017-07-23**): money-back guarantees enhance brand value, increase satisfaction and encourage repeat purchase, and can be profitable even against high return costs — US return costs exceed **$260B annually (~8% of retail sales)**. **Cohort label: that is physical retail, where a return costs shipping, restocking and often the unit itself.** Software has none of those costs, so the figure establishes that guarantees can pay against *expensive* returns and says nothing about the size of the effect where returns are nearly free. **No credible controlled test of guarantee *placement* was located** in any lane. The widely-circulated "money-back guarantee increases sales 21% and overall revenue by 6.4%", attributed to Neil Patel, has no reachable study — see `## Folklore`. Live placements observed 2026-08-12: page-level band (ClickUp), under the tier cards (Basecamp), adjacent to the trial CTA (Semrush, Fathom).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the signalling mechanism has published academic support; the placement claim is prevalence only)*
- **sources**: https://theconversation.com/the-economics-of-the-money-back-guarantee-80876 · https://esmt.berlin/knowledge/research-insights/economics-money-back-guarantee · 33-page pricing-page census, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: One sentence, immediately beneath the price, same visual weight as the price, no conditions inside it. Conditions, if any, one click away. Read the Accenture composition in P-195 before you decide how generous to be: the overwhelming majority of returns are not defects, which means a guarantee mostly buys the *appearance* of low risk rather than covering real product failure — and that is precisely why placement, not wording, is where its value is realised.
- **example-copy**: "100% Money-back Guarantee" — ClickUp, 2026-08-12 · "Risk-free, cancel anytime, no long-term lock-in" — Basecamp, 2026-08-12 · "Try Semrush free for seven days. Cancel anytime." — Semrush, 2026-08-12.
- **worked-example**: On Ledgerly's Solo card, directly under "$9 /month": **"The Evening-Back Guarantee — if your first month isn't faster than doing it by hand, I'll refund it."** The conditions page it links to says exactly one more thing: how to ask.

### P-194 — Choose a trial or a guarantee, and know which job the loser is still doing

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A trial and a guarantee both reverse risk, at different points: the trial removes it *before* payment, the guarantee *after*. Once a trial exists, the guarantee has little conversion work left, and its remaining job is signalling (P-193). Stacking them and expecting additive lift is the most common error in this layer.
- **works-when**: You pick based on where drop-off actually is. Pre-payment hesitation → trial. Post-payment remorse and refund volume → expectation-setting language (P-195), not a longer guarantee.
- **fails-when**: You add a guarantee to fix what is actually an activation problem — users who never reached value will not be rescued by a promise about money.
- **observed-effect**: **Single-operator test, $197 digital product, self-reported, no sample size or significance:** guarantee alone → sales +21%, 12% requesting refunds, ≈$20,977/mo (from $19,700); free trial alone → roughly **2× the signups**, ~33% cancelling during the 7-day trial, only **~78% of remaining accounts billing successfully**, ≈$24,822/mo; **trial + guarantee → the same revenue as trial alone (≈$24,822/mo)** with virtually no post-billing refund requests. Corroborating prevalence: in consumer subscription the trial has displaced the guarantee entirely (**6 of 7** pages carry no money-back language, L13, 2026-08-12), and in the 33-page SaaS census only ClickUp offers an explicit money-back guarantee — the dominant grammar is "no card + cancel anytime".
- **evidence**: `anecdote` *(one operator, one price point, no counterfactual; the prevalence data is a census, not a test)*
- **sources**: https://www.quicksprout.com/what-converts-better-free-trial-versus-money-back-guarantee/ · https://www.getmonetizely.com/faqs/is-offering-a-money-back-guarantee-say-30-day-refund-on-paid-plans-a-good-idea-to-reduce-perceived-risk-and-how-does-that-compare-to-or-complement-having-a-free-trial-period · L13/L17 censuses, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Instrument the funnel before choosing. If you already have a trial or a free tier, keep the guarantee copy to one short sentence and treat it as a trust badge rather than expecting lift from it. And note the least-discussed number in that operator test: **~78% billing success on trial conversion**. Payment failure at the trial-to-paid boundary is a larger and cheaper leak than most copy changes, and it is invisible unless you look for it.
- **example-copy**: "Try it free for 14 days. No card until you decide."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly runs a 30-day card-required trial (P-183/P-185). The Evening-Back Guarantee therefore earns its place as a *signal*, not as a second reversal — one sentence under the price, and no expectation that it moves conversion. The measurable thing to fix instead is the first charge. Ledgerly acquires roughly **5 new paying customers a month** — the 40 is a stock accumulated since launch, not a monthly rate — so a 22% billing-failure rate costs about **one customer a month**. This passage claimed 5 until 2026-08-13, which silently equated the leak with Ledgerly's entire monthly acquisition. One customer a month is still the right thing to fix first, not because it is large but because it is the only quantity in this layer that Ledgerly can observe exactly, whereas no copy test at 2,000 monthly visitors can resolve an effect of any size at all.

### P-195 — Lower refunds with pre-purchase clarity, not with tighter conditions

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The composition of returns says most refunds are not product failures. If the dominant causes are "nothing was wrong with it" and buyer's remorse rather than defect, then the lever that reduces refunds is clearer expectation-setting *before* purchase. Conditions suppress **claims**; clarity suppresses **regret**. The two look identical on a refund-rate dashboard and are opposite in every other respect.
- **works-when**: You know your actual top refund reason and can state its inverse plainly on the page — which is the same move as the damaging admission: name the limitation that generates your most common refund, above the price, then say who the product *is* for.
- **fails-when**: You use conditions as the primary tool. Recorded refunds fall while disputes, chargebacks and public complaints rise — the cost moves rather than disappearing. Also fails when the "clarity" is a hedge that quietly narrows the promise; buyers read that as the guarantee being withdrawn.
- **observed-effect**: **Accenture survey of electronics returns, reported via Akcay and Boyaci, 2017-07-23: 68% "no trouble found", 27% buyer's remorse, 5% genuinely defective.** Same source: US return costs exceed **$260B annually, ~8% of retail sales**. ⚠️ The study is about retail returns economics, not about copy — **the inference from that composition to guarantee wording is the L04 lane's, not the authors'**, and it has not been tested on software. The corroborating direction is Blinkist's honesty paywall (P-188): **complaints −55%** when the paywall's only job became confronting the charge fear.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(strong data on the composition; the copywriting inference is unmeasured)*
- **sources**: https://theconversation.com/the-economics-of-the-money-back-guarantee-80876 · https://esmt.berlin/knowledge/research-insights/economics-money-back-guarantee · https://payproglobal.com/how-to/set-up-saas-refund-policy/
- **how-to**: Read your last 30 refund requests and cluster the reasons. For each cluster, write the one sentence that would have prevented that purchase, and put it on the page above the price. Keep the guarantee itself unconditional and short. Then measure refunds **by cohort** after the change, not in aggregate — an aggregate rate mixes pre- and post-change buyers and will hide the effect for months.
- **example-copy**: "If it isn't for you, write to me within 30 days and I'll refund it. No form, no questions." — paired above with: "This won't help if what you need is [X]. It's built for people who [Y]."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's top refund reason is handwritten receipts, where OCR accuracy collapses. The clarity move is a line above the pricing table: **"Ledgerly reads printed receipts. If most of yours are handwritten, it will frustrate you — the accuracy isn't there yet."** That sentence loses some sales and refunds fewer of them, and every buyer who proceeds has already accepted the limitation. The conditions alternative — "refunds only where OCR accuracy fell below 90%" — refunds nobody and generates a support thread per claim.

### P-196 — Fence the refund on consumption, not on the calendar

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A calendar window ("30 days") is a proxy for "hasn't extracted the value yet", and it is a bad one in both directions: it refunds the heavy user who generated everything on day one, and denies the light user who was merely slow to start. A consumption fence refuses the refund on the basis of the thing you actually cannot recover — spent compute, generated output, delivered units — which is auditable, explicable in one sentence, and lets you offer a genuinely generous-sounding guarantee with bounded exposure. **This is the answer to the consumed-value problem for any product whose output is produced on demand.**
- **works-when**: The product has a countable unit the buyer already understands (images, minutes, analyses, receipts), and your meter is trustworthy enough to quote in a refund policy.
- **fails-when**: The unit is not legible to the buyer, so the fence reads as a technicality invented to deny refunds. Also fails where consumer-protection law imposes an unconditional cooling-off period regardless of consumption — an EU/UK 14-day right is not something your policy can fence away (see Framer's jurisdiction-scoped disclosure), and P-201's compliance check applies here too.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. One live instance, second-hand: **Midjourney**, per eesel (published 2026-06-05), refunds only where consumption is *"fewer than 20 GPU minutes total — roughly 15-20 basic image generations"*. Calibration from the same source: "A single still image uses roughly one GPU minute, while an HD video batch uses about 26." L12's census of AI-tool pricing pages found **Midjourney is the only product of the cohort denominating its refund in consumed units**; most say nothing, and ElevenLabs and Gamma offer access-through-period-end, which is standard rather than generous. **All Midjourney strings are quoted by third parties, not fetched from the primary page.**
- **evidence**: `anecdote` *(one product, second-hand sourcing, no measured effect)*
- **sources**: L12 AI-tool teardowns, 2026-08-12, citing eesel (2026-06-05) and techjacksolutions
- **how-to**: Pick the unit the buyer counts. Set the fence at the smallest quantity that could plausibly answer "does this work for me" — for Midjourney that is 15–20 images. State it as a positive: "full refund if you've used fewer than N." Show the consumed count in the account UI so the buyer can check their own eligibility without asking, which is what stops the fence reading as a trick. Keep a calendar backstop only where the law requires one, and say which jurisdiction it applies to.
- **example-copy**: "Full refund if you've processed fewer than 10 receipts — no time limit, no form. Your count is on your account page."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's unit is a processed receipt. A 30-day window refunds the freelancer who ran a year of backlog through it in one evening (400 receipts, all the value extracted) and denies the one who signed up in March and opened it in May. **Fewer than 10 receipts** is the right fence: it is more than enough to evaluate accuracy, it costs Ledgerly about **19 cents** of unrecoverable cost-to-serve at the canonical post-lever rate of $0.0186 a receipt (about 60 cents at the $0.060 pre-lever rate), and it lets the page say "no time limit" — which is a stronger-sounding promise than "30 days" at strictly lower exposure.

### P-197 — Auto-refund the failed unit without being asked, and show the refund in the meter

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The dominant objection to any metered or generative product is "I paid for garbage." A published no-charge-on-failure rule removes that objection at a cost you control, and — because the refund is visible in the usage meter — it converts an abstract policy into something the buyer watches working. It is the cheapest credible quality signal a generative product can make, and the flag stream it produces is the best evaluation dataset you will get for free.
- **works-when**: Failures are detectable — errors, timeouts, guardrail refusals, or an explicit "this didn't work" from the user.
- **fails-when**: Quality is subjective and the rule becomes an unbounded refund lever. Bound it: auto-refund hard failures without limit, and cap discretionary "this wasn't useful" refunds per cycle. Also fails if the meter is not visible — Cursor removed its request counter from the UI in June 2025 while redefining limits, and the backlash forced a public apology on **2025-07-04** plus refunds of unexpected charges for the window "between June 16 and July 4".
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the refund rule itself. The principle is core to outcome pricing, where **Intercom's Fin charges $0.99 per resolution** on the stated basis that "you pay only when Fin successfully resolves a conversation end-to-end", and **Zendesk narrowed billing in May 2026 to *Verified* Resolutions** confirmed by LLM evaluation after billing more loosely. Credit models are criticised for the inverse — charging for activity rather than results, which "creates anxiety instead of value". Adoption of outcome pricing was **5% in mid-2025, expected 25% by 2028**.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` *(well-established in outcome pricing; rare inside credit systems, which is the gap)*
- **sources**: https://fin.ai/learn/ai-customer-service-agent-pricing-comparison · https://www.productgrowth.blog/p/ai-pricing-credits-vs-seats-vs-outcomes · https://cursor.com/blog/june-2025-pricing
- **how-to**: Auto-refund the unit on any hard failure, with no request required, and surface it in the meter as a line item. Add a one-click "this wasn't useful" that refunds up to a bounded number of units per cycle. Never remove the meter — Cursor is the cautionary case and Claude's `/usage` is the positive one. Log every discretionary refund flag with the input that produced it; that stream is your eval set.
- **example-copy**: "1 receipt refunded — we couldn't read this image."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's Solo plan includes 100 receipts. A blurry photo that returns no line items is refunded automatically and shows in the meter as "63 of 100 used · 2 refunded". A "wrong category" button refunds up to 5 receipts per cycle. At Ledgerly's canonical post-lever cost-to-serve of **$0.0186 a receipt**, the bounded discretionary exposure is about **nine cents** a cycle against a $9 plan, or about thirty cents at the $0.060 pre-lever rate — and every refunded receipt arrives labelled as a training example.

### P-198 — Where the meter is the fear, ship a spend cap instead of a refund

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: For usage-based pricing, the buyer's fear is not "this might not work", it is "I might wake up to a bill I did not authorise." A refund promise does not answer that fear, because the buyer would still have to notice, ask, and argue. A hard spend ceiling answers it structurally: the bad outcome cannot happen. **For a metered product, the cap *is* the risk reversal.**
- **works-when**: Pricing is usage-based or has overage, and you can enforce the ceiling technically rather than by policy.
- **fails-when**: The cap silently degrades the product mid-task — a ceiling that stops work without warning trades a billing complaint for a reliability complaint. It also fails as marketing if it is not stated on the pricing page: a cap nobody knows about reassures nobody.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Live instance: **Vercel** publicises a default **$200 on-demand spend budget on all new teams** on its pricing page (observed 2026-08-12) — L11 reads this as the page's real guarantee: not a refund, but a cap. Demand-side evidence: **78% of IT leaders reported surprise AI-related charges in the past year** (Zylo 2026 SaaS Management Index, n=218), and HubSpot's buyer guide recommends buyers set credit limits and pause features — controls vendors can offer proactively instead.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the fear is measured with a stated sample; the cap-as-reversal claim is one live instance plus a mechanism)*
- **sources**: L11 prosumer-SaaS teardowns (Vercel, observed 2026-08-12) · https://blog.hubspot.com/website/ai-credits-buyers-guide
- **how-to**: Ship a default cap on every new account, set it visibly, let the buyer raise it, and warn at 80% with the exact number and date. Put the default on the pricing page in one sentence. When the cap is hit, stop *new* work and finish work in flight — and say which of the two you do, because a buyer who has been burned will ask.
- **example-copy**: "Every account starts with a $20 monthly cap on top-ups. You can raise it, and we'll email you at 80%. You will never get a bill you didn't set."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's top-up pack ($5 for 50 receipts, the corrected price) creates exactly this exposure once auto-top-up exists. The cap goes on the pricing page next to the pack: "Top-ups are capped at $20/month by default." Without it, the first customer who scripts a bulk upload discovers the cap by receiving the bill, and that is the story that gets posted.

### P-199 — Decide the proportional-refund rule in advance, and publish which way it rounds

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: When a buyer cancels mid-term on a prepaid plan, there are two defensible refund rules — refund the unelapsed *time*, or refund the unconsumed *units* — and they produce different numbers. Whichever you have not specified, the buyer will assume the larger one. Publishing the rule before it is invoked converts a negotiation into an arithmetic answer, which is the difference between a refund and a dispute.
- **works-when**: You sell prepaid annual or bundled units, and you can compute both figures from data you already hold.
- **fails-when**: There is no meaningful consumption metric, in which case pro-rata time is the only honest rule. It also fails when the rule is used to manufacture a non-refund: "no pro-rating" as a blanket policy is defensible only when paired with a short unconditional window, which is exactly Notion's construction.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The two live rules observed: **Notion** — *"We'll be happy to refund you the full amount (not pro-rated) if you downgrade your subscription within three days of signing up for monthly billing, or within 30 days of signing up for annual billing"* (2026-08-12), a full-refund-inside-window / nothing-outside-it rule; **Midjourney** — a consumption fence with no time component (P-196). No source in any lane publishes refund-rate or dispute-rate data for either construction.
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: Notion pricing page (observed 2026-08-12) · L12 AI-tool teardowns, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Write the rule as a sentence with a worked number in it, and put it in the billing FAQ before the first annual customer cancels. State which of the two bases you use and why in plain terms ("we refund the months you haven't used" or "we refund the receipts you haven't processed"). If the two bases can produce materially different answers for the same customer, expect to be asked for the larger one — so either pick the more generous basis deliberately or explain the choice in the same sentence.
- **example-copy**: "Cancelling an annual plan? We refund the whole months you haven't started — not the receipts you haven't used. If you've had it four months, you get eight months back."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly Books annual is **$190/yr**, including **4,800 receipts** (400/month). A customer cancels after **4 months** having processed **900 receipts**. *Time basis:* 8 unelapsed months × $15.83 = **$126.67**. *Unit basis:* 900/4,800 = 18.75% consumed, so 81.25% × $190 = **$154.38**. The two differ by $27.71 on a $190 plan — about 15% — and the customer will ask for the second. Ledgerly publishes the time basis, because the unit basis rewards the customer who prepaid a year and used it in four months, which is the same asymmetry P-196 exists to prevent.

### P-200 — Sell the cancellation path on the pricing page

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: For a recurring charge, the buyer's live fear is not the price — it is the difficulty of stopping it. Stating the exit plainly, at the point of decision, removes the fear at its source and costs nothing but the honesty of actually building the path. This is why "no card + cancel anytime" has displaced the money-back guarantee as the dominant risk-reversal grammar in subscription software: it answers the fear people actually have.
- **works-when**: Cancellation genuinely is one click on your own site, and what happens to the buyer's data on cancellation is stated. For products holding valuable user data, the data sentence matters as much as the billing one — lock-in anxiety is a separate fear from money anxiety.
- **fails-when**: The claim is not true. "Cancel anytime" beside a flow that requires an email to support, a phone call, or three confirmation screens is the exact claim the FTC's ROSCA counts address (P-201). Also fails on mobile IAP, where cancellation is store-mediated and you cannot honestly promise a one-click path you do not own.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the copy itself. Prevalence, 33-page census 2026-08-12: cancel-anytime language appears in the CTA microcopy slot on most consumer-facing pages, and L17's read is that in subscription SaaS the guarantee has been **replaced** by "free trial + cancel anytime". The strongest observed variant adds data portability: Fathom Analytics — *"There are no contracts, meaning you can cancel anytime (with a complete data export)."*
- **evidence**: `medium` *(consistent prevalence across a 33-page census plus a clear mechanism; no controlled test)*
- **sources**: 33-page pricing-page census, 2026-08-12 (L17 copy bank) · L11 prosumer-SaaS teardowns
- **how-to**: Put the cancellation sentence in the CTA microcopy, not in the FAQ. Name the number of clicks and where the control lives. Add what happens to the data. Then walk the flow yourself, from the pricing page to a confirmed cancellation, and count the screens — if it is more than two, the page is currently lying. Layer 11 owns what the save flow may *offer*; this layer owns the promise the page makes about reaching it.
- **example-copy**: "Cancel anytime from Settings — two clicks, no email to anyone. Your receipts and categories export as CSV on the way out." Live instances: "No credit card required. Cancel anytime." — Squarespace · "Risk-free, cancel anytime, no long-term lock-in" — Basecamp · "All pricing is in USD and renews automatically unless cancelled." — Fathom.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's page carries the cancellation sentence directly beneath the Solo CTA, and the FAQ's first question is "How do I cancel?" rather than "How is pricing calculated?" — because for a $9/mo product bought by someone who already resents subscriptions, reversibility is the blocking objection and pricing mechanics are not.

### P-201 — Verify the current click-to-cancel regulatory status before any cancellation-flow recommendation ships

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: Negative-option and cancellation rules are an active, moving enforcement area, and stale guidance here is worse than none — it produces confident advice about obligations that may have changed. This pattern is therefore a **blocking check**, not a recommendation: no deliverable touching cancellation, auto-renewal disclosure or save flows ships without a same-week verification of the current rule's status.
- **works-when**: Always, before any cancellation-flow work. The check is cheap: read the current FTC rule status and the applicable state auto-renewal statutes on the day you write the recommendation.
- **fails-when**: You substitute this library's date for a current one. **This layer does not state a 2026 status for the FTC click-to-cancel / negative-option rule, and no pattern here should be read as asserting one.** The research lane that would have verified it did not complete.
- **observed-effect**: What *is* documented is that the enforcement environment is live. **FTC v. JustAnswer LLC and CEO Andrew Kurtzig, filed 2026-01-13 in the Northern District of California, two ROSCA counts**: failure to "clearly and conspicuously disclose the subscription fee" and failure to "obtain express informed consent." The complaint quotes the defendant's own on-screen copy verbatim — the chatbot's *"Here's a secure form to join JustAnswer for $5 (fully-refundable)"* against a payment-form disclosure reading *"By clicking 'Confirm now' I agree to the Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, to be charged the one-time join fee, and a $39 monthly membership fee today and each month until I cancel"*, with the complaint noting the material terms were *"still difficult to see in comparison with the much larger text on the payment form"* and the checkbox involved the *"omission of any language regarding the price consumers would pay for the subscription"*. Advertised entry "as little as $1 or $5"; actual recurring charge **"$28–$125" per month by category**. The FTC alleges this affected "hundreds of thousands of consumers between January 2022 and the present."
- **evidence**: `medium` for the enforcement fact (a sworn, dated filing, reached through named secondary carriers because ftc.gov returned 403 to automated fetch); **`no verified status`** for the rule itself
- **sources**: FTC complaint filed 2026-01-13, N.D. Cal., via `ppc.land`, `regulatoryoversight.com`, `allaboutadvertisinglaw.com` (all fetched 2026-08-12) · primary `justanswer.com` and `ftc.gov` both returned HTTP 403
- **how-to**: Before shipping cancellation guidance: (1) check the current status of the FTC negative-option / click-to-cancel rule *on the day of writing*; (2) check the applicable state auto-renewal statutes for the customer's jurisdiction, which impose their own disclosure and cancellation-parity requirements independently of the federal rule; (3) check EU/UK cooling-off obligations if you sell there. Then, regardless of what the check returns, build to the stricter standard the JustAnswer complaint implies: the recurring price and cadence disclosed at the same visual weight as the rest of the payment form, express affirmative consent captured separately, and a cancellation path no harder than the signup path. That standard is defensible under any version of the rule and is the one your customers already expect.
- **example-copy**: n/a — this is a process gate. The negative example is quoted above, verbatim, from the complaint.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's checkout discloses "$9 a month, charged on the 14th, until you cancel" in the same type size as the card fields, with a separate unticked consent checkbox naming the price. That construction costs nothing at 40 customers and would cost a great deal to retrofit at 4,000 — and it is the single item in this layer with legal, rather than commercial, consequences for getting it wrong.

### P-301 — Read your own Stripe peer benchmark instead of hunting for a published trial-conversion rate

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: Every published trial-conversion figure describes a cohort that is not yours, and the mismatch that matters most is price, because a consumer product at under $30 a month and a B2B tool in the $50–$249 ARPU band that produces most of this layer's figures select entirely different buyers at the card step. Stripe already computes a trial-conversion percentile against a peer group matched on revenue per user and industry, and it shows it inside the merchant's own dashboard. That comparator is strictly better than any public number, and the reason no public number exists in this shape is that the billing providers who could publish it keep it private.
- **works-when**: You bill through Stripe, you have at least five active subscriptions, and you have had at least one paid subscription in the past year. Those are the only two access conditions Stripe states.
- **fails-when**: You have fewer than five active subscriptions, in which case no comparator exists and you should not substitute a published one. It also fails when the in-dashboard figure is compared against an annual industry report, because Stripe's definition is a trailing-30-day ratio and the reports are not. Note further that a percentile against peers tells you where you sit, not what to change, so it is a diagnostic rather than a lever.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. What is documented is the mechanism's availability. From `docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/analytics/benchmarking` (accessed **2026-08-12**), access requires "Have at least five active subscriptions" and "Have had at least one paid subscription within the past year"; the peer group is built by "a k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) algorithm" over "Publicly available information about your business", "Merchant category code (MCC)", "An AI-generated industry we've identified for your business", "Annual recurring revenue (ARR)" and "Average revenue per user (ARPU)"; and the comparison shows "the top, median, and bottom trends from a group of **at least 100 similar businesses**, and show your position relative to peers by percentile" over the past 12 months. **Trial conversion rate is explicitly one of the benchmarked metrics.** Stripe's own definition, verbatim from `docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/analytics` (accessed 2026-08-12): "Trial conversion rate is the number of subscriptions that converted from a trial to a paid plan in the last 30 days, divided by the number of trials that ended in the last 30 days. […] might exceed 100% if some subscriptions convert to a paid plan after the end of their trial period."
- **evidence**: `medium` *(platform documentation read at primary source; the tool's existence and its access conditions are facts, and no effect on anything has been measured)*
- **sources**: https://docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/analytics/benchmarking (accessed 2026-08-12) · https://docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/analytics (accessed 2026-08-12) · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Open the Billing analytics benchmarking view before you go looking for an industry figure, and record your trial-conversion percentile with the date you read it. Write Stripe's definition next to the number so that nobody later diffs it against an annual report. Re-read it quarterly rather than continuously, because the trailing-30-day window makes it noisy at small volume. If you are below the fifth active subscription and therefore locked out, say so in the deliverable rather than importing a benchmark to fill the hole.
- **example-copy**: n/a (diagnostic)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly has about 40 paying customers on Stripe, so the benchmarking view is unlocked and its peer group is matched on an ARPU near $9 rather than on the $50–$249 band that produces every figure in P-181 and P-183. The correct move is to read the percentile, note the reading date, and stop quoting the n=200 survey as though it described Ledgerly's market.

### P-302 — Prefer a short trial, because the only randomised test of trial length says shorter wins

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A longer trial does not give a wavering buyer more chance to be convinced, it gives them more chance to stop using the product before the decision arrives. The randomised evidence supports a consumer-learning account rather than a cannibalisation account: users learn what the product is worth early, and additional trial days past that point add inactivity rather than information. This pattern exists in direct tension with P-185, and both are kept because they rest on different kinds of evidence.
- **works-when**: Time-to-first-value is short enough that a seven-day window spans it, and the product is used more than once inside that window. The experiment's own segment finding is that beginners respond better to short trials, so a product acquiring mostly first-time users of its category is the strongest case.
- **fails-when**: Value is cyclical rather than continuous, so that the moment worth paying for occurs on a monthly rhythm the trial cannot reach — that is the situation P-185's worked example describes, and it is where the longer trial remains correct. It also fails as a magnitude: the underlying data was collected between **2015-12-01 and 2016-01-06** at a single large SaaS company, so the direction is robust and the effect sizes are indicative. Finally, note that the same experiment found 14-day and 30-day trials statistically indistinguishable, so "shorten the trial" means going to roughly a week, not trimming 30 days to 14.
- **observed-effect**: **Yoganarasimhan, Barzegary & Pani (2023), "Design and Evaluation of Optimal Free Trials", *Management Science* 69(6); published online 2022-08-10, issue June 2023; DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2022.4507; verified 2026-08-12.** Randomised field experiment at a large SaaS company, **337,724 users** randomised across 7-day, 14-day and 30-day conditions. Against the 30-day baseline, the 7-day trial produced **+5.59% subscriptions, +6.4% retention at two years, and +7.91% revenue**; the 14-day trial was **not statistically different from 30-day**. The authors state in the abstract that "on average, shorter trial lengths (surprisingly) maximize customer acquisition, retention, and profitability", and report that they "rule out the demand cannibalization theory, find support for the consumer learning hypothesis".
- **evidence**: `medium` *(a genuine randomisation at very large N, published in a peer-reviewed journal, and the only such test anywhere in this library's corpus; downgraded from higher because it is one company, one product category, and data now a decade old)*
- **sources**: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4507 (verified 2026-08-12) · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Set the default trial at roughly a week unless you can name the reason your value moment cannot occur inside one. Do not treat a move from 30 days to 14 as a shortening in the sense this experiment supports, because 14 and 30 performed the same. If you already run a long trial, change it as a randomised split rather than a flip, and measure to a revenue endpoint at a horizon long enough to include the delayed first charge. Where a long trial is genuinely required by a monthly value cycle, keep it and take P-304 seriously instead.
- **example-copy**: "Try it free for 7 days. No charge until 20 August."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's value moment is the monthly reconciliation, which is why P-185 argues for 30 days. This pattern does not overturn that, because Ledgerly is the cyclical case the fails-when names. It does overturn the intermediate compromise: a 14-day Ledgerly trial buys nothing a 30-day trial does not, on the only randomised evidence available, while reaching the value moment for a smaller share of signups. The choice is therefore 7 days or 30 days, and for Ledgerly it is 30.

### P-303 — Do not build personalised trial lengths

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: Personalising trial length looks like an obvious application of a propensity model, and the same experiment that established the short-trial result also tested it. A uniform short trial captured almost the entire benefit that the best personalised policy achieved, and most of the model families tried performed worse than the uniform rule. The lesson generalises: where a lever has only three settings and one of them is broadly better, segmentation adds engineering cost and model risk in exchange for a fraction of a point.
- **works-when**: Essentially never at the scale this library addresses. The pattern is recorded because personalised trial length is a recurring engineering proposal and the counter-evidence is unusually clean.
- **fails-when**: The comparison is against a badly chosen uniform default rather than against the best uniform default. If you are currently on 30 days, the gain you would attribute to personalisation is mostly the gain from moving to a short uniform trial, and you should take that first and measure what is left.
- **observed-effect**: **Yoganarasimhan et al. (2023), n=337,724, randomised, data 2015-12-01 to 2016-01-06:** a lasso-based personalised policy produced **+6.8%** against the uniform 30-day baseline, against **+5.59%** for a uniform 7-day trial — a difference of roughly one point. The authors further report that "personalized policies based on other methods (e.g., causal forests, random forests) perform worse than a simple uniform policy that assigns a short trial length to all users." The one segment result they do report is directional rather than actionable: "skilled and experienced users are more likely to benefit from longer trials, whereas beginners are more responsive to shorter trials."
- **evidence**: `medium` *(same randomised experiment as P-302, same cohort limits; the policy comparison is a within-experiment result rather than a separate test)*
- **sources**: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4507 (verified 2026-08-12) · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Ship one trial length. If someone proposes personalising it, ask what the uniform default currently is, and if it is 14 or 30 days, run the uniform short trial first and re-open the question only if the residual is worth the model. The one variation this library does endorse is the plan-level asymmetry in P-186, which is a two-value design decision visible to the buyer, not a per-user prediction.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly could plausibly give bookkeepers a short trial and first-time self-filers a long one, and the segment direction from the experiment would even support it. At 2,000 monthly visitors there is no way to fit or validate that model, and the published headroom over a well-chosen uniform default is about one percentage point. Ledgerly ships one length.

### P-304 — Instrument the end of the trial for silence, and treat silence as the churn signal

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The conversion decision is made against the buyer's most recent experience of the product, not against the whole trial. A trial that ends in a stretch of inactivity therefore converts against a memory that has gone cold, which is why long trials underperform without the product being any worse. The actionable consequence is that dead air in the final third of the trial is a measurable, addressable leading indicator, and it is available to any product regardless of trial length.
- **works-when**: You can observe usage per user per day inside the trial, and you have some intervention available — a reminder, a prompt to do the next thing, a dated summary of what the product has produced so far.
- **fails-when**: Inactivity is being caused by an onboarding failure rather than by a satisfied user drifting away, in which case a nudge at day five treats a symptom that began at day zero. It also fails if the intervention is another marketing email in a sequence the user is already ignoring, because the mechanism requires re-contact with the product's output, not with the company.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for any intervention. The association is reported in **Yoganarasimhan et al. (2023), n=337,724, randomised across trial lengths, data 2015-12-01 to 2016-01-06**: the authors "show that long stretches of inactivity at the end of the trial are associated with lower conversions." That is an observational finding inside a randomised experiment, and the experiment randomised trial length rather than any anti-inactivity intervention.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` *(the association comes from a large randomised study, but the recommended action was never tested by anyone; treating the inference as measured would be exactly the error this library's fifth folklore test exists to catch)*
- **sources**: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4507 (verified 2026-08-12) · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Define dead air for your product as some number of consecutive unused days in the final third of the trial, and log it as an event. Send the intervention as product output rather than as marketing — what the product made, what it found, what is waiting. Then measure conversion for triallists who hit the dead-air flag against those who did not, which is a cohort split you can read at low traffic even though you cannot A/B test the intervention. If dead air is common in the first third rather than the last, you have an activation problem and P-302 will not fix it.
- **example-copy**: "You've had Ledgerly for 20 days and processed 6 receipts. Here's your March summary so far — it takes one photo to add the rest."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's 30-day trial is precisely the shape this pattern warns about, because the freelancer signs up mid-cycle, processes a handful of receipts, and then does nothing until month end. If the trial expires two days before their reconciliation, they convert on a cold memory of six receipts. The fix is not a shorter trial, which would miss the cycle entirely, but a day-22 message containing the month's partial summary and the count of receipts still unphotographed.

### P-305 — Keep the free trial to one month or less, for a legal reason as well as a conversion one

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: New York's auto-renewal statute imposes a mandatory pre-charge reminder on free trials longer than one month and imposes none on trials of a month or less. The obligation is a real engineering and timing commitment, and it applies to any seller reaching New York consumers, which a self-serve web product does by default. Because the same boundary is where the conversion evidence also points, the length that avoids the duty is the length the randomised data prefers, and there is no trade to make.
- **works-when**: You sell to United States consumers over the web and your value moment fits inside a month. Both conditions hold for most consumer subscription products.
- **fails-when**: Your product genuinely requires a longer evaluation, in which case the duty applies and you build the reminder rather than shortening the trial to dodge it. Note also that this pattern addresses one state's trial-specific clause only, and it does not discharge the broader disclosure and cancellation obligations in P-306 and P-307, which apply at every trial length. **The statute's own territorial-scope provision was not read at primary here** — what was read and quoted is the trial-length clause — so treat "it applies to any seller reaching New York consumers" as this library's working reading rather than as verified text, and confirm applicability with counsel before a deliverable turns on it. **Statutes move: this status was verified 2026-08-12 and the New York provision was last revised 2025-11-07.**
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the length choice as a compliance decision. The obligation itself is documented: **New York General Business Law § 527-a** (signed 2023-12-13, amended 2025, page showing last revision **2025-11-07**, verified at `nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/527-A` on **2026-08-12**) requires, for free trials **longer than one month**, notice **3 to 21 days** before the trial converts to a paid subscription. Trials of one month or less do not trigger the clause. The conversion evidence pointing the same way is P-302's **+5.59% subscriptions at 7 days versus 30 days (n=337,724, randomised)**.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(statutory text read at the primary legislative source and dated; the convergence with the conversion evidence is a coincidence of direction, not a combined finding)*
- **sources**: https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/527-A (verified 2026-08-12) · https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4507 · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Set the trial at 30 days or fewer and record in the page brief that the length was chosen partly to stay under the New York threshold. If you keep a longer trial, build the 3-to-21-day pre-conversion notice as a system obligation with monitoring, not as a marketing email that can be paused during a campaign freeze. Send a pre-charge reminder at every trial length regardless, because P-187 already requires it as a conversion and trust device and the statutory version is then a subset of what you ship.
- **example-copy**: "Your free month ends on 12 September. We'll email you on the 10th before anything is charged."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's 30-day trial sits exactly at the boundary and does not trigger the New York reminder duty, while its P-187 reminder on day 28 satisfies the spirit of it anyway. A 45-day trial, which someone will eventually propose to cover two bookkeeping cycles, would convert the reminder from a good practice into a statutory obligation with a defined window, and it is not worth it for a second cycle the buyer has already seen once.

### P-306 — Build to the vacated click-to-cancel standard, because the statutes underneath it never went away

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The federal negative-option rule that would have codified click-to-cancel was vacated on procedural grounds, and a replacement rulemaking has restarted at its earliest stage. That change is easy to misread in both directions. It does not mean subscriptions became unregulated, because ROSCA is a statute and the vacatur of an agency rule did nothing to it, and it does not mean a new rule is imminent, because the rulemaking is at the advance-notice stage. The practical consequence is that the compliance target is unchanged, so the correct posture is to build to the vacated rule's standard and stop tracking the docket as though it were a deadline.
- **works-when**: Always, for a self-serve subscription reaching United States consumers. Building to the stricter standard costs a cancellation link and an honest disclosure, and it is defensible under any future version of the rule.
- **fails-when**: You read the vacatur as permission. The exposure that actually produces complaints and enforcement runs through ROSCA and the state statutes rather than through the vacated rule, and *FTC v. JustAnswer* (P-201) was pleaded on ROSCA counts, not on the negative-option rule. This pattern also fails as a substitute for P-201, which remains a same-week verification gate, because **every status below is dated and at least one element of it is live-moving.**
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The status, as verified on **2026-08-12**: the revised Negative Option Rule had a compliance date of **2025-05-14** with enforcement postponed to **2025-07-14** `[secondary — multiple law-firm alerts]`; the **Eighth Circuit vacated the Rule in its entirety, per curiam, on 2025-07-08**, on the procedural ground that the FTC skipped a required preliminary regulatory analysis `[secondary — Mayer Brown, Covington, Greenberg Traurig and Sheppard Mullin alerts, all July 2025]`; the FTC submitted a draft advance notice to OMB on **2026-01-30** `[secondary — Crowell alert 2026-02-11]`; the FTC issued an **Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking on 2026-03-11**, published in the Federal Register on **2026-03-13** as "Rule Concerning the Use of Prenotification Negative Option Plans", action "Advance notice of proposed rulemaking; request for public comment", **document 2026-04952** `[primary — Federal Register API, verified 2026-08-12]`; and the **comment period closed 2026-04-13** `[primary — same document]`. **As of 2026-08-12 no NPRM has issued**, an advance notice is the step before a proposed rule, and Kirkland's 2026-03-19 alert states that "A final rule is likely years away." What remains in force regardless: the original **1973 Negative Option Rule, 16 CFR Part 425**, which governs prenotification plans and is the vehicle the 2026 advance notice proposes to amend; **ROSCA, 15 U.S.C. §§ 8401–8405**, which requires clear and conspicuous disclosure of material terms, express informed consent before charging, and "simple mechanisms for a consumer to stop recurring charges"; **FTC Act Section 5**; and the Telemarketing Sales Rule where applicable.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the Federal Register document and its comment dates were read at a primary API; the vacatur date and the postponement come from converging law-firm alerts because ftc.gov returns 403 to automated fetch, so they are `[secondary]` with multiple independent carriers)*
- **sources**: Federal Register API, document `2026-04952` (verified 2026-08-12) · Kirkland, Covington, Manatt, Troutman alerts 2026-03-11→13; Kirkland 2026-03-19 · Mayer Brown, Covington, Greenberg Traurig, Sheppard Mullin alerts, July 2025 · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Write the cancellation flow to the vacated rule's standard — an online cancellation path for an online signup, no worse than the signup path, with no mandatory human contact — and treat that as settled rather than as a bet on the rulemaking. Disclose the recurring price and cadence at the same visual weight as the rest of the payment form and capture consent separately, which is what ROSCA requires and what the JustAnswer complaint alleges was missing. **Re-verify this status before relying on it beyond roughly Q1 2027**, per P-201, and re-verify it immediately if a deliverable turns on whether an NPRM has issued.
- **example-copy**: n/a (compliance posture)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's cancel path is two clicks from the account page with no email to anyone, which is what P-200 already promises on the pricing page. Nothing about the vacatur changes that, and a founder who reads the headline as "click-to-cancel is dead" and routes cancellation through support would be adding ROSCA exposure in exchange for retention the save flow is not allowed to capture that way anyway.

### P-307 — Meet the state auto-renewal floor, and put the save offer beside the cancel path rather than in front of it

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: With the federal rule vacated, the binding constraints on a consumer subscription are the state auto-renewal statutes, and two of them are strict enough to set the design. California requires online cancellation for online signups and retained proof of consent. New York requires cancellation parity through the same medium the consumer used to consent. Both are drafted around the observation that sellers comply with the letter of a cancellation right while making it expensive to exercise, which is why the California amendment reaches the save flow itself and constrains what may be shown before the cancel button.
- **works-when**: You sell to United States consumers. Jurisdiction follows the customer, so a single-market assumption is not available to a self-serve web product.
- **fails-when**: You treat a retention offer as a gate. California's amended law requires the offer to be presented **alongside** a click-to-cancel path rather than instead of one, so a save flow that must be dismissed before the cancel control appears is the specific construction the statute addresses. It also fails if consent records are not retained, because the statute sets a retention period and an unrecorded consent is functionally an absent one. **Statutes move: verified 2026-08-12.**
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. **California:** the Automatic Renewal Law as amended by **AB 2863**, signed **2024-09-24**, amending **Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17601 and 17602**, **effective 2025-07-01** and applying to contracts "entered into, amended, or extended on or after" that date (§ 17601(b), § 17602(j)); verified against the bill text at `leginfo.legislature.ca.gov` on **2026-08-12**. It requires express affirmative consent recorded and retained for **3 or more years**, clear and conspicuous plain-language disclosure, **online cancellation for online signups reachable without speaking to anyone**, annual renewal reminders, **7 to 30 days' notice before a price change**, and it constrains save-flow dark patterns by requiring a retention offer to sit alongside a click-to-cancel path. **New York:** **GBL § 527-a**, signed **2023-12-13**, amended 2025, last revision shown **2025-11-07**, verified at `nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/527-A` on **2026-08-12**. Verbatim, § 527-a(d), a business may not "fail to provide the consumer with the option to cancel at any time using a **simple cancellation mechanism that is as easy to use as the mechanism that the consumer used to provide consent and that is through the same medium** that the consumer used to provide consent." It further requires material terms clear and conspicuous before consent, including "the deadline by date or frequency by which the consumer must act to prevent or stop further charges", and renewal notice **15 to 45 days** ahead for terms of six months or more.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(statutory and bill text read at the primary legislative sources and dated; no measured effect of compliance on any commercial metric exists)*
- **sources**: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov (AB 2863, verified 2026-08-12) · https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/527-A (verified 2026-08-12) · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Build one cancellation flow that satisfies the strictest of the statutes you are exposed to rather than branching by state, because branching costs more than compliance. Make the cancel control visible on the same screen as any save offer, so that the offer is a choice rather than a step. Record the consent event with its timestamp and the exact disclosure text shown, and keep it for at least three years. Send the annual renewal reminder and the 7-to-30-day price-change notice as system obligations with monitoring. Layer 11 owns what the save flow may contain; this pattern owns the constraint that it may not stand in the way.
- **example-copy**: "Cancel your subscription" as a visible control on the same screen as any pause or discount offer, not behind it. Consent line at signup: "I agree to be charged $9 a month, starting 14 September, until I cancel."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's cancellation screen offers a pause and a downgrade to the 3-receipt demo, and it shows "Cancel my subscription" as a control of equal prominence on the same screen. The consent record stores the timestamp, the price shown, and the disclosure string, which costs one database row at signup and is the only evidence that would exist if the disclosure were ever questioned.

### P-308 — Perform the withdrawal-waiver ritual before the first delivery, or the consumption fence is worth nothing

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The consumption fence in P-196 refuses a refund on the basis that value was already delivered, and in the EU and the UK that argument is only available if a specific three-part ritual was performed before delivery began. The ritual is not paperwork for its own sake. It is the mechanism by which the buyer knowingly trades a statutory right for immediate access, and the sanction for skipping any part of it is not a reduced refund but a complete one, with the buyer keeping whatever was delivered. That asymmetry is what makes this a design constraint rather than a legal footnote.
- **works-when**: You sell digital content to EU or UK consumers, delivery begins during the withdrawal period, and you can capture the consent and the acknowledgment as separate, recorded, pre-delivery actions with a durable-medium confirmation afterwards.
- **fails-when**: Any one of the three elements is missing, in which case the fence collapses entirely rather than partially. It also fails if the offering is characterised as a service rather than as digital content, because a different article then governs and only a pro-rata refund is available regardless of consumption — which is P-309. And it fails if the consent is buried in accepted terms rather than captured as its own act, because the provision requires prior express consent and a separate acknowledgment of what is being given up.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The obligation is documented at primary. **EU Consumer Rights Directive 2011/83/EU, Article 16(m)** (consolidated text 02011L0083-20220528, verified on EUR-Lex **2026-08-12**): the right of withdrawal does not apply to "digital content which is not supplied on a tangible medium if the performance has begun", but only where the consumer gave "prior express consent to begin the performance during the right of withdrawal period", the consumer "acknowledged that he thereby loses his right of withdrawal", and the trader provided "confirmation in accordance with Article 7(2) or Article 8(7)" on a durable medium. **Article 14(4)(b) is the sanction:** where consent, acknowledgment or confirmation was missing, the consumer "shall bear no cost for" the "supply, in full or in part, of digital content which is not supplied on a tangible medium". Not a reduced cost — **no cost.** **UK Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013, reg. 37** (verified at `legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/regulation/37` on **2026-08-12**) mirrors it; verbatim, reg. 37(2): "The consumer ceases to have the right to cancel such a contract under regulation 29(1) if, before the end of the cancellation period, supply of the digital content has begun after the consumer has given the consent and acknowledgement required by paragraph (1)."
- **evidence**: `medium` *(consolidated directive text and the UK statutory instrument both read at primary sources and dated; no measured commercial effect, and no case outcome is cited here)*
- **sources**: EUR-Lex consolidated text 02011L0083-20220528, Articles 16(m), 14(3), 14(4)(b) (verified 2026-08-12) · https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/regulation/37 (verified 2026-08-12) · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Put the consent and the acknowledgment on the screen immediately before the first delivery, as two things the buyer actively does rather than as a term they accepted at signup. Then send the confirmation email, and keep it, because the durable-medium confirmation is the element most often forgotten and its absence alone voids the fence. Only after all three exist may your refund policy state a consumption fence for EU and UK buyers, and the policy should name the jurisdictions it applies to rather than implying a single global rule. **The UK subscription regime under the DMCC Act 2024 is not yet in force** — originally slated for spring 2026, moved to autumn 2026, and now scheduled for **spring 2027** per the Government's consultation response updated **2026-04-02** — and when it arrives it adds a 14-day cooling-off right that reopens on renewal, which is stricter than today's position and worth designing toward.
- **example-copy**: "Start processing now. I understand that once processing begins, I give up my right to cancel this order and get my money back." — presented as a distinct checkbox above the start button, followed by a confirmation email restating both sentences. Check the current withdrawal-period length for the jurisdiction before naming any number of days in this string.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly processes a receipt the moment it is uploaded, which is the performance beginning. For an EU or UK buyer, the first upload screen carries the consent and the acknowledgment as one explicit control, and the confirmation email restates them. Without that, a buyer in Berlin who uploads 400 receipts in one evening and withdraws on day three keeps every categorised entry and pays nothing, and the "fewer than 10 receipts" fence in P-196 has no effect on the outcome.

### P-309 — Decide whether you are selling digital content or a service before you write the refund policy

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The same subscription can be characterised as digital content or as a service, and the characterisation decides which refund fence exists at all. Digital content, with the P-308 ritual performed, extinguishes the withdrawal right and lets you keep the full amount. The same offering framed as a service falls under a different article, where a withdrawing consumer pays only an amount proportionate to what was provided, and no full-retention fence is available regardless of how much was consumed. A product that delivers discrete generated artefacts on a subscription can plausibly be either, which means the characterisation will be made — the only question is whether you make it deliberately or discover it in a dispute.
- **works-when**: The offering has a defensible reading as digital content, meaning the buyer receives files or artefacts rather than ongoing labour, and your marketing copy, terms and delivery model all describe it the same way.
- **fails-when**: The three descriptions disagree. Terms that say "digital content" against a page that sells "your dedicated analyst" invite the less favourable reading. It also fails where the offering is genuinely a service, in which case the honest move is to design around pro-rata rather than to relabel — the label does not change what you deliver, and the pro-rata outcome is not catastrophic, only different from what a consumption fence would have produced.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The fork is documented in the same consolidated text verified on **2026-08-12**. Under **Article 16(m)**, digital content plus the completed ritual extinguishes the withdrawal right. Under **Article 14(4)(b)**, digital content with the ritual botched means the consumer "shall bear no cost". Under **Article 14(3)**, which governs where the offering is a service rather than digital content, a withdrawing consumer pays "an amount which is in proportion to what has been provided" up to the point of withdrawal. The three outcomes are therefore keep 100%, keep 0%, and keep pro rata, and the characterisation plus the ritual selects between them.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(directive text read at primary and dated; how any particular offering would be characterised in a dispute is not established here and would be a legal question, not a pricing one)*
- **sources**: EUR-Lex consolidated text 02011L0083-20220528, Articles 16(m), 14(3), 14(4)(b) (verified 2026-08-12) · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Write down which of the two your offering is, in one sentence, and then read your pricing page, your terms and your delivery flow against it. Align the three or change the characterisation. If you land on digital content, P-308's ritual is mandatory and the consumption fence in P-196 becomes available. If you land on service, drop the fence for EU and UK buyers and publish a pro-rata rule instead, which is P-199's time basis and is the rule Stripe's proration machinery natively computes anyway (P-310).
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly delivers categorised expense entries as files the buyer keeps and exports, and its page sells a tool rather than a bookkeeper, so digital content is the defensible reading and Ledgerly should commit to it explicitly. The consequence is that the P-308 ritual becomes a build item on the first-upload screen, and only then does the "fewer than 10 receipts" fence in P-196 mean anything to a European customer.

### P-310 — Cancel at period end and grant refunds as a separate, explicit act

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: Stripe's cancellation machinery has two behaviours that diverge sharply from what a customer expects, and the gap between them is where refund disputes are manufactured. A cancellation at period end generates no proration at all, so the customer simply keeps access until the period runs out. A cancellation dated mid-period does generate a proration, but the resulting credit is not money returned to the card; it sits on the customer balance unless someone refunds it manually. Choosing the first behaviour and issuing any refund as a deliberate, separately-granted act keeps the platform's default and the customer's expectation aligned, and removes the need to write custom proration arithmetic.
- **works-when**: You bill subscriptions on Stripe and can honour a "keeps access to the end of the paid period" promise, which is what most consumer subscriptions already do.
- **fails-when**: You have sold a long prepaid term where running out the period is not an acceptable answer, in which case you owe a real refund and should issue it explicitly rather than by setting a mid-period cancellation date and assuming the platform will do it. It also fails where a scheduled cancellation falls outside the current period: Stripe's documentation states that "if you cancel the subscription for a date outside the current period, you can't disable the proration", so the proration arrives whether or not you wanted it.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The platform behaviour is documented, verbatim from `docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/prorations` (accessed **2026-08-12**): "Negative prorations aren't automatically refunded and positive prorations aren't immediately billed, although you can do both manually." Setting `cancel_at_period_end` generates no proration and the customer keeps access to the end of the period, whereas setting `cancel_at` mid-period does trigger proration. Same source on the basis and granularity: prorations are computed as "Unused time on original plan" against "Remaining time on new plan", and "By default, Stripe calculates prorations down to the second."
- **evidence**: `medium` *(platform documentation read at primary and dated; the architectural recommendation that follows from it is this library's, and no operator data compares the two constructions)*
- **sources**: https://docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/prorations (accessed 2026-08-12) · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Make `cancel_at_period_end` the default path for every self-serve cancellation, and say on the page and in the confirmation what the buyer keeps and until when. Where you decide a refund is warranted, issue it as an explicit refund to the original payment method and send a message saying the money is going back to the card, with the amount. Never let a mid-period cancellation stand in for a refund, and check any automation that sets `cancel_at` to a date outside the current period, because the proration there is not optional.
- **example-copy**: "Cancelled. You keep full access until 14 September, and you won't be charged again." Refund confirmation: "We've refunded $9 to the card you paid with. It usually lands within a few business days."
- **worked-example**: A Ledgerly customer cancels on the 3rd of a period that runs to the 14th. Under `cancel_at_period_end` they keep the remaining eleven days, no proration is computed, and nothing needs refunding. If the founder instead decides to return the month as goodwill, that is a separate refund of $9 to the card with its own message, which costs one action and removes every ambiguity about what was promised.

### P-311 — Build the consumption fence in application logic and disclose it at checkout, because the platform prorates on time

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: P-196 argues for fencing refunds on units consumed rather than on the calendar, and the platform underneath cannot express that. Stripe's proration basis is time, not consumption, and its usage-based billing is explicitly excluded from proration altogether. Any consumption fence is therefore application logic that the payment processor will not enforce and the card networks will not infer, which has one hard consequence: the fence has to be disclosed at checkout, in the buyer's own view, or it is a rule that exists only in your head at the moment the buyer disputes the charge.
- **works-when**: The product has a countable unit (P-196's precondition), you can compute consumption at refund time from data you already hold, and you can show the buyer their own count.
- **fails-when**: The fence is written only in a terms page the buyer never opened, in which case a dispute is adjudicated on what was visible at checkout and the fence is worth nothing. It also fails if you try to implement it as proration rather than as policy — Stripe's stated position is that "Usage-based billing isn't subject to proration", so a unit-basis proration must be computed by hand and pushed in as invoice items, which is custom arithmetic in the highest-stakes part of the billing system. And it fails wherever P-308's ritual was not performed for an EU or UK buyer, because the fence is void there regardless of what the checkout said.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The platform constraint is documented, verbatim from `docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/prorations` (accessed **2026-08-12**): proration is computed as "Unused time on original plan" against "Remaining time on new plan"; "By default, Stripe calculates prorations down to the second", with granularity coarsenable to day, hour, week or month but the basis remaining time; and "Usage-based billing isn't subject to proration." To prorate by units consumed you must compute the amount yourself and pass `add_invoice_items` with a negative `unit_amount`, or run custom proration scripts.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(platform documentation read at primary and dated; the disclosure recommendation is a prior, and no source measures dispute rates for disclosed versus undisclosed consumption fences — see `## Where this layer is thin`, item 6)*
- **sources**: https://docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/prorations (accessed 2026-08-12) · L18b lane research, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Write the fence as one sentence at checkout, above the button, in the same words the account page will later use. Show the consumed count in the account UI so eligibility is self-serve. Compute the refund decision in your own code at refund time, and issue the result as an explicit refund per P-310 rather than as a proration. Do not attempt unit-basis proration inside Stripe unless you are prepared to own the arithmetic, and if you are not, publish the time basis instead and say so, which is P-199's recommendation.
- **example-copy**: "Full refund if you've processed fewer than 10 receipts. Your count is on your account page." — shown at checkout, not only in the terms.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's fence is "fewer than 10 receipts". Stripe knows nothing about receipts, so the fence lives in Ledgerly's code and is applied when a refund is requested. The load-bearing detail is that the sentence appears on the checkout screen: a buyer who processed 400 receipts in one evening and then asks for a refund is being refused on a rule they saw before paying, which is a conversation, whereas the same refusal from an unread terms page is a chargeback.

### P-312 — Refund in full or not at all, because a partial refund keeps the chargeback door open at 100%

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A fully refunded payment cannot be disputed at all — the card networks close the path. A *partially* refunded payment remains disputable, and disputable for the entire original amount rather than the unrefunded remainder. So the posture that feels like a prudent middle course is the only one that carries full downside, and it carries it on top of money already returned.
- **works-when**: always, wherever you have discretion over refund size, and most sharply on subscription products where a mid-term cancellation invites a prorated gesture.
- **fails-when**: the amount is large enough that a full refund is materially worse than the expected cost of a dispute, or a statutory regime requires a proportional refund. Then refund partially with open eyes, log delivery per P-315, and expect to defend the full amount.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the frequency of this outcome. The rule itself is documented verbatim at `docs.stripe.com/disputes/prevention/best-practices` (accessed **2026-08-13**): "While customers can't dispute fully refunded payments, they can still dispute partially refunded payments. Card network rules even allow for a payment that has been partially refunded to be disputed for the full payment amount." Corroborated twice on the same date — "Disputes and chargebacks aren't possible on credit card charges that are fully refunded" (`docs.stripe.com/refunds`) and "Inquiries on partially refunded charges can still escalate to a chargeback" (`docs.stripe.com/disputes/how-disputes-work`). Stripe describes the resulting outcome as "an over-refund of more than the original purchase."
- **evidence**: `strong` — a card-network rule stated at primary by the payment processor and corroborated across three of its own pages.
- **sources**: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/prevention/best-practices · https://docs.stripe.com/refunds · https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/how-disputes-work (all accessed 2026-08-13) · L18b sub-lane research, 2026-08-13
- **how-to**: Decide refund size on dispute exposure, not on apparent fairness. Where you intend to refund at all and the amount is small relative to a dispute's cost, refund the whole charge and close the path. Reserve partial refunds for cases where the retained amount genuinely exceeds the expected dispute cost, and pair every one with the delivery evidence P-315 requires. Note also that a refund issued *before* a dispute does not erase the event at the network — "Monitoring programs don't consider refunds when identifying disputes" — so refunding generously does not protect the ratio that P-313 governs.
- **example-copy**: "We've refunded your payment in full. Nothing further will be charged." — the full-refund path stated plainly, because a customer who believes they were partly refunded is the one who disputes.
- **worked-example**: A product that grants a year's allowance on the first invoice and then closes the resulting arbitrage by revoking proportionally on refund has moved from a full-refund posture to a partial-refund one. It has closed the arbitrage and opened full-amount chargeback exposure on every refunded annual plan. The arithmetic that decides it is the retained amount against the dispute cost in P-314, and on a low-priced plan the retained amount rarely wins.

### P-313 — At low volume, count disputes rather than rating them, because five is the number that matters

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: Card-network monitoring programmes trigger on a count as well as a ratio, and the count is absolute rather than scaled to your size. A product taking tens of payments a month reaches a programme's count threshold long before its ratio would look alarming on a dashboard, and the ratio itself swings wildly when the denominator is small.
- **works-when**: any product under roughly 100 payments a month — which is every pre-launch and most early-stage products.
- **fails-when**: volume is high enough that the ratio binds before the count does. The thresholds then behave the way operators expect.
- **observed-effect**: **Visa VAMP**, effective **2025-06-01**, consolidating VAMP, VFMP and VDMP: non-compliant at a **0.5%** ratio **or a count of 5**; excessive at **1.5%** (2.2% CEMEA) or a count of 1,500. The ratio is `[TC40 fraud reports + TC15 disputes] ÷ settled transactions`, and Visa's own note states a transaction appearing in both is **counted twice**. **Mastercard ECM** requires both limbs — 100–299 disputes/month *and* a 1.5–2.99% rate — with fines from $1,000 in month 2 rising to $100,000 at month 19+, and exit requiring three consecutive months below threshold. Stripe's own guidance warns that businesses with "fewer than 100 payments per month" face outsized dispute-rate swings from one or two disputes, and states the industry line: "dispute activity above 0.75% as excessive." (Visa VAMP fact sheet and `docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs`, both accessed 2026-08-12.)
- **evidence**: `strong` — network programme rules read at primary, with thresholds stated numerically.
- **sources**: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs · https://corporate.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/corporate/visa-perspectives/security-and-trust/documents/visa-acquirer-monitoring-program-fact-sheet-2025.pdf · https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/measuring (accessed 2026-08-12)
- **how-to**: At low volume, track the raw dispute *count* as the operative metric and treat the ratio as secondary. Set the internal alarm at two, not at a percentage. Do not ask a cardholder to withdraw a dispute — Stripe is explicit that "a withdrawn dispute still counts as a dispute" — and where you agree the customer should keep the funds, accept rather than contest. Distinguish **dispute activity** (disputes received this month ÷ payments this month, the network's basis) from **dispute rate** (by charge date, which tells you which cohort went bad); Stripe's worked example gives 1% activity against 0.3% rate on the same data, and conflating them misreads your own position.
- **example-copy**: *(internal, not customer-facing)* "Alarm: 2 disputes in a rolling 30 days. VAMP non-compliant count is 5."
- **worked-example**: A product taking roughly 31 charge events a month sits far below any ratio threshold on a dashboard, and yet five disputes in a month — a 16% rate on that denominator — puts it in Visa's non-compliant band outright. The count binds first and by a wide margin, which is why the alarm is set on a count.

### P-314 — Price the dispute before you price the refund policy

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A dispute is not a reversed sale. It is the sale reversed *plus* non-refundable fees, staff time and a ratio consequence. On low-priced products the fees alone can exceed the transaction, which inverts the usual intuition that a cheap product carries cheap risk.
- **works-when**: any product priced under roughly $50 a transaction, where the fixed cost of a dispute is a large fraction of the charge.
- **fails-when**: high-ticket products, where the fees are noise against the amount and the decision is driven by the amount itself.
- **observed-effect**: Stripe charges a dispute **received** fee plus, for disputes initiated after **2025-06-17**, a **countered** fee — **USD $15 each**, so **up to $30 of fees on a single charge**. The countered fee is returned on a win; the received fee never is. Published win-rate bands by Radar prediction are **60% / 40% / 25% / 15% / 5%** across five to one dots, with Stripe's own gloss that "even in the most favorable cases, it's very difficult to overturn a disputed payment." Fraudulent disputes account for "over half of all disputes", and **80% of early fraud warnings convert into a fraud dispute** if unaddressed. Dispute window is typically **120 days** (180 for some local methods), response 7–21 days, lifecycle 2–3 months. (`support.stripe.com/questions/june-2025-pricing-updates-for-disputes` and `docs.stripe.com/disputes/best-practices`, accessed 2026-08-12.)
- **evidence**: `strong` — processor fee schedule and published win-rate bands, read at primary and dated.
- **sources**: https://support.stripe.com/questions/june-2025-pricing-updates-for-disputes · https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/best-practices (accessed 2026-08-12)
- **how-to**: Before setting a refund policy, compute the expected cost of the dispute it will produce: the charge, plus $15 that never returns, plus $15 at risk, against a win probability that tops out at 60%. Compare that number to the amount you would have refunded. On a low-priced product the comparison usually settles it immediately in favour of refunding, and the policy should say so. Contest only in the top prediction bands; in the lower ones the countered fee is a second loss.
- **example-copy**: *(internal)* "Contest only at 4–5 dots. Below that, accept: the $15 countered fee exceeds the expected recovery."
- **worked-example**: On a $5 top-up, a dispute costs $5 returned plus $15 received plus $15 at risk if contested. Contesting a one-dot dispute spends $15 for a 5% chance at $5. The pack is not worth defending at any prediction band, and the policy should refund on request without argument.

### P-315 — Log delivery, because it is the only evidence that makes the consumed-goods argument available

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: The strongest representment argument for a digital product is that the thing was delivered and consumed before the billing it is being disputed over. That argument is evidentiary rather than contractual — it needs a record that the customer actually received and used the output, and a record that does not exist at dispute time cannot be created later.
- **works-when**: any product delivering discrete digital output, and especially subscriptions where consumption precedes or coincides with billing.
- **fails-when**: Cartes Bancaires disputes, which cannot be contested at all, so no evidence helps. It is also unavailable where the product's value is access rather than delivered artefacts, since there is nothing discrete to point to.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for win rates using this argument specifically. Stripe names it as a sanctioned representment basis across the Subscription-canceled, Credit-not-processed and Product-unacceptable categories, verbatim: "If the digital product or service was consumed prior to the billing (in cases where billing occurs regularly, but consumption of whatever is being billed for happens prior to the billing)", and asks whether "the dispute amount exceeds the value of the unused portion." The evidence it names for digital goods is "an IP address or system log proving the customer downloaded the content or used your software or service", alongside the structured fields `refund_policy`, `refund_policy_disclosure`, `refund_refusal_explanation`, `cancellation_policy`, `cancellation_policy_disclosure` and `cancellation_rebuttal`. (`docs.stripe.com/disputes/prevention/best-practices`, accessed 2026-08-13.)
- **evidence**: `medium` — the argument and its required evidence are documented at primary; no published data establishes how often it succeeds.
- **sources**: https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/prevention/best-practices (accessed 2026-08-13)
- **how-to**: Log, per unit of output, the timestamp of delivery, the account, and an access record. Populate Stripe's structured policy fields at charge time rather than at dispute time. Note that this is a **usage basis**, not a calendar basis, which means it is the dispute-stage counterpart to the consumption fence P-311 says the platform will not enforce natively — the fence lives in your code, and this log is what makes it defensible afterwards.
- **example-copy**: *(evidence field)* "Delivered 2026-08-02 14:11 UTC; report opened by the account 2026-08-02 14:19 UTC and downloaded 2026-08-04."
- **worked-example**: A product selling discrete generated analyses can defend a cancellation dispute by showing the analyses were generated, opened and downloaded before the disputed billing date. Without a per-analysis delivery log it has only a policy page, which Stripe does not list as sufficient evidence for digital goods.

### P-316 — Treat refunds and disputes as substitutes, because tightening one fills the other

- **layer**: `risk-reversal`
- **mechanism**: A customer who wants their money back has two routes. Closing the cheap route does not remove the demand; it redirects it to the expensive one. A refund is a reversal you control, priced at the charge. A dispute is the same reversal with fees attached, a win rate below one, and a ratio consequence you cannot refund your way out of.
- **works-when**: always, when setting or tightening a refund policy, and specifically when a policy change is being justified by the refund rate it will reduce.
- **fails-when**: the refund demand is itself driven by policy generosity rather than dissatisfaction — a frictionless policy can manufacture requests that would never have become disputes. The substitution is real but not one-for-one, and no published figure establishes the conversion rate.
- **observed-effect**: The mechanism is inferred rather than measured, and the inference rests on a documented contrast. **Steam's** policy is frictionless (14 days, under 2 hours playtime; **48 hours** for renewable subscriptions) and its independently-surveyed refund rate is correspondingly high — **median 9.5%, mean 10.8%** across ~150 self-reported developer/publisher figures (GameDiscoverCo, 2025-02-04), with the gradients that higher price and lower engagement both raise it. Valve publishes no rate itself and declines to. Against that, the network side is unforgiving: refunds do not reduce the dispute count (P-313), fees are non-refundable (P-314), and only a **reversal within roughly two hours of capture** erases the event.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — the contrast is documented and the mechanism is well understood, but no study measures refund-to-dispute substitution and the Steam analogue is a poor fit for subscription software.
- **sources**: https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/steam-refunds-how-many-should-you (2025-02-04, ~150 self-reported figures) · https://docs.stripe.com/disputes/monitoring-programs (accessed 2026-08-12)
- **how-to**: When a policy tightening is proposed to reduce a refund rate, price the disputes it will produce before adopting it. State the trade explicitly in the deliverable rather than reporting the refund reduction alone. Where the product is low-priced, the arithmetic in P-314 will usually favour the generous policy, and that conclusion should be written down with its numbers so it is not relitigated on instinct.
- **example-copy**: *(internal)* "Policy tightening rejected: projected refund saving is smaller than the projected dispute cost at $15 non-refundable per event."
- **worked-example**: A product weighing a stricter refund window to cut a 4% refund rate should first ask what share of the suppressed refunds return as disputes. At a $15 irrecoverable fee per dispute and a win rate under 60%, converting even a quarter of them costs more than the refunds saved, and the ratio consequence in P-313 arrives on top.

## Interactions

**P-181 (entry mechanic) forecloses most of the rest.** This is the layer's spine. Choosing a hard paywall deletes P-184 entirely and makes P-190–P-195 load-bearing, because the guarantee is now your only reversal. Choosing a card-required trial makes P-187–P-189 load-bearing and demotes the guarantee to signalling. Choosing freemium makes almost everything here optional and moves the real risk-reversal work into Layer 03's free-tier design. Applying patterns from more than one branch is the failure this layer exists to prevent.

**P-183 (card-required, ~30%) versus P-184 (reverse trial, 70–80% claimed).** The two published estimates of card-required trial conversion differ by more than 2×. **They are not averaged and not reconciled here.** The surveyed 30% (n=200, Jan 2026) carries a stated sample, definition and window; Verna's 70–80% is one operator with no counterfactual. The L05 lane recommends preferring the survey, and this library follows that recommendation while recording the disagreement. If your deliverable needs a number, quote the 30% *with its definition attached* and state that a well-known operator source reports figures more than twice as high.

**P-185 (longer trials convert better) versus P-196 (the consumed-value fence).** For a product whose value is extracted in one session, these point in opposite directions: a long trial hands the whole product away for free, and no fence applies because nothing was paid. The decider is whether value is *episodic* (extract once → short trial or hard paywall) or *cyclical* (recurs monthly → long trial). Ledgerly is cyclical; an image generator is episodic.

**P-194 (trial or guarantee) versus P-193 (place the guarantee at the price).** These look contradictory and are not. P-194 says do not expect a guarantee to add conversion on top of a trial; P-193 says if you ship one anyway, ship it where it can signal. The resolution is that the guarantee's job changes rather than disappearing — and that means it should be *short*, because a long conditional guarantee is paying page real estate for a job that only needs a sentence.

**P-192 (no anti-guarantee on subscriptions) versus P-196 (consumption fence).** A consumption fence is the legitimate version of the instinct behind the anti-guarantee. Both refuse a refund; only one refuses it on a fact the buyer can check. If you find yourself wanting to write "all sales are final", write the consumption fence instead.

**This layer versus `urgency` (Layer 08).** Risk reversal and urgency pull opposite ways on the same buyer state. Reversal says *you can undo this*; urgency says *you cannot delay this*. Running both hard, on the same page, produces a page that argues with itself — and P-202's finding (that honest scarcity still costs benevolence) means the urgency half is spending the trust the reversal half just bought. Where they must coexist, put the deadline on the *offer* and the reversal on the *purchase*.

**This layer versus `trust-and-proof` (Layer 09).** P-193 (guarantee at the price) and P-222 (proof subordinate to the CTA) compete for the same block. The decider is visual contrast, not distance: if the guarantee line makes the button no longer the loudest element in the block, move the guarantee, not the button. P-236's pre-launch proof set and this layer's guarantee are also substitutes — a product with no customers has no proof, and a plain unconditional guarantee is the cheapest credible thing it can put in that slot.

**This layer versus `expansion-retention` (Layer 11).** P-200 promises the cancellation path is easy; Layer 11 governs what the save flow may offer once the buyer reaches it. The boundary is strict: a save flow that offers a pause or a downgrade is honouring P-200, and a save flow that adds screens, buries the confirm button, or requires an email is breaking it — and, per P-201, is doing so in the exact territory of an active federal complaint.

**This layer versus `experimentation` (Layer 12).** P-285 already establishes that structural experiments beat cosmetic ones; Adapty's cut is the mobile confirmation of it — **trial-structure experiments won on LTV 59.6% of the time versus 34.6% for visual and copy changes** (16k apps, 2026). Read as: if you can only run a handful of tests, spend them on P-181, P-183 and P-185, not on the guarantee wording.

**P-185 (longer trials convert better) versus P-302 (shorter trials win).** This is the sharpest contradiction in the layer and both patterns are kept, because they rest on different kinds of evidence and neither dissolves the other. P-185 is a cross-sectional correlation across 115,000+ mobile apps with no randomisation anywhere in it, and the confound is obvious: products that can afford long trials are products with long time-to-value. P-302 is a randomised field experiment on 337,724 web users with a stated mechanism, and it is the only randomisation in this library's entire trial corpus. **The evidence hierarchy favours P-302, and the cohort match favours neither** — the experiment is a decade old and from one company, and the mobile cross-section is the wrong billing rail. What decides between them for a given product is the shape of the value moment, exactly as the P-185-versus-P-196 note already puts it: **continuous value points to a short trial, cyclical value points to a trial long enough to span one cycle.** Do not average the two, and do not read "shorten the trial" as a move from 30 days to 14, because those two lengths performed the same in the experiment.

**P-302/P-305 (short trials) versus P-183 (require the card).** These compose rather than conflict, and the composition is the layer's most defensible trial configuration for a web product: a card-required trial of a month or less, with the dated reminder from P-187 and the visible timeline from P-189. The card does the qualification, the short length limits the dead air P-304 warns about, and staying under one month avoids the New York pre-charge reminder duty in P-305. What the composition does not give you is a benchmark to judge it against, which is what P-301 is for.

**P-196/P-199 (the refund fences) versus P-310/P-311 (what Stripe actually does).** The consumed-value patterns describe a policy; the platform patterns describe what the billing system will execute. They must be read together, because Stripe prorates on time and never on consumption, and a mid-period cancellation leaves a credit on the customer balance rather than money on the card. **A refund policy written without P-310 and P-311 will promise a behaviour nobody has built.** The resolution is architectural rather than editorial: cancel at period end, compute the fence in application logic, disclose it at checkout, and issue any refund as an explicit separate act.

**P-196 (the consumption fence) versus P-308/P-309 (the withdrawal-waiver ritual and the digital-content fork).** P-196's `fails-when` already flags that an EU or UK cooling-off right is not something a policy can fence away, and these two patterns supply the mechanism it was gesturing at. The fence is not simply unavailable in Europe; it is **conditionally** available, and the condition is a three-part ritual performed before delivery begins. Skip any part of it and the outcome is worse than having no fence, because the buyer keeps the artefact and owes nothing at all. Characterise the offering as a service instead and the fence is unavailable regardless of consumption. Sequence these before P-196, not after it.

**P-306/P-307 (the dated compliance statuses) versus P-201 (the blocking check).** P-201 is a process gate that refuses to state a status, and it remains the gate. P-306 and P-307 are its most recent reading, dated **2026-08-12**, and they exist so that a deliverable written soon after that date is not forced to re-derive the whole picture. The relationship is strictly one-way: **a status in P-306 or P-307 does not discharge P-201.** The federal position in particular is live-moving — the comment period on the advance notice closed 2026-04-13 and no proposed rule had issued at the time of writing — so re-verify before relying on any of it beyond roughly Q1 2027.

## Where this layer is thin

**This layer was reconstructed from secondary coverage after its dedicated research lane (L18 — risk reversal, trials and cancellation) died before writing its file.** Everything below is a named gap, not a hedge. A follow-up pass should treat this section as its brief.

**1. The guarantee-duration question is completely open, and it was L18's assigned verification.** Does a longer or unlimited guarantee reduce refund rate, raise it, or do nothing? Both directions circulate with equally thin sourcing (see `## Folklore`). **A follow-up needs:** the primary operator cohorts behind the 1-year-vs-90-day and lifetime-guarantee claims, with refund rates by month-since-purchase; any academic work on money-back-guarantee *duration* as distinct from presence (Akcay/Boyaci cover presence only); and, failing both, an explicit statement that the question is unanswerable from public data. **Until then, no deliverable may recommend a guarantee length on the basis of its refund effect** — only on the basis of what you are willing to honour.

**2. The 2026 regulatory status of click-to-cancel is not stated here and must be checked before any cancellation-flow recommendation ships.** P-201 is a process gate for exactly this reason. **A follow-up needs:** the current status of the FTC negative-option rule with its date; the state auto-renewal statutes that operate independently of it (California, New York and others impose their own cancellation-parity and disclosure duties); EU/UK cooling-off interaction with consumption fences (P-196), which is the point where this layer's most novel recommendation may collide with consumer law; and the disposition of *FTC v. JustAnswer* itself, which was a live complaint at the time of research.

**3. There is no web-native trial benchmark anywhere in the corpus.** Every trial-conversion figure in this layer is either mobile IAP (RevenueCat/Adapty, ~95% of the numbers) or a B2B survey at $50–$249 monthly ARPU (ChartMogul n=200). **Nobody has published Stripe-checkout trial-to-paid rates by trial length, by card requirement, or by price point.** A sub-$30/mo consumer web product is outside the frame of every cohort quoted here, in both directions. **A follow-up needs:** any Stripe-, Paddle- or Lemon-Squeezy-published aggregate on web trial conversion, and any operator cohort at consumer price points.

**4. Refund-rate data for software is essentially absent.** The only refund figures in the whole corpus are two mobile category cuts (Photo & Video **6.4%** global / **14.1%** APAC; MEA **2.5%** non-trial vs **3.1%** trial, Adapty 2026) and one self-reported operator claim (12% on a $197 product). There is **no** benchmark for refund rate on a low-priced consumer web subscription, no breakdown by refund reason for software, and no data on how refund rate moves with guarantee wording. The Accenture 68/27/5 composition that P-195 rests on is **consumer electronics returns from 2017**, and the inference to software copy is the L04 lane's, not the study's.

**5. Nothing here measures guarantee placement or wording.** No controlled test of guarantee placement was located in any lane. The three observed positions (page band, under the cards, beside the CTA) are prevalence from a 33-page census. Every wording recommendation in P-190 and P-193 is a prior.

**6. The consumed-value patterns rest on a single product, quoted second-hand.** P-196 is built on Midjourney's "fewer than 20 GPU minutes" fence, which **no fetch path could retrieve from the primary source** — it is quoted by two third-party articles that agree with each other. No other product in the AI-tools census denominates a refund in consumed units, so there is no second instance and no measured effect. P-199's proportional-refund arithmetic is derived, not observed: **no source publishes refund-rate or dispute-rate data for time-basis versus unit-basis refunds.** A follow-up should look specifically for chargeback-rate differences between the two constructions, which is where the real cost sits.

**7. The consumed-value problem for *LLM output specifically* is unaddressed by any evidence.** The patterns here transfer the Midjourney logic to generated text, but a generated document differs from a generated image in a way that may matter: the buyer can keep and use the text after a refund, without any artefact you can revoke. **Nobody in this corpus has tested a refund policy on a text-generation product.** The honest position is that P-196 is a reasoned transfer, not a validated one.

**8. Save flows are covered only from the promise side.** This layer says what the page may promise about cancellation (P-200) and what the law environment implies (P-201). What a save flow may *contain* — pause, downgrade, discount, survey — sits in Layer 11 and is sourced from vendor-published retention data (Churnkey, Recurly, Chargebee) with conflicting figures (38% vs 67% preferring pause) and obvious commercial incentive. A follow-up should establish whether any *independent* measurement of save-flow effectiveness exists at all.

**9. Nothing here is testable at COLD traffic.** At ~2,000 monthly visitors and ~40 customers, no pattern in this layer can be A/B tested to a revenue endpoint within a useful horizon. Every recommendation is therefore a prior, and the falsifiable moves are the cheap operational ones: does the trial-end reminder actually send, what fraction of converting trials fail to bill (the $197 operator test suggests ~22%, which nobody would notice without looking), and what do the last 30 refund requests actually say.

**10. The published-A/B discount applies throughout.** The two headline effects borrowed here — Blinkist's +23% and the $197 operator's +21% — have between them **no sample size, no duration, no significance and no stated stopping rule**, and one of them is not clearly an A/B test. Per the winner's-curse reasoning in Layer 12, read the direction as weakly informative and the magnitude as uninformative.

### The method note that travels with all of this

Several primaries block automated fetching — **ftc.gov and federalregister.gov's HTML both return 403** — and the Federal Register **JSON API** was used as the primary route instead. Where a claim below the pattern level rests on law-firm alerts rather than on the agency's own text, it is marked `[secondary]` in the pattern that carries it. **The Recurly 2026 trial-conversion figure is gated behind a report download and was not retrievable**, which is why the trend rather than the level is what this library quotes from Recurly.
