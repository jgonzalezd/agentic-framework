# Layer 04 — Price points

**Layer slug:** `price-points` · **Loaded at:** Phase 5

This layer governs the number itself: the level, the ending, the annual ratio, the volume-discount slope, the top-up rate, how tax and currency are displayed, and how the number changes over time without burning the people who already paid it. The thing that goes wrong most often is not choosing badly between $19 and $24 — it is spending the entire pricing conversation on the last digit while the *level* is 40% too low. Founders systematically underprice, then optimise the ending of the wrong number. The second most common failure is importing a famous pricing statistic across a margin boundary it does not survive: the "1% price beats 1% volume" argument that motivates most of the pricing canon is a `1/CM%` multiple, and at software margins it is 1.11×, not 5× (P-087).

## How to use this layer

Read **P-081 → P-085 first.** They set the level, and the level dominates everything else in this file by an order of magnitude. **P-086 → P-088** are the arithmetic that tells you whether a move is safe — run P-086 before any price change in either direction, and treat P-087 as a correction to be applied to anything you have read in the pricing canon. **P-089 → P-092** are the endings, and they are deliberately placed after the level and after the arithmetic because that is the order in which they matter; P-089 is heavily demoted from its folklore and should be read as a warning, not a tactic. **P-093 → P-098** are the structural ratios — annual, all-in display, volume slope, top-up — and any one of them can invert a ladder that Layer 03 declared sound. **P-099 → P-102** are how the number changes: over time, by cohort, by market, and by tax jurisdiction.

In a **COLD** regime, P-086, P-088, P-092, P-093, P-097 and P-098 are all pure arithmetic you can run today with no traffic — they are the highest-value patterns in the library for a product with 40 customers. Everything with a behavioural mechanism (P-089, P-090, P-091, P-095) is a prior you cannot test at that scale, and P-099/P-100 are the only ways to learn anything about price without a split test. In **HOT**, P-089's trade-off (conversion up, retention down) is the one thing in this layer worth instrumenting on both sides.

## Patterns

### P-081 — Settle the level before you touch the ending

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: The distance between $19 and $29 is a 53% revenue difference per customer. The distance between $19 and $20 is 5%. Any effect an ending can produce is bounded by a few percent of conversion, so an ending decision taken before the level is settled optimises a rounding error while the real money sits untouched. Endings are also the easiest decision on the page, which is exactly why they absorb attention that belongs elsewhere.
- **works-when**: always, and hardest to obey when the level is genuinely uncertain — which is when the temptation to do the easy decision is strongest.
- **fails-when**: the level is already externally fixed (a platform price tier, a regulated price, an app-store price point grid), where the ending is the only variable you have. Also fails as an excuse to defer the ending indefinitely: the annual-derivative constraint in P-092 means the ending and the level are not fully separable and must be resolved together at the end.
- **observed-effect**: The best subscription-specific ending dataset measures a 15–20% conversion difference between 9- and 0-endings below $200/month ARPU (Paddle/ProfitWell, "just over ten thousand potential buyers", published 2020-10-15). No comparable published figure exists for a level change, because level changes are not run as A/B tests — but the arithmetic is not in dispute: $19 → $29 is +53% revenue per customer, and the breakeven volume loss it can absorb at 90% contribution margin is 36.9% (derived via P-086).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the arithmetic is definitional; the claim that founders misallocate attention this way is Kevin Hale's observation, not a measurement)*
- **sources**: https://www.paddle.com/blog/end-prices-in-9s · https://www.startuparchive.org/p/yc-partner-kevin-hale-shares-the-four-common-pricing-mistakes-startups-make
- **how-to**: Fix the level from a Phase 3 method — the interview band, the comparables gap, or the EVE ceiling — and write it down as a round number before anyone says the word "ending". Only once the level survives P-086 and P-093 do you choose the last digit, and then you choose it under the annual-derivative constraint in P-092. If a conversation about the ending starts before the level is written down, that is the finding.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's real question is not whether Solo should be $9 or $9.99. It is whether Solo should be $9 or $14 — a 56% revenue difference across 40 customers, or roughly $200/month. At 90% contribution margin, moving Solo $9 → $14 (+55.6%) breaks even at `−55.6 / (90 + 55.6) = −38.2%` — up to **38.2% of units** can be lost before the increase stops paying. `[Ledgerly COGS — unknown; the 90% margin is a software-typical assumption, not a measurement, and every figure derived from it inherits that]`

### P-082 — Take the upper third of your derived band, not the bottom

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Every derivation method returns a range, and a founder standing in front of a range picks near the bottom of it — because the bottom feels safe, because a low price is the only concession you can make without asking anyone's permission, and because the cost of being too low is invisible while the cost of being too high arrives as a visible "no". The asymmetry in *felt* cost is the opposite of the asymmetry in *real* cost.
- **works-when**: you have a genuine band from a method — interview medians, a Van Westendorp PMC–PME range, a Gabor-Granger revenue curve, or the gap in a comparables table.
- **fails-when**: the band came from a method that anchors (a Gabor-Granger ladder you designed, a survey with a dropdown), where the "top of the band" is the top of *your own hypothesis* rather than of the market. Also fails when the constraint is genuinely adoption rather than revenue — a freemium upgrade price feeding an expansion motion, or a land-grab with a real network effect.
- **observed-effect**: Kevin Hale's first named pricing mistake: "most startups price their products too low because they think it'll help them grow faster" (Y Combinator, via Startup Archive, accessed 2026-08-12). In the illustrative Gabor-Granger ladder in `derivation-methods.md`, the adoption-maximising price ($9, 720 buyers) produced $6,480 while the revenue peak ($29, 380 buyers) produced $11,020 — the low price attracted **1.9×** the buyers (720 ÷ 380) and 41% less revenue. **That example is synthetic; the shape is the finding, not the magnitude.** In the illustrative Van Westendorp example, OPP sat at $21.00 against a PME of $26.50 — a **26.2%** gap between the timid choice and the top of the acceptable range.
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://www.startuparchive.org/p/yc-partner-kevin-hale-shares-the-four-common-pricing-mistakes-startups-make · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Westendorp%27s_Price_Sensitivity_Meter
- **how-to**: Write the band down with both endpoints before you pick. Take the upper third, then check two things: that a meaningful minority still calls it a bargain at that point (the "cheap" curve above ~20% if you ran a PSM; at least a third of interviewees naming a higher number than yours), and that the value ceiling from Phase 3 is comfortably above it. If you cannot bring yourself to take the upper third, price at the top of the lower third and schedule the increase in writing with a date — an increase you have already committed to is easier to ship than one you have to decide again.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Suppose Ledgerly's 15 interviews return a floor (median current spend on bookkeeping admin) of $8/month, a bargain line of $10, and an "I'd have to think about it" line of $22. The band is $10–$22 and the upper third is $18–$22. Solo at $9 sits *below the bargain line* — which is not a cautious price, it is a price outside the band on the wrong side. `[Ledgerly interview data — does not exist; run the sequence in derivation-methods.md before treating this band as anything but an illustration]`

### P-083 — Anchor the number to the alternative the buyer named, not to a competitor's list price

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: The buyer arrives with a reference price already installed, and it is whatever they would otherwise do — which is usually not your named competitor. Pricing against the competitor set instead of the alternatives set means anchoring to a number that is not in the buyer's head, and it is the specific blind spot the Van Westendorp method is criticised for having: "price/value perceptions are always relative to an internal point of reference," and the survey never surfaces that reference.
- **works-when**: always, and most sharply in categories with no established price, where the buyer's reference is a person, a habit, or nothing at all.
- **fails-when**: commoditised categories where buyers genuinely do compare only direct competitors on a spec sheet, and the list price *is* the reference. Also fails when applied lazily — inventing the alternatives set from your own imagination rather than harvesting it from interview Block 7 produces a comparables table that reflects your assumptions and confirms them.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The competing-alternatives frame holds that alternatives "include direct competitors, substitutes in adjacent categories, potential entrants, and — always — the status quo, or do-nothing option" (BRI Associates, accessed 2026-08-12). Nagle's version is the single NBCA question — "if I, the seller, did not exist, what would my customer do?" — and he names customer apathy as frequently the real competitor (Nagle & Müller, 6th ed., Ch. 2). Nagle's own cautionary case: a uniform maker believed it held 85% share; once department stores and discounters were counted as the real comparison set, its true share was 35%.
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://www.bri-associates.com/terminology-index/competing-alternatives · https://www.relevantinsights.com/articles/van-westendorp-price-sensitivity-meter/ · Nagle & Müller, *The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing*, 6th ed., Ch. 2
- **how-to**: Get the alternatives from the interview, not from a spreadsheet — the question is "if this didn't exist and never would, what would you do instead?" Annualise every alternative to cost-per-year-for-the-job and weight each by the fraction of interviewees who actually considered it. Then look for the **gap** in the price distribution rather than the average: the average across incommensurable categories is meaningless, and the gap is where you sit. Finish by writing the sentence: *"It costs less than [one unit of the expensive substitute], and it does [the thing the cheap substitute can't]."* If you cannot write that sentence, you have a spreadsheet, not a price.
- **example-copy**: "Less than one hour of your accountant's time, every month, for a year."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's alternatives set is not "other receipt apps". It is: the receipt-capture feature already bundled in the freelancer's accounting app; a shoebox and one miserable Sunday per quarter; a bookkeeper at an hourly rate; and doing nothing until the tax deadline. Three of those four are priced at $0 in cash and high in dread, and one is priced in the hundreds. The gap between them is where Solo belongs — and it is materially above $9.

### P-084 — Respect the ceiling of the decision neighbourhood your buyer puts you in

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Buyers do not price your product against its cost or its value. They price it against the category of thing they have decided it is, and each category has an observable ceiling that no product in that shape sells above. Crossing the ceiling does not produce a stretched price — it moves you into the *next* category, where you are compared against a different and usually better-resourced alternative, and you lose that comparison.
- **works-when**: your product has a recognisable shape that other products already occupy — a one-off report, a monthly tool, an hour of a person's time.
- **fails-when**: you genuinely change the shape. Adding a human being to the delivery moves you across the seam legitimately. It also fails as a permanent law: a ceiling is a snapshot of a market, and the teardown that produced it covered one neighbourhood at one date.
- **observed-effect**: Teardown of the personal-decision-help neighbourhood, **fetched 2026-08-12**: a one-off, self-serve, no-human, immediately-delivered personal deliverable bears **$9.95–$36**, corroborated across four independent products (UnderstandMyself $9.95; Enneagram RHETI $20 per test; 16Personalities $29; Enneagram bundle $40 → $36). **No researched product in this shape priced above $36.** Above it sits a structural gap: **$36–$39 is the seam** where the buyer's comparison set flips from "a report" to "a person" — $39 is the floor of the cheapest human video call in the same neighbourhood, whose ceiling runs to $2,500 for a named expert. The middle is empty.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(four independent products clustering tightly, one neighbourhood, one date; it is a census of a market, not a controlled test)*
- **sources**: L14 decision-neighbourhood teardown, fetched 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Before setting the number, write down the sentence a buyer would use to describe what they just bought — "a report", "an app", "a session with someone". Then find three to five products that would be described the same way and record their prices with dates. If your intended price sits above that cluster's ceiling, you have two honest options: price inside the ceiling, or change the shape so the sentence changes. Do not price into a seam; anything in the gap has to be unambiguously one thing or the other.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly is a monthly tool, not a one-off deliverable, so the $9.95–$36 one-off ceiling does not bind it — the relevant neighbourhood is prosumer utility subscriptions. The pattern still applies in the other direction to a potential Ledgerly one-off: a "year-end receipt pack — upload everything, get one categorised CSV" priced as a single purchase sits squarely in the no-human deliverable shape, and **$36 is its ceiling**, whatever the annual subscription implies. Pricing that one-off at $49 would put it in the seam and invite comparison with an hour of a real bookkeeper.

### P-085 — Price high enough that the price is not the evidence against you

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Price-quality inference. Where quality cannot be verified before purchase, buyers read price as a quality signal — and the inference operates on *experienced* quality, not only on stated belief. A price well below the category norm supplies an argument against you that no amount of copy retracts.
- **works-when**: quality is hard to verify before purchase (credence goods, services, tools whose value only emerges over weeks); the buyer has few other signals; the category has an established premium band.
- **fails-when**: quality signals are abundant — reviews, a real free trial, screenshots, a demo — in which case the buyer stops using price as a proxy. Also fails where a visible commodity floor exists in the category, and pricing above it reads as overcharging rather than as quality. And the lab estimates inflate the effect: Rao & Monroe found the observed size significantly influenced by experimental design and the strength of the price manipulation, and Völckner & Hofmann (2007) find the price→quality effect has **declined over time** as information availability improved.
- **observed-effect**: Rao & Monroe (1989) meta-analysis of **54 price–perceived-quality relationships**: positive and statistically significant price→quality and brand→quality effects for consumer products; mean effect size reported as **η²≈0.12** *(this figure comes from a secondary summary — the hosted PDF is image-only and could not be verified against the original; treat as approximate)*. Plassmann, O'Doherty, Shiv & Rangel (*PNAS* 2008, **n=20**): manipulated price labels raised both reported flavour pleasantness and medial-orbitofrontal BOLD activity, with no association between actual retail price and pleasantness under blind tasting. **Cohort note, carried in the same form as P-095: Rao & Monroe pool consumer-goods studies published up to 1989, and Plassmann is a 20-person wine-tasting lab. Neither observes a subscription, a software product, or a 2026 buyer, and Völckner & Hofmann find the effect has weakened as information became cheaper. What transfers is the mechanism — price is read as a signal where quality cannot be checked first — not the effect size, and certainly not a specific price.**
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002224378902600309 · https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0706929105 · https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-007-9013-2
- **how-to**: Do not set the lowest price you can survive on. **Run this pattern as a qualitative check rather than as a calculation, because the evidence behind it is 1989 consumer goods and a wine lab, and neither yields a number you can apply to a monthly subscription.** The practical test is the objection: if a prospect's most likely reaction is "this seems too cheap to be serious", you are under-priced. Raise the price and spend the margin on the signals — a real trial, real screenshots, a checkable proof number — that let buyers stop using price as a proxy at all. Where you cannot raise the level, at least stop signalling cheapness in the ending (P-091).
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly at $9/month against a job the buyer describes as "the thing I dread most all month" is inviting the inference that it is a weekend project that will lose their receipts. The countermeasure is not copy; it is $14 or $19 plus a visible export guarantee, because a freelancer's real fear is data loss at tax time and that fear is answered by evidence, not by discount. **The $14 and $19 come from Ledgerly's own band (P-082) and its ladder arithmetic, not from the price-quality literature — a 1989 consumer-goods meta-analysis and a wine lab cannot set a subscription price, and this pattern only argues the direction.**

### P-086 — Compute the breakeven sales change before any price move

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Elasticity estimates are expensive, unstable and rarely worth their cost. The breakeven sales change replaces the unanswerable empirical question ("what is our elasticity?") with a falsifiable judgement call ("do I believe volume will move more than *this*?"), and it costs nothing to compute. It converts a pricing argument into a threshold anyone in the room can accept or reject.
- **works-when**: any proposed price change, up or down, on a product with a known contribution margin. It is equally the right first move before matching a competitor's discount.
- **fails-when**: cannibalisation across your own tiers, incremental fixed costs, or competitor retaliation are material — each needs a variant formula (Nagle gives four). **And for a subscription it ignores churn and lifetime effects entirely**, which usually makes the naive breakeven too optimistic about a price rise and too pessimistic about a cut. The formula is a single-period contribution identity; it cannot see that a 10% increase which loses 8% of new signups may also move renewal 18 months later.
- **observed-effect**: Westside Manufacturing (Nagle & Müller, 6th ed., Ch. 9): a 5% price cut at a 45% contribution margin requires **+12.5% units** (500 of 4,000) to break even — i.e. it requires true price elasticity above **2.5**, which is above both published meta-analytic means (Tellis 1988, 367 elasticities, mean −1.76, bias-corrected ≈ −2.5; Bijmolt, van Heerde & Pieters 2005, 1,851 elasticities across 81 studies, mean −2.62). `no quantified effect published` for any aggregate benefit to firms that adopt the practice.
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: Nagle & Müller, *The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing*, 6th ed., Ch. 9 and App. 9B · Tellis (1988), *JMR* · Bijmolt, van Heerde & Pieters (2005), *JMR*
- **how-to**: Put `%breakeven(units) = −ΔP / (CM + ΔP)` on the whiteboard before the discussion starts, with ΔP and CM in consistent units — note the denominator is the **new** contribution margin. Quote the **unit** figure, never the dollar figure (`%BE($) = %BE(units) + %ΔP × [1 + %BE(units)]`), because the dollar figure is smaller and flatters the discount. Then convert the number into a demand on whoever is advocating the change: Nagle's move is to ask whether they will accept a quota increase equal to the breakeven in exchange for the discount authority. For a subscription, run the formula and then model the churn effect separately — the formula will not do it for you.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly raises Solo from **$9 to $11** (+22.2%). At an assumed 90% contribution margin: `−22.2 / (90 + 22.2) = −19.8%`. The increase is profitable unless more than **19.8% of Solo units** are lost. Cross-check the long way: old contribution $8.10/unit, new price $11 at $0.90 variable cost gives $10.10/unit, and `8.10 / 10.10 = 0.802` — you may keep 80.2% of units and still be ahead. With 40 customers, 19.8% is **eight people**, which is a number a founder can hold in their head and check against reality. `[Ledgerly COGS — unknown; 90% is a software-typical assumption and the whole result hangs on it]`

### P-087 — Do not import the "1% price beats 1% volume" multiple into a software margin

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: A 1% price change flows to profit undiluted — no extra unit is produced, no extra cost incurred. A 1% volume change carries its variable cost along, so it contributes 1% of *contribution* rather than 1% of *revenue*. The ranking price > volume > cost is therefore definitional at any positive margin. But the **size** of the advantage is exactly `1/CM%`, and that is the half of the claim nobody quotes. The canon's dramatic examples come from low-margin firms, where the multiple is large. At software margins it is close to a wash.
- **works-when**: you are using the price lever as an argument for *attention* — pricing genuinely does deserve more of it than it gets — and when you are reasoning about a low-margin line inside your business (hardware, human delivery, resold inference).
- **fails-when**: you carry the rhetoric into a high-margin web product and conclude that price work dominates growth work. At 90% contribution margin the price lever is 1.11× the volume lever, and a founder who reallocates a quarter's effort from acquisition to pricing on the strength of a 5× claim has been misled by a cohort transfer. This does **not** invalidate value-based pricing; it invalidates the slogan.
- **observed-effect**: The ratio of the price lever to the volume lever is `P / (P − VC) = 1 / CM%`. **At 20% contribution margin it is 5×; at 90% it is 1.11×** (derived from the profit identity `Profit = Q(P − VC) − F`; Simon, *Confessions of the Pricing Man*, Ch. 5, via the Pai summary, proposition 37). Related published magnitudes, and the reason this pattern must police its own file: **Simon-Kucher's widely-repeated "a 1% price increase yields ≈ +20% profit margin for the average US company" (Oct 2017) is a consultancy claim with no URL, no sample, no method and no margin stated, and it is the same `1/CM%` mechanism quoted without the denominator that produces it. It belongs to the barred "1% price → double-digit profit" family, it is recorded in this layer's Folklore table, and it must not be cited here or anywhere else.** Simon reports that a **2%** across-the-board rise with no volume loss would raise **Sony's** profits by **236%** and **Walmart's** by **41.4%** — *`[unverified — sourced only via published book summaries, not a page cite; verify against* Confessions *Ch. 5 before quoting externally]`*, and note the mechanism is `1/net margin`, so the figures say those companies have thin margins, not that price is magic. Managerial attention splits roughly **70% cost / 20% volume / 10% price** (Simon, anecdotal).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the ratio is definitional; the headline corporate figures are summary-sourced and one is explicitly unverified)*
- **sources**: Simon, *Confessions of the Pricing Man*, Ch. 5 · https://jamesclear.com/book-summaries/confessions-of-the-pricing-man · Simon-Kucher, Oct 2017
- **how-to**: Compute your own `1/CM%` before quoting anybody's price-lever claim, and write the multiple down next to the claim. If it comes out near 1.1, say so out loud in the deliverable — it changes which recommendations deserve the quarter. Then use the correct version of the argument: at high margin, price changes are *cheap to make and cheap to reverse in both directions* (P-086 shows a 10% increase surviving a 10% unit loss at 90% CM), which is an argument for **iterating** on price, not for treating it as the dominant lever.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly at 40 customers, all on Solo $9, is $360/month. A 1% price rise adds `0.01 × $360 = $3.60`/month of profit. A 1% volume rise adds `0.01 × 40 × ($9 × 0.90) = $3.24`. The ratio is **1.11×**. Both are worth about three dollars — which is the honest finding: at this scale neither lever matters, and the number that matters is the level (P-081), where $9 → $14 is +$200/month.

### P-088 — Treat cost-plus as a floor, and compute it at the 95th-percentile user

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Cost-plus is a floor, never a price — value-based pricing typically allows you to charge a great deal more. But where marginal cost is real and usage-linked, the *average* user's cost tells you nothing about risk: the heavy user determines whether a price is viable, and averaging hides the tail that eats the margin.
- **works-when**: any product with usage-linked marginal cost — AI inference, API resale, storage, human-in-the-loop delivery, support-heavy onboarding.
- **fails-when**: genuinely zero-marginal-cost products, where the floor is trivially low and tells you nothing — which is the normal and correct outcome for software, and is not a failure of the calculation. It fails destructively only when used as a *price*: Kevin Hale's second named mistake is founders who underestimate costs by leaving customer acquisition out of the margin entirely.
- **observed-effect**: a16z (2020-02-16) puts AI companies at **50–60% gross margin** against **60–80%+** for traditional software, driven by "the 25% or more of revenue that AI companies often spend on cloud resources", and argues the cost drivers are "structurally difficult to eliminate entirely, even with optimization" (human-in-the-loop data work consuming up to 10–15% of revenue). **That band is a 2020 essay and it has drifted: measured AI-product gross margins ran 41% in 2024, 45% in 2025 and 52% in January 2026 (`02-packaging-models.md` P-021), which is a moving series climbing through the middle of a band written six years earlier. Use 50–60% as a historical reference point, re-derive the current floor from your own cost-to-serve, and never quote the a16z band as today's benchmark.** In the illustrative COGS model in `derivation-methods.md`, moving one user from 300 to 3,000 monthly model calls dropped gross margin at $20 from **87.9% to 33.9%**.
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://a16z.com/the-new-business-of-ai-and-how-its-different-from-traditional-software/ · https://www.startuparchive.org/p/yc-partner-kevin-hale-shares-the-four-common-pricing-mistakes-startups-make
- **how-to**: Enumerate true COGS per subscriber-month — third-party inference, attributable hosting and bandwidth, **payment processing** (a real line routinely forgotten), support minutes at a loaded rate, any human delivery. Solve rather than mark up, because payment fees scale with price: `price = (fixed_COGS + payment_fixed_fee) / (1 − target_GM − payment_pct_fee)`. Compute it at your expected **95th-percentile** usage, not the mean. Then add CAC: a price that clears COGS but never clears acquisition cost inside your churn-implied lifetime is below the true floor. If the floor lands close to your intended price, add a usage cap or a metered dimension **before** launch, not after.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's per-receipt cost is an OCR call plus storage. At Solo ($9 for 100 receipts) with Stripe-style fees, the payment line alone is `(0.029 × $9) + $0.30 = $0.56`, which is **6.2% of the price** — a materially bigger share than at $20, because the fixed $0.30 does not shrink. `[Ledgerly OCR cost per receipt — unknown; no margin argument is possible until this exists, and the 95th-percentile Books user at 400 receipts is where it will bind first]`

### P-089 — Treat a 9-ending as a cheap test, not a law — the evidence for subscriptions is weak

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Left-digit truncation — buyers encode prices left-to-right, so $4.99 is represented nearer $4 than $5 — plus a learned "this is a discount" signal that operates independently of the arithmetic. Both mechanisms are plausible. Neither reproduces reliably in modern well-powered experiments.
- **works-when**: field-catalogue conditions of the kind Anderson & Simester tested, and mass-market consumer products where the ending costs nothing to choose and the field evidence is mildly positive.
- **fails-when**: **much more often than the folklore admits.** (a) Anderson & Simester themselves found $9 endings are *less* effective when a "Sale" cue is also present — the two signals compete, which points badly for anyone stacking six tactics on one page. (b) The two best modern replications are null (see observed-effect). (c) The Fenneman authors' own leading hypothesis is that consumers have developed compensatory heuristics — the effect may be **decaying as the tactic saturates**. (d) For premium or craft positioning a `.99` actively signals discount retail (P-085). (e) **For subscriptions specifically the evidence is weak in both directions**: the one substantial dataset finds a conversion gain paired with a retention loss (P-090), and a second SaaS source states flatly that no definitive A/B result exists.
- **observed-effect**: **Split, and contested.** Positive: Anderson & Simester (*Quantitative Marketing and Economics* 1(1):93, 2003) ran three field experiments with a women's clothing catalogue and found "$9 price endings increased demand in all three experiments", strongest for newly introduced merchandise. **The primary PDF is a scan with no text layer and could not be read**; the famous specific result — the same dress selling 16 units at $34, **21 at $39** and 17 at $44 — rests entirely on secondary reporting (`[unsourced against the primary paper]`; Neil Patel independently reports $39 sold best, corroborating direction but not magnitude). Null: **Fenneman et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2022-08-18 — large incentivised online experiment, N=266, 4,788 purchase decisions — 47.1% just-below vs 45.9% rounded vs 45.3% control, not statistically distinguishable; the authors explicitly failed to reproduce either a left-digit or a perceptual-fluency effect.** **Escher, Troll, Henjes & Loschelder, *Frontiers in Behavioral Economics*, 2026 — two preregistered high-powered studies, N=284 and N=417 (N=701 total) — price endings did not affect purchase intentions at all.**
- **evidence**: `medium` *(contested — the field/lab split is unresolved, and the modern preregistered evidence is null)*
- **sources**: https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/qmktec/v1y2003i1p93-110.html · https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0270850 · https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-economics/articles/10.3389/frbhe.2026.1828446/full · https://www.getmonetizely.com/faqs/
- **how-to**: Choose the ending last (P-081), pick it on the buying-mode logic in P-091, and do not spend an experiment slot on it. Never stack a 9-ending with an explicit "Sale" cue. Never use one if you are positioning on quality or craft. Above all, do not treat it as a lever you have already pulled: **if someone quotes you a double-digit conversion figure for `.99`, they are quoting 1990s catalogue data at a 2026 web audience that has seen the trick ten thousand times**, and two preregistered studies totalling 701 participants could not find the effect at all.
- **example-copy**: n/a (the pattern is the number)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's choice between $9 and $9.99 on Solo is worth at most a few percent of a 40-customer base — call it under a dollar a month in expectation, against an effect size that two preregistered studies could not detect. It is explicitly **not** on the test queue, and the deliverable says so, so the omission does not read as an oversight.

### P-090 — Price the 9-ending trade explicitly: conversion up, retention down

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: A 9-ending reads as a discount. That lifts conversion at low ARPU — and it selects for a more price-sensitive cohort that retains worse. For a transaction that is the whole story; for a subscription, where LTV is the game, the second half can swamp the first.
- **works-when**: ARPU under roughly $200/month, and payback is fast enough that the conversion lift is banked before the retention gap bites. Also when you are pre-product-market-fit and need volume to *learn* rather than to earn.
- **fails-when**: ARPU above roughly $5,000/month, where the effect measures near zero and a 9-ending reads as unserious in a contract; and any product where retention rather than acquisition is the growth constraint. Note this pattern and P-089 are kept separate deliberately: P-089 says the ending effect may not exist at all in modern data, and this pattern says that *if* it exists, it comes with a bill. Both are live; the separator is whether you believe a 2020 vendor dataset over two 2022–2026 preregistered nulls.
- **observed-effect**: Paddle/ProfitWell, published 2020-10-15, **"just over ten thousand potential buyers"**: conversion increased **15–20%** with prices ending in 9 versus 0 at ARPU **under $200/month**; the effect "trends to 0 with some natural variation" above **$5,000/month** ARPU; and customers acquired at prices ending in 0 "have roughly **10% better net retention** than those who converted with a 9 based price". Paddle's own explanation is selection — "discounted customers tend not to be the best customers for lifetime value and retention" — and their own conclusion is that this "is something you need to test in your business individually". **No sample breakdown, no confidence intervals, no significance published, and the publisher is a payments vendor.**
- **evidence**: `medium` *(one substantial vendor dataset with no statistics published; contradicted on the base effect by the preregistered nulls in P-089)*
- **sources**: https://www.paddle.com/blog/end-prices-in-9s
- **how-to**: Decide which side of the trade you need before you pick the digit, and write the decision down. If you are pre-PMF and volume is how you learn, take the 9. If you are optimising LTV, or selling above $200/month, use a round number. Either way, settle the level first (P-081) and check the annual derivative (P-092). If you do adopt a 9-ending, instrument **net revenue retention by acquisition cohort**, not conversion alone — reporting only the conversion side is what makes this trade look free.
- **example-copy**: n/a (the pattern is the number)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's growth constraint is retention, not signup: 40 paying customers churning at a consumer-typical rate lose roughly one and a half people a month, and replacing them from 2,000 visitors is the whole business. On that reading the round number wins, and Solo should be $14 rather than $13.99 — a decision taken on the retention side of a trade-off, and recorded as such. `[Ledgerly churn — no instrumentation; the retention-constrained framing is an assumption until a cohort table exists]`

### P-091 — Match the ending to the buying mode, and remember round prices are the ones people can repeat

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Rounded prices are processed more fluently and "feel right", which suits feeling-driven purchases; non-rounded prices cue cognitive processing, which suits deliberative, ROI-justified ones. A precise number also signals that a computation stands behind it, which makes it harder to negotiate away from. And a round price is the only kind a customer can carry out of the room in their head.
- **works-when**: you know whether your buyer is buying on feeling (identity, relief, confidence) or on analysis (hours saved, cost avoided, head-to-head comparison). Precise numbers earn their place inside quotes, ROI calculations, usage overages, and first offers in a negotiation.
- **fails-when**: the buyer has to remember or repeat the price — word-of-mouth referral cannot carry $47.53. It also fails for low-involvement impulse purchases and anywhere the price is a brand asset ("the $99 plan"). And note the ceiling on the whole pattern: in the same preregistered studies that found the price-image advantage, **price endings did not affect purchase intentions** — the precision effect showed up on perception measures, not on behaviour.
- **observed-effect**: Wadhwa & Zhang (2015), *Journal of Consumer Research* 41(5):1172–1185: rounded prices are more fluently processed and encourage reliance on feelings, and the "rounded price effect is mediated by a sense of feeling right" *(abstract-level reading only; full paper not accessed)*. Escher et al. (*Frontiers in Behavioral Economics*, 2026, **N=701 across two preregistered studies**): precise prices produced **superior price image** versus both just-below and round prices (Study 1 F=8.04, p<.001, ηp²=.054; Study 2 F=12.14, p<.001, ηp²=.056) — but **round prices were recalled correctly 84% of the time versus 4% for precise prices** (F=714.83, p<.001, ηp²=.836), with precise prices underestimated in recall 65% of the time versus 11% for round. Paddle's retention finding (P-090) points the same way for subscriptions.
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-economics/articles/10.3389/frbhe.2026.1828446/full · Wadhwa & Zhang (2015), *JCR* 41(5):1172–1185 · https://www.paddle.com/blog/end-prices-in-9s
- **how-to**: Classify the buying mode from the interview transcripts, not from your own view of the product: if buyers describe relief, confidence or identity, use round ($20, $100); if they describe hours saved, cost avoided or comparisons, precise or 9-endings are defensible. Use round numbers for every price a customer will say out loud, and reserve precise numbers for figures inside a quote, an overage rate, or a negotiation. Never combine a precise self-serve price with a referral programme and expect the number to survive the retelling.
- **example-copy**: "Solo — $14 a month." (round, repeatable) versus "Overage — $0.11 per receipt beyond your plan." (precise, derived)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyers describe dread and relief, not ROI, so the plan prices are round — Free / $14 / $29 — and the only precise number on the page is the top-up rate, which is precise on purpose because it is meant to read as a calculation rather than as a menu price.

### P-092 — Pick a monthly price whose annual derivative rounds cleanly

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: The annual price is a *derived* number, and derived numbers advertise their derivation. If the monthly price times twelve minus your intended discount lands on an ugly figure, you must round it, and the rounding silently changes the discount you told everyone you were giving. Choosing the monthly figure with the annual arithmetic already in view is free at the time and expensive to retrofit.
- **works-when**: any product with both a monthly and an annual price — i.e. nearly every subscription.
- **fails-when**: annual-only pricing (no derivative to compute), or usage-based pricing with no fixed period. It also fails if applied so rigidly that it overrides the level: a clean derivative on the wrong level is worthless (P-081).
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. This is an arithmetic constraint, not a behavioural claim. The observable market convention it interacts with: annual discounts across a credit-and-indie teardown sample cluster tightly at **16.7–25%** and are almost always expressed as *time* rather than percent — "two months free" (ElevenLabs, Fathom), "Save up to 35%" (Descript), 25% (TinyWow), 20% (Runway), 33% (Photoroom), with PhotoAI an outlier at ~57% (fetched 2026-08-12). **"Two months free" is the market default and it is exactly 16.67%.**
- **evidence**: `anecdote` *(the constraint is arithmetic; the convention is a small observed sample at one date)*
- **sources**: L15 credit-and-indie teardown, fetched 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Before fixing the monthly number, compute `monthly × 12` and `monthly × 10` (two months free) and check both are prices you would print. $19/mo × 12 = $228; at 20% that is $182.40, which you must round to $179 or $180, which then implies 21.5% or 21.1% — a discount you did not choose. Prefer monthly figures where "two months free" lands clean: $9 → $90, $12 → $120, $14 → $140, $19 → $190, $20 → $200, $29 → $290. If you want a percentage rather than months, choose a monthly figure divisible enough that 12× minus the percentage is a whole number.
- **example-copy**: "$14 a month, or $140 a year — two months free."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly is already clean and should stay that way: Solo $9 → $90/yr and Books $19 → $190/yr are both exactly two months free (16.67%). If Solo moves to $14, the annual is $140 and the framing survives untouched. If Solo moved to $13, the annual would be $130 — also clean. **$12.50 would not be**, and that is the kind of number a pricing discussion produces at 11pm.

### P-093 — Set the annual discount from your churn rate, then sanity-check it against the market median

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: The annual plan buys you the revenue a monthly subscriber would otherwise have churned out of, plus the cash today, minus the margin you hand over. The value of the first term is exactly a function of monthly churn, which makes the break-even discount computable rather than conventional. Most founders copy 20% from a competitor and never learn whether it was a gift or a bargain.
- **works-when**: you have a monthly churn rate, or can defensibly assume one.
- **fails-when**: very early, where churn is genuinely unknown — then use the market median and revisit at 100 customers. It also fails on a 12-month horizon alone: extending the horizon favours **monthly**, because a low-churn monthly subscriber keeps paying in month 13 while the annual subscriber faces a fresh renewal decision. Run 24 months if the product has a multi-year life.
- **observed-effect**: **Market:** median published annual discount **20%**, core band 16–25%, across **560 plans / 207 products** with both monthly and annual prices on live vendor pricing pages (CompareEdge, *SaaS Annual Billing Discount Report 2026*, published **2026-07-02**, updated **2026-08-10**); category medians run from Email Marketing 12% to VPN 80%, and CompareEdge itself notes gaps above 40% "represent introductory rates rather than genuine discounts". Vendr's 2024 dataset via Torii puts the **median annual prepay discount at 18%**; a secondary citation of Vendr 2024 reports 17% across ~6,000 transactions. **Do not average these with Vendr's ~10% figure via SaaStr (~mid-2023) — that is *negotiated discount off list*, a different quantity.** **Churn:** Recurly benchmark data (as of 2026-07) puts overall subscription churn at **3.60% monthly** (2.34% voluntary, 1.25% involuntary), SaaS 3.22%, education 4.99%, falling as ARPC rises from 4.29% in the $10–$25 band to 2.87% in the $100–$250 band. At 3–4% monthly churn the computed break-even discount is **~15–19%** — which is where the market actually sits. That convergence is the strongest argument for the market median being roughly right.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(CompareEdge is the only source with disclosed primary methodology and it is a comparison site, not a research firm)*
- **sources**: https://comparedge.com/reports/annual-discount-report-2026 · https://www.toriihq.com/articles/monthly-vs-yearly-saas-contracts · https://recurly.com/research/churn-rate-benchmarks/ · https://www.saastr.com/vendr-the-average-saas-discount-is-about-10-and-trending-down/
- **how-to**: Compute expected 12-month revenue from a monthly subscriber as `p × Σ(1−c)^k` for k = 0..11, divide by `12p`, subtract from 1 — that is your break-even discount. At 3% monthly churn it is **15.0%**; at 5% it is **23.4%**; at 8% it is **34.1%**. Give more than break-even only if you are deliberately buying cash to fund acquisition, and say so internally — that is financing, not discounting. If break-even comes out above 25%, churn is the problem and a discount is paying to hide it. Two credits nobody counts: annual billing also removes eleven payment-processing charges (twelve $20 charges cost `12 × ($0.58 + $0.30) = $10.56` in Stripe-style fees against `$5.87` on one $192 charge — $4.69/year recovered, ~2.4% of the annual price, offsetting about an eighth of a 20% discount). Express the result as "N months free" where the arithmetic is exact (16.67% = exactly 2 months; 20% = 2.4 months, so do not call it "2 months free").
- **example-copy**: "Pay yearly — two months free."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's Solo at $9/month. At an assumed 3% monthly churn: `Σ(0.97)^k` for k = 0..11 = 10.2062, so expected 12-month revenue is `$9 × 10.2062 = $91.86` against `12 × $9 = $108` — break-even discount **15.0%**. The shipped 16.67% ("two months free") is therefore **slightly generous** at 3% churn and a clear bargain at 5%. The recommendation is to keep two months free, because it is clean, conventional and within a point or two of break-even — and to recompute the moment a real churn number exists. `[Ledgerly churn — no instrumentation at 40 customers; 3% is Recurly's benchmark applied across a cohort boundary, and the whole calculation inherits that]`

### P-094 — Show one all-in number, and know exactly what transparency costs you

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Partitioned and drip pricing work because buyers anchor on the base price and adjust insufficiently for surcharges revealed later; by the time the fee appears the buyer is deep in the funnel and has already committed attention. The effect is driven by **sequence**, not by the existence of a line item. This is the best-evidenced effect in the behavioural price literature — and the honest use of it is defensive.
- **works-when**: mechanically, always. As advice: use it to *size the deficit you are choosing to accept* by being transparent, and to understand that a competitor's apparent conversion advantage may be fee-shrouding rather than product.
- **fails-when**: increasingly, legally and reputationally. The Morwitz (2016) review documents partitioned pricing backfiring when the surcharge reads as unjustified or arbitrary, when consumers attribute the split to deceptive framing, and when it violates fairness norms — and that mandatory all-inclusive disclosure regimes neutralise the gain entirely. Multiple jurisdictions have moved against drip pricing since the StubHub experiment ran. **For a subscription with recurring billing and a cancel button, a surprise fee at checkout poisons the renewal**, and the experiment measured a single transaction context.
- **observed-effect**: Blake, Moshary, Sweeney & Tadelis, *Marketing Science* 40(4):619–636 (2021), experiment run **Aug 2015**, **several million StubHub users randomised 50/50** between upfront-fee and back-end-fee presentation of a ~15% buyer fee: back-end-fee users were **14.1% more likely to purchase at least once** and 12.43% more likely to purchase per visit; conditional on purchase they spent **5.42% more per ticket**; total revenue rose **~21%** (the paper's decomposition computes ΔR/R = 19.52%, of which the quality-upgrade channel accounts for at least 28%). Even the most experienced users (top 6% of the base) showed a **15%** revenue effect, and back-end-fee users were **3.3% less likely to churn** — contradicting a backlash story within the observed window.
- **evidence**: `strong`
- **sources**: https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/stadelis/AIP.pdf · https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mksc.2020.1261 · https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Morwitz-Vicki-Wroe-Alderson-PAPER-JCP-2016.pdf
- **how-to**: Fold unavoidable pass-through costs — payment processing, VAT, platform fees — into a single displayed price and say that you have. Then write down the number you are giving up (roughly 14 percentage points of purchase probability at a 15% fee level) so the decision is priced rather than assumed, and so nobody later mistakes a transparent competitor's lower conversion for a worse product. If you genuinely must partition, show the total on the same screen as the components — sequence is the mechanism, not itemisation.
- **example-copy**: "$14 a month. That's the price — no processing fees, no per-receipt charges, no VAT surprise at checkout."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly has no shroudable fee to hide, which means the pattern applies as a **copy** decision rather than a pricing one: state explicitly on the card that the top-up is the only other charge that can ever appear, because a metered product generates financial anxiety and the FAQ already ranks overage second (Layer 05, P-137).

### P-095 — Make a component free rather than nearly free — $0 is a category, not a smaller number

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: The zero-price effect. Demand at a price of zero is far higher than any continuous demand curve through nearby positive prices predicts. Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely show this is not a ratio artefact and is not explained by social-exchange norms — it survives a negative-price control — and is driven substantially by affect: a free offer with no downside produces a categorically more positive reaction, not an incrementally better one.
- **works-when**: some component of the offer can genuinely be set to zero — onboarding, data import, the first N units, migration, a starter tier, overage up to a threshold. It also works for the *removal* of a small fee: killing a $2 charge buys more than cutting a $12 charge to $10.
- **fails-when**: the free item cannibalises the paid one rather than seeding it; when "free" invokes a price-quality inference that the paid product is also cheap (P-085); and when buyers are forced into deliberate cognitive evaluation — Shampanier et al.'s Experiment 6 showed that making buyers explicitly reason about the trade-off reduces the effect. A free tier also changes your unit economics before it changes your conversion rate.
- **observed-effect**: Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely (2007), six experiments. Experiment 2 (real purchases, **N=398**): moving from Hershey's 1¢ / Lindt 15¢ to Hershey's **0¢** / Lindt 14¢ — a 1¢ change on both — moved Hershey's share from **27% to 69%** and Lindt's from 73% to 31%. Experiment 3 (cafeteria field, real money): Hershey's **21% → 71%**, Lindt 79% → 29%, t(189)=4.7, p<.001. Experiment 4 (**N=342**) added a −1¢ condition: going from 0&13 to −1&12 left Lindt flat at 16% while Hershey's rose 34% → 50%. Experiment 5 (**n=243**) affect ratings: free Hershey's **4.10** versus 2.82 (1¢ Hershey's), 2.78 (13¢ Lindt), 2.97 (14¢ Lindt), t(113)=7.0, p<.001.
- **evidence**: `strong`
- **sources**: https://www-2.rotman.utoronto.ca/facbios/file/ZeroPrice.pdf · https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.1060.0254
- **how-to**: Find the smallest positive charge anywhere in your offer and ask what it would cost to make it zero. Charging $0 for onboarding, data import, the first month, or overage up to a threshold buys disproportionate response relative to the revenue forgone. Where the product itself cannot be free, make a *component* free and name it. **Never price anything at $1 when $0 is affordable** — you pay the entire behavioural cost of being "priced" for a dollar. Note the cohort: these are candy-bar experiments and a laboratory affect measure; the transfer to a $14 subscription is an inference, though the mechanism is about the *category boundary* rather than the magnitude.
- **example-copy**: "Your first 50 receipts are on us — no card, no trial clock."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's free tier at 5 receipts/month is arguably the wrong free thing: 5 receipts is not enough to experience the product working, so the zero-price pull attaches to something that cannot convert. The higher-value application is to zero out the **migration**: "we'll import and categorise last year's receipts for free" removes the switching cost that P-083's EVE names as the largest negative differentiation term, and it costs one batch job rather than 12 months of free allowance.

### P-096 — Keep the volume discount inside 20–40% unless the unit is genuinely fenced

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: A discount steeper than about 40% on a unit that is identical at every rung is a claim the buyer can check with a calculator. Products that hold a steep curve all do something else first: they make the unit itself worth less at the bottom, through output quality, tier-exclusive features, or rollover privileges. Without such a fence, a steep curve prices the entry rung as a penalty and invites large buyers to consolidate.
- **works-when**: you sell a metered unit across three or more rungs and can state plainly whether the unit is the same thing at every rung.
- **fails-when**: your tiers do not meter anything (a pure feature ladder has no per-unit curve to shape); or the entry rung is deliberately a paid trial rather than a volume tier, in which case its unit price is meant to look bad and should be excluded from the curve.
- **observed-effect**: Teardown of seven credit-and-indie products, prices **fetched 2026-08-12**, all per-unit figures `derived` from published monthly prices. **Shallow cluster (17–33%), commodity units:** ElevenLabs core 17.5% across a 200× volume range; Descript AI credits 27.8%; Descript media hours 32.3%. **Steep cluster (58–95%), fenced units:** PhotoAI **94.8%** (tiers differ in output quality); Bannerbear **69.5%** and Runway **58.3%** (tier-exclusive bandwidth and credit rollover). Reception AI sits between at 46.0%. **The practical band for a small product selling a uniform unit is 20–40% from entry to top — everything in the sample selling a truly identical unit lands there.** Two secondary regularities: the curve **flattens or inverts at the top rung in almost every product** (ElevenLabs Scale is worse per unit than Pro; Bannerbear Enterprise worse than Scale), because the top tier is sold on bandwidth, seats, support and terms rather than unit price; and **the entry rung is often not a volume tier at all** (PhotoAI Starter is 7.8× the next rung's per-unit price).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(seven products, one date, one adjacent category; a census of practice, not a test of effect)*
- **sources**: L15 credit-and-indie teardown, fetched 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Build the per-unit column for every rung including add-ons, then compute entry-to-top discount as `(entry $/unit − top $/unit) / entry $/unit`. If it exceeds 40%, either narrow it or write down the fence in one sentence a buyer would accept — a quality difference, a feature difference, or a rollover privilege. "Bigger customers deserve a better rate" is not a fence. You are allowed to stop discounting at the top rung; everyone does.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's curve: Solo `$9 ÷ 100 = $0.0900`/receipt, Books `$19 ÷ 400 = $0.0475`/receipt — a **47.2% entry-to-top discount** on a unit that is identical at both rungs (a receipt is a receipt). That is outside the 20–40% band with no unit fence; Books's differentiators (multi-client tagging, accountant seat) are *feature* fences, not unit fences. Two fixes: narrow the curve by moving Books to **$19 for 300** (`$0.0633`, a 29.6% discount, inside the band), or fence the unit — e.g. Books receipts carry line-item extraction and Solo receipts carry totals only. Narrowing is the cheaper answer at 40 customers.

### P-097 — Peg one flat top-up rate just above your entry tier's per-unit price

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: If a top-up pack costs less per unit than the plan it attaches to, the ladder inverts: customers stay on the cheap rung and top up forever, and the tiers cannibalise each other. Pegging a **single flat top-up rate** above the *entry* tier's per-unit price makes inversion structurally impossible rather than merely managed — and because in-plan unit prices fall as you climb, the same flat rate automatically produces escalating upgrade pressure at higher tiers, with no extra rules to write or publish.
- **works-when**: any metered plan with an allowance plus a top-up or overage mechanism.
- **fails-when**: you peg to your *top* tier's rate instead — then entry customers get an arbitrage and the ladder inverts at the bottom, which is the exact failure Phase 4.5 exists to catch. It also fails as advice for products that deliberately refuse top-ups altogether and force the tier upgrade (Bannerbear does this): that is a legitimate alternative answer whose cost is a hard wall for a customer mid-campaign.
- **observed-effect**: ElevenLabs Reception AI, prices **fetched 2026-08-12**, per-minute plan rates `derived`: a single flat overage of **$0.40/min** against in-plan rates of $0.3667 (Basic, `$22 ÷ 60`), $0.2750 (Plus, `$55 ÷ 200`) and $0.1980 (Premium, `$99 ÷ 500`) yields a top-up premium of **+9% → +45% → +102%** as you climb. Because $0.40 sits above the most expensive in-plan rate, no customer at any tier can buy top-up units cheaper than their plan's marginal unit. *(Caveat carried from the source: the plan is metered in minutes and the overage in credits, and the page does not publish the mapping; if credits map to chat minutes rather than phone minutes, every figure halves.)* A second mechanism observed in the same sample: **purchased top-up credits never expire while plan credits do** (Runway), a non-price sweetener that justifies a higher top-up rate without ever saying "we're charging you more".
- **evidence**: `medium` *(one exceptionally clean instance plus a consistent pattern across the sample; no controlled test)*
- **sources**: L15 credit-and-indie teardown, fetched 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Compute the per-unit price of your **cheapest paid** tier. Set one flat top-up or overage rate at or slightly above it — one number, publishable, no per-tier table, no footnotes. Verify the premium widens monotonically as you climb; if it does not, a tier's allowance is mispriced. If you want to charge more for top-ups than the arithmetic alone justifies, buy the difference with permanence: make purchased units never expire while plan units reset.
- **example-copy**: "Extra receipts: $5 for 50, any time. They never expire."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's current top-up is **$4 for 50 = $0.08/receipt**, against Solo's marginal `$0.09`. **The add-on is inverted**: a Solo customer buys top-up units 11% cheaper than their own plan's units, so the rational move is to stay on Solo and top up indefinitely — and Books never earns its upgrade. Fix: **$5 for 50 = $0.10/receipt**, which is `+11%` over Solo and `+111%` over Books — the same escalating shape as Reception AI's +9%/+45%/+102%. This is a Phase 4.5 structural finding and outranks everything else in the deliverable.

### P-098 — Grandfather existing customers and read the new cohort

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: A price increase applied only to new customers creates two cohorts differing in price and acquisition date and nothing else, which is the closest thing to a controlled price experiment available to a product with no traffic. It is also the version of a price rise that does not punish the people who believed you first — and you must never test on people who cannot leave.
- **works-when**: you are raising a price on a self-serve subscription and can segment billing by signup date.
- **fails-when**: you need a clean causal read — this is a quasi-experiment, confounded by seasonality, marketing changes and your own product improvements. And note precisely what it **cannot** tell you: nothing about the existing cohort's price sensitivity, because they never faced the new price. It fails outright if you grandfather forever, which permanently splits your billing surface and your support load.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for grandfathering itself. What the cohort split lets you read: new-cohort **conversion rate** against old at the same traffic mix (the price elasticity of acquisition), and new-cohort **retention curve** against old (whether the higher price selected for better-fit customers — Paddle's finding that round-price cohorts retain ~10% better is exactly this kind of read, P-090). Common practice is to grandfather for **6–12 months** and test new pricing on new customers first *(practitioner recommendation via secondary source, not measured; `[unsourced — would need a study of grandfathering windows against churn]`)*.
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: https://www.paddle.com/blog/end-prices-in-9s · practitioner guidance via secondary citation, accessed 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Announce the new price with a date, keep existing customers on the old price for a stated window (6–12 months is the practitioner default), and change nothing else in the same fortnight. Then compare the two cohorts on conversion *and* on the day-30/day-90 retention curve, not on conversion alone — the whole value of the exercise is seeing whether the higher price bought better customers. Write down what the comparison cannot tell you before you run it, so the confounds do not get discovered after the decision.
- **example-copy**: "Solo is moving to $14 on 1 October. If you're already on Solo, you stay at $9 until at least October next year — we'll give you 60 days' notice before anything changes."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly raising Solo $9 → $14 grandfathers all 40 existing customers for 12 months. The readable signals are new-signup conversion at $14 against the current rate, and the day-90 retention of the $14 cohort against the $9 cohort. With ~2,000 monthly visitors the conversion comparison will take months to say anything, and the deliverable should say so rather than presenting it as an experiment with a read date.

### P-099 — Publish a cohort ladder instead of A/B testing price

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: A public launch ladder — "first 50 at $9, then $19, then $29" — produces a genuine willingness-to-pay reading at each rung (you observe how fast each tier fills) while showing every visitor at any given moment the same public price. That removes the fairness objection that makes live price A/B tests hazardous, and it gives early buyers a real reason to buy now.
- **works-when**: pre-launch or low traffic, where a real A/B test lacks the sessions to resolve anything; and where you can hold yourself to the schedule.
- **fails-when**: you do not actually raise the price when you said you would. Then the signal is noise, and the trust cost is permanent. It also fails where the audience is small enough that "first 50" is your entire addressable list for the quarter — you will have sold your whole market at the lowest rung.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for cohort ladders. The negative evidence for the alternative is strong: Amazon's 2000 random price test on DVDs ended in a public apology, with **6,896 customers refunded an average of $3.10 each**, and Bezos stating "we've never tested and we never will test prices based on customer demographics", calling the random test "a mistake because it created uncertainty for customers" (Amazon press release, **2000-09-27**). Amazon's remedy is the policy worth copying pre-emptively: customers who purchase during a price test automatically receive the lowest test price. A 2005 Annenberg Public Policy Center study found nearly two-thirds of adult internet users incorrectly believed price discrimination was illegal and **87% strongly objected to personalised pricing** *(secondary citation)*.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the practice is indie-launch convention; the evidence is about what the alternative costs)*
- **sources**: https://press.aboutamazon.com/2000/9/amazon-com-issues-statement-regarding-random-price-testing · https://www.convert.com/blog/shopify-ab-testing/is-ab-testing-pricing-legal-ethical/
- **how-to**: Announce the ladder publicly with counts, not dates, so it is checkable ("47 of 50 left"). Raise the price on schedule regardless of how it feels. Record how long each rung took to fill and the traffic mix during it — the fill rate *is* the demand curve. If you ever vary price for any other reason, honour the lowest shown price for everyone who bought during the variation. Alternatives when even this is too much: sequential A-B-A testing over windows long enough to accumulate purchases (a quasi-experiment, and say so), or pre-orders and deposits, which need no traffic at all.
- **example-copy**: "Launch pricing: the first 50 Solo accounts are $9/month for life. After that Solo is $14. 47 left."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly at 2,000 monthly visitors and ~40 customers **cannot** run a price A/B test — detecting a plausible conversion difference would take longer than a year. The runnable alternative is the ladder: publish "$9 for the next 60 accounts, then $14", and read the fill rate at each rung. If rung two fills at a similar pace, the $9 was leaving money on the table, which is the finding P-082 predicts.

### P-100 — Vary price by market, never by person

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Territory-differentiated pricing offered on a non-discriminatory basis is legally durable and publicly defensible — a customer who reads about it on a forum accepts it. Pricing inferred from an individual's personal data is under active regulatory attention and is overwhelmingly rejected by consumers, and the reputational constraint binds long before the legal one does.
- **works-when**: PPP and geo pricing, launch cohorts, publicly findable coupons, student and non-profit rates — any reason a customer would accept if they read about it.
- **fails-when**: device-based, browsing-history-based, or demographic pricing. Also within a single jurisdiction (e.g. US state-level pricing), where the market-pricing justification is thin and the reputational exposure is identical to personalised pricing.
- **observed-effect**: **EU Regulation 2018/302 Art. 4(1)** prohibits applying "different general conditions of access to goods or services, for reasons related to a customer's nationality, place of residence or place of establishment", while **Art. 4(2)** preserves conditions differing between Member States "offered to customers on a specific territory... on a non-discriminatory basis"; exemptions cover copyright-protected electronic content, audiovisual services, retail financial services and transport. Automatic country-based redirects are forbidden — users must opt in. On the US side, the **Robinson-Patman Act** is narrower than founders assume: a charge requires price discrimination on at least two consummated sales to two different purchasers, sales must cross state lines, and enforcement is limited; for a solo founder selling a consumer subscription it is essentially not the binding constraint. **FTC surveillance-pricing study**, 6(b) orders to eight intermediaries, preliminary findings published **2025-01-17**: "precise location or browser history can be frequently used to target individual consumers with different prices."
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32018R0302 · https://www.convert.com/blog/shopify-ab-testing/is-ab-testing-pricing-legal-ethical/ · https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-surveillance-pricing-study-indicates-wide-range-personal-data-used-set-individualized-consumer
- **how-to**: Write down the reason for every price variation you run and ask whether you would be comfortable seeing it quoted in a news article. Territory, launch cohort and public coupon pass; inferred income, device type and browsing history do not. Never auto-redirect EU visitors by country — offer the local option and let them opt in. And never test on people who cannot leave: existing subscribers mid-term get grandfathered (P-098), not experimented on. **This is not legal advice; the citations above are to primary instruments, and a lawyer should see any scheme that varies price across EU member states.**
- **example-copy**: "Prices shown in your local currency. Regional pricing is available in 40+ countries — see the list."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's defensible variations are the launch ladder (P-099) and a currency display. A "returning visitor sees $19, first-time visitor sees $14" scheme is refused outright, and the refusal is stated in the deliverable so the omission does not read as an oversight.

### P-101 — Localize currency early, localize price late

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: Showing local currency and accepting local payment methods is a *conversion* fix with near-zero operational cost. True purchasing-power-parity pricing is a *pricing decision* that adds support surface, tax questions, refund edge cases and arbitrage exposure, and its return scales with international volume you may not have yet. Collapsing the two into "we should do PPP" spends a month of implementation on the wrong half.
- **works-when**: the cosmetic layer always pays for any product selling internationally. The full PPP layer pays once non-US traffic is material, and pays early for inherently global categories — developer tools, creator tools, education.
- **fails-when**: you implement full PPP at 30 customers and spend the next month on VPN detection instead of the product. It also fails when treated as a discount mechanism only: **the same data says several markets will pay more than the US**, and founders who read PPP as charity leave that money behind.
- **observed-effect**: Paddle (2021-01-13) documents regional willingness-to-pay differentials: **UK, Northern Europe and Western Europe 20–30% *higher* than the US**; **Southeast Asia for some on-premise solutions 60–70% lower**; and even intra-US variation ("San Francisco/NYC customers willing to pay more than Chicago customers"). Paddle's stage model: early stage should "cosmetically localize your prices in a few major regions" using automated currency conversion; growth stage runs regional price-sensitivity studies and adjusts packaging; late stage goes full localization. **`[unsourced — current per-country WTP indices; Paddle's figures are from 2021 and are the best located]`** The widely cited indie PPP result — "sales jump in developing countries by 800%, with a 15% lift in overall revenue" — comes from a **PPP tooling vendor's own blog, returned 403 on direct fetch, and is captured from a search snippet only**. Treat as unverified anecdote with a conflict of interest.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(for the stage model and the direction of the differentials; `anecdote` and conflicted for any reported PPP revenue result)*
- **sources**: https://www.paddle.com/blog/saas-localized-pricing · https://developer.paddle.com/build/products/offer-localized-pricing/ · https://docs.paritydeals.com/getstarted/introduction-to-paritydeals
- **how-to**: Turn on local currency display and local payment methods on day one via Stripe or Paddle — that is a conversion fix, not a pricing decision. Defer PPP tiers until non-US traffic is material and your interviews show non-US prospects naming numbers 50%+ below your band. When you do implement: **cap the discount** (a 40% discount is worth cheating for far less often than an 80% one), **require the billing-address country to match the detected country** (cheap, effective, needs no vendor), **make PPP opt-in via a visible banner** rather than silent (which converts arbitrage from automatic into a deliberate lie the buyer must tell), **lock the price to the first-purchase country** so nobody re-arbitrages at renewal, and accept some leakage — a leaked discount is a customer you would not otherwise have had, unless it is coming from your core market at scale.
- **example-copy**: "Prices adjusted for your region. Not where you live? Switch country."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly at ~40 customers should ship currency display and nothing else. The arithmetic against PPP is `(non-US share of traffic) × (incremental conversion from the discount) × (discounted price)`: at 15% non-US traffic and 40 customers, that is a handful of dollars a month against a week of implementation and a permanent support surface. Revisit above roughly $5–10k MRR. `[the $5–10k MRR threshold is reasoning, not a cited figure — no study of PPP implementation cost against incremental revenue for small SaaS was located]`

### P-102 — Show tax-inclusive prices to EU consumers, and model the margin swing

- **layer**: `price-points`
- **mechanism**: EU law defines the consumer selling price as the final price including all taxes, so an exclusive display is non-compliant for B2C. The consequence founders miss is downstream: because VAT rates differ by member state, one inclusive price means your **net revenue varies by where the customer lives**, and the headline price stops being the number you keep.
- **works-when**: selling any B2C subscription into the EU.
- **fails-when**: B2B, where buyers reclaim VAT and "$X + VAT" is conventional and expected; and the US, where tax-exclusive display is the norm and sales tax is added at checkout. `[unsourced — a citation confirming there is no US federal requirement for tax-inclusive consumer price display; would need an FTC or state AG source]`
- **observed-effect**: **Directive 98/6/EC Art. 2(a)** defines "selling price" as "the final price for a unit of the product, or a given quantity of the product, including VAT and all other taxes", and Art. 3(1) requires the selling price and unit price be indicated. Stripe's mechanics: "Inclusive tax is part of the price. For example, if the price is €600 and you set an inclusive tax rate of 10%, the invoice total will still be €600. Exclusive tax is applied on top of the price" — a single `inclusive` boolean in the API. The arithmetic consequence: a **€600 inclusive price nets €545.45 at 10% VAT and €480 at 25% VAT — a 12% swing in what you keep**, purely from customer location.
- **evidence**: `strong` *(the legal requirement is primary-sourced and the arithmetic is definitional; note this is a compliance and margin pattern, not a conversion claim)*
- **sources**: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A31998L0006 · https://support.stripe.com/questions/inclusive-and-exclusive-tax-options-in-the-tax-rate-api-and-stripe-tax · https://docs.stripe.com/tax/tax-rates
- **how-to**: Pick inclusive or exclusive and be **consistent across the pricing page, the checkout and the invoice** — a number that changes between the pricing page and the payment step is the largest tax-attributable source of checkout abandonment. For EU consumers, either show inclusive prices to EU visitors or show a single global inclusive price and absorb the jurisdictional variance in your margin; the second is simpler and is what most small subscription businesses do, and its cost is the swing above. Model blended net revenue across your **actual** EU country mix rather than assuming the headline. If multi-jurisdiction VAT registration is a burden, use a merchant-of-record (Paddle, Lemon Squeezy) and treat the higher processing rate as the price of the compliance — for a solo founder selling internationally that is usually the right trade.
- **example-copy**: "€14/month, VAT included."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly at $14/month showing one global inclusive price: a German customer at 19% VAT nets `$14 ÷ 1.19 = $11.76`; a Hungarian customer at 27% nets `$14 ÷ 1.27 = $11.02`; a US customer nets the full $14 less processing. Blended margin therefore depends on country mix, and the P-088 floor must be computed against the **worst** jurisdiction in the mix, not the headline.

## Interactions

**P-081 (level first) versus P-089/P-090/P-091 (endings).** These are not in tension so much as strictly ordered, and the ordering is the pattern. The failure mode is not disagreement between them — it is spending the pricing meeting on P-089 because it is the only decision that feels tractable. If a run produces a confident ending recommendation and a bracketed level, the run went wrong.

**P-086 (breakeven) versus P-087 (the price-lever multiple) versus Layer 11 (expansion and retention).** P-086 is a single-period contribution identity and P-087 is a single-period ratio. Both are correct and both are blind to the thing that determines a subscription's outcome: the rolling cohort. A 10% increase that survives its breakeven on units may still lose money if the surviving cohort renews worse, and the breakeven formula cannot see month 18. Always run P-086, then hand the churn question to Layer 11 explicitly rather than letting the clean arithmetic imply the question is closed.

**P-082 (take the upper third) versus P-084 (neighbourhood ceiling) versus P-085 (price-quality).** Two of these push up and one caps. The separator is which constraint is real: P-085's floor is about *inference* (too cheap reads as untrustworthy) and P-084's ceiling is about *category* (too expensive moves you into a comparison you lose). When they collide — a band whose upper third crosses the neighbourhood ceiling — the ceiling wins, unless you change the shape of the offer so the category changes with the price. Adding a human being is the standard way to cross the seam legitimately.

**P-093 (annual discount) versus P-092 (clean derivative) versus Layer 05 P-124 (annual default).** The discount you compute from churn is rarely a number that lands cleanly on twelve months, and the market's own convention resolves the conflict in favour of *time* framing ("two months free" = 16.67%). If your break-even says 23% and the clean framings available are 16.67% and 25%, take 25% only if you are deliberately buying cash and say so; otherwise take 16.67% and price the difference as retained margin. Then note that P-124's annual default and P-093's discount compound: defaulting to annual on a discount above break-even multiplies the loss across every new customer.

**P-094 (all-in number) versus P-097 (top-up) and Layer 10 (checkout).** A metered product with a top-up has an inherent partitioning problem: the second charge is real and will arrive. The resolution is not to hide it and not to bury it — it is to publish the single top-up rate on the pricing page (Layer 05, P-136 publishes a price rather than "contact us") so the buyer can compute their own worst case before they enter checkout. The StubHub effect is about *sequence*; a rate disclosed before purchase is not a dripped fee.

**P-095 (zero-price) versus P-096 (volume curve) and Layer 03 (tier architecture).** A free tier is the steepest possible point on the volume curve — infinite discount — which is exactly why the fence matters more there than anywhere else. If the free tier meters the same unit as the paid tiers with no other difference, the curve from free to top is undefined and the fence is doing all the work alone. Fence the free tier on breadth or on capability, not only on quantity.

**P-099 (cohort ladder) versus P-098 (grandfathering) versus Layer 12 (experimentation).** All three are attempts to learn about price without a split test, and they are compatible: the ladder generates the price change, grandfathering protects the people already paying, and Layer 12 decides whether the resulting read is worth anything. The one incompatibility is with Layer 12's isolate-one-variable discipline — every one of these is a quasi-experiment, and the honest posture is to report them as such rather than dressing a confounded before-and-after as a result.

**P-100 (market not person) versus P-101 (PPP) versus P-099 (cohort ladder).** These three describe the *only* three legitimate axes on which a price may vary: territory, time, and a publicly stated cohort. Anything else — device, history, inferred income, returning-visitor status — is refused. Where PPP and the geo-blocking regulation appear to conflict, the resolution in the instrument itself is that differentiated conditions *by territory on a non-discriminatory basis* are preserved, while refusing an EU customer another member state's price *because of their nationality* is not.

## Where this layer is thin

- **The canonical charm-pricing citation cannot be read.** Anderson & Simester (2003) is a scanned PDF with no text layer, so the most-quoted number in consumer pricing rests on secondary reporting. Verifying it needs a text-layer copy or library access to *Quantitative Marketing and Economics* 1(1). Meanwhile the two modern preregistered replications are null, and nobody has run a well-powered field replication in a subscription context — which is precisely the context this skill is used in.
- **`.95` versus `.99` versus `.00` on a subscription has never been tested publicly.** Everything located compares 9-endings to round numbers.
- **"N months free" versus "N% off" has never been tested.** It is near-universal practice and completely unmeasured.
- **There is no empirical distribution of value-capture ratios.** The 10–50% range and the 10x heuristic are consultancy folklore. A defensible version would need transacted prices paired with measured customer ROI across a cohort.
- **There is no time series for published annual-plan discounts.** CompareEdge gives one cross-section (2026-07-02, updated 2026-08-10); the "discounting is falling" claim rests on Vendr's *negotiated* discount trend, which is a different quantity measured on a different population.
- **The volume-discount and top-up findings come from seven products in one adjacent category on one day.** The 20–40% band and the peg-to-entry rule are the cleanest operational guidance in the layer and they are a census of practice, not a test of effect. Nobody has published what happens to revenue when a curve is narrowed.
- **The decision-neighbourhood ceiling is one neighbourhood at one date.** $9.95–$36 is well corroborated across four products *within* the personal-insight category. Whether every category has such a ceiling, and how to find yours without doing the teardown, is not established.
- **Subscription and retention economics are essentially absent from the pricing canon.** Neither Simon nor Nagle models churn, lifetime value, expansion revenue, trial-to-paid conversion, or the fact that a price change applies to a rolling cohort rather than a transaction. The breakeven formula in P-086 is a single-period contribution identity and cannot see month 18. **This is the single largest gap for anyone selling software.**
- **Nagle is explicit that economic value "does not indicate the appropriate price to charge", and neither the canon nor its derivative literature offers a capture percentage.** You are handed a ceiling and left to pick. Every "capture X%" number in circulation is an invention.
- **Current per-country willingness-to-pay indices do not exist publicly.** Paddle's 20–30%-higher / 60–70%-lower figures are from 2021 and nothing more recent and non-vendor was located. Any PPP factor you set is a guess dressed as arithmetic.
- **There is no independent measurement of PPP's revenue impact at any scale**, and no study of the implementation cost against incremental revenue that would establish the threshold at which it becomes net-positive.
- **A product at ~2,000 monthly visitors and ~40 customers cannot learn any of the behavioural patterns here from its own traffic.** The honest posture is to run the arithmetic patterns (P-086, P-088, P-092, P-093, P-096, P-097) which need no traffic at all and which at this scale are worth an order of magnitude more than any ending or framing decision — and to treat everything else as a prior with an instrumentation plan attached.
