# Audience Pack — Investors

## What they're actually buying

**A business, not a product.** They will never use the thing. What they're underwriting is a claim that this becomes big, that it keeps its customers, and that someone else can't trivially do it too.

So the product is one block in a larger structure. An investor listening to a product description is waiting for four questions to get answered, and mentally scoring you on how long you take to get there:

1. How big can this get, and who exactly buys first?
2. Why do they stay? (retention is the whole business)
3. Why can't someone else do this? (the Core)
4. How do you make money, and what have you actually proven?

The failure that kills most descriptions is spending 80% of the airtime on question zero — what the product does — and never reaching the four that matter.

**Stage governs the entire posture.** Pre-launch, you have no traction and the investor knows it; claiming any is the fastest way to lose the room. The honest version — *here's the riskiest assumption in this business and here's how we're testing it* — is both the Lean Startup posture and, in practice, the more impressive one. It says the founder knows what could kill them.

---

## Section skeleton

**1. Market opportunity + beachhead** — the wide market in one line, then immediately the narrow group who buys first and why they feel it most. A beachhead that's specific enough to sound small is a good sign, not a weak one. Every figure here is sourced or bracketed.

**2. The problem, and why now** — what people do today instead, why that's broken, and what changed recently that makes this possible or urgent now. "Why now" is the question founders skip and investors always ask.

**3. The product** — high-level, described as the customer experiences it (Aulet Step 7). Two or three sentences. Enough that they can picture someone using it. This is not the section to spend your budget on.

**4. Why they stay** — the quantified value proposition pointed at retention. What the product does for the user that keeps them coming back, and the metric that would prove it. If unmeasured, bracket the number and name the measurement.

**5. The Core** — what makes this defensible (Aulet Step 10). The single hardest section to write honestly and the one investors weigh most. See the standards below.

**6. Business model** — how money is made, at what price, and what the acquisition path looks like. If pricing isn't set, say so and give the hypothesis.

**7. Traction and what's next** — what's actually true today, stated at the right stage, followed by the specific next thing being proven and the metric that settles it.

**8. Vision** — where this goes if it works. One paragraph, ambitious, clearly labelled as the long game so it isn't mistaken for a claim about today.

---

## Register

- Numerate and specific. Vagueness reads as not knowing.
- Concedes openly. "We haven't proven this yet — here's the test" is a strength; hedging everything is not.
- Compressed. Investors read the first two lines of each section and decide whether to read the rest.
- No adjectives doing the work of evidence. "Massive", "revolutionary", "game-changing" are all noise.

---

## The Core — standards

Most stated moats are not moats. Reject the draft's Core and rewrite it if it's any of these:

- **"Network effects"** where the product has no network. A single-player app where user data makes *that user's* experience better is a **switching cost**, not a network effect. Call it what it is — switching costs are a legitimate moat, just a different one, and using the right term signals you know the difference.
- **"Our team"** alone. Sometimes true, rarely sufficient, and it isn't a product moat.
- **"First mover"**. Almost never a moat by itself.
- **"Better UX"**. Copyable in a quarter.

Legitimate Cores, roughly in order of how often they're real for a small product: accumulated user-specific data that gets more valuable over time (switching cost); a proprietary dataset nobody else can assemble; a distribution channel that's owned rather than rented; genuine network effects where users bring value to other users; regulatory or licensing position; unit economics nobody else can match.

If the honest answer is **"we don't have one yet, and here's how we'd build one"** — write that. It's an acceptable answer at pre-seed and an unacceptable bluff at any stage.

---

## Disqualifiers — instant loss

- **Any unsourced number.** A TAM with no report behind it, a retention figure from a product that hasn't launched, a "users report X%" from users who don't exist. This is the single rule the skill exists to enforce.
- Fabricated traction, pilots, waitlists, LOIs, or press.
- A feature list with no business model attached.
- A Core that fails the standards above.
- Claimed validated learning that never happened — "our customer discovery found…" when there were no interviews.
- Vanity metrics presented as evidence: downloads, signups, impressions, without retention behind them.

---

## Failure modes to watch for

**Product tour instead of a pitch.** Four paragraphs on features, one line on the business. Fix: hard-cap the product section at three sentences.

**Optimism laundered as data.** The number appears without a source, gets repeated, and becomes load-bearing. This is exactly how the pasted examples produced "40% increase in social satisfaction" for a product with no users. Every number gets challenged in Phase 5.

**Stage inflation.** Describing an idea as if it's a beta, or a beta as if it has product/market fit. Investors talk to each other and check.

**A TAM that swallows the pitch.** "$13B wellness market" is a number that means nothing without the path from your beachhead to a slice of it. Bottom-up beats top-down: number of people in the beachhead × what they'd pay.

## Worked example

*Illustrative only — never a template. The fictional product below must never appear in a deliverable.*

**Product:** *Sundial* — sleep planning for night-shift workers. Stage: pre-launch, no users.

**Fabricated (wrong — this is the failure mode):**
> We're targeting the $85B sleep-health market. Early users report 34% better sleep quality and we're seeing strong retention signals, with a projected LTV:CAC of 4:1.

Every one of those numbers is invented for a product with no users. Nobody measured 34%. There are no early users to report it.

**Honest (right):**

> **Market + beachhead.** The wide market is consumer sleep health — [market size — needs a sourced figure; cite the report, don't estimate]. We don't start there. We start with rotating-shift nurses in [region]: roughly [headcount — pull from national nursing workforce data], a group whose schedules change every week and who already pay out of pocket for sleep aids and trackers built for people who work days.
>
> **Problem + why now.** Shift workers currently improvise — sleep advice, melatonin, generic trackers that assume a fixed bedtime. Nothing takes the roster as an input. What changed: hospital scheduling systems now expose rosters through APIs, so the shift data can come in automatically instead of being typed by an exhausted person at 6am.
>
> **Product.** You connect your roster once. Sundial produces the sleep windows that fit the shifts you're actually working, plans the return to daylight around rest days, and shows which shift patterns cost you most.
>
> **Why they stay.** The value compounds: the longer you log, the better it knows which patterns wreck you specifically. [retention target — unmeasured; the metric that settles it is 30-day retention of users who log 3+ sleeps in week one.]
>
> **The Core.** Accumulated per-user sleep-and-roster history, which raises switching cost — a competitor starts from zero on a user we've known for six months. This is a switching-cost moat, not a network effect; users don't make the product better for each other. A stronger second moat, if we get it: direct distribution through hospital scheduling vendors, which would be owned rather than rented.
>
> **Model.** Consumer subscription, hypothesis [price — not set], with employer-paid as a later channel once retention is proven. No revenue today.
>
> **Traction and what's next.** Pre-launch. No users, no revenue — nothing is validated yet. The riskiest assumption is that shift workers will log sleep consistently enough for the plan to improve; if they don't, the moat never forms and the product is a static calculator. We're testing it with [n] beta users over [duration], and the number that settles it is week-one logging rate.
>
> **Vision.** If the logging habit holds, the dataset becomes the first real picture of how rotating schedules damage sleep at scale — which is a research asset and a route into employer and health-system contracts.

Note what makes the honest version stronger: it names its own kill condition. The fabricated version can't, because it's pretending the risk was already retired.
