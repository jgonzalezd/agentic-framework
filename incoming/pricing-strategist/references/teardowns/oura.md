## Ladder arithmetic

**There is no ladder — and the annual "discount" is arithmetically negligible.**

$5.99 × 12 = **$71.88**. Against $69.99 annual, the saving is **$1.89 — 2.63%.**

**Oura is the anomaly of the consumer-subscription cohort: it does not push annual.** No "Best value" badge, no strikethrough, no savings percentage, no monthly-to-annual ratio worth quoting. And the hero CTA quotes the **monthly** price: **"Take control of your health for just $5.99/month"** — the opposite of the five peers who divide the annual number down to make it look small.

**The reason is structural, and it is the most important single observation in the consumer lane.** Oura already captured the large commitment at the ring purchase (**$349+ hardware**), so the membership's job is to be **small enough to feel like nothing.** Pushing annual would re-raise a price objection Oura has already cleared. **Push annual only when the subscription is the primary purchase decision.**

**Hardware sunk-cost arithmetic.** At $349+ for the ring against $5.99/month, the membership is **1.7% of the hardware price per month**, and it would take **58 months** of membership fees to equal the ring. A user holding a $349 ring will not cancel a $5.99 membership, **because cancelling strands the asset.** The retention mechanism is not the price — it is the hardware.

**HSA/FSA arithmetic — the strongest form of price reduction is not a discount.** "HSA/FSA eligible membership coverage" does not change the number; it changes the **category**. A $69.99 wellness app is discretionary; a $69.99 HSA-eligible health expense is not, and is often paid pre-tax. **Recategorisation beats discounting** because it reduces the felt cost without reducing revenue.

## What transfers, and what doesn't

**Hardware-driven, and this is the caveat that makes the rest legible.** Oura's whole pricing posture depends on having already extracted a $349+ commitment. A pure software product with a $5.99 subscription and no prior purchase is in the opposite position: **the subscription *is* the decision**, and declining to push annual would simply forgo the retention and cash-flow benefit for nothing.

**The transferable version is the question, not the answer.** Oura is the counter-example that proves the cohort's rule, and the rule is: **push annual only when the subscription is the primary purchase decision.** Two tests follow:
- **Has the buyer already made a large commitment to you?** If yes, the subscription's job is to be forgettable.
- **Does the buyer's need have an end?** A product that sells a *practice* (Headspace, Strava, Calm) should push annual hard. A product that sells a *resolution* — something the buyer will be done with — is closer to Oura, and locking a resolved user into eleven more months produces the reviews that define a category.

**Genuinely universal:**
1. **Lead the testimonial wall with the highest-stakes outcome, in the customer's flat voice.** One specific, unadorned, consequence-describing quote outperforms twenty enthusiastic ones. Get it in their words and resist editing.
2. **Reclassify the spend if you honestly can.** HSA/FSA, professional development, reimbursable business expense — **the strongest form of price reduction is not a discount but a recategorisation.** It costs you nothing and moves the purchase out of "discretionary".
3. **Quantify your features into proof.** "Over 50 health metrics" reads as evidence of work done.
4. **If the decision is binary, build a binary page.** One plan, one price, no badge. Tier architecture exists to route buyers, and a page with nothing to route should not pretend otherwise.
