# Anti-patterns and the folklore register

**Loaded at:** Phase 6 (page and paywall spec). Read it before you draft, not after — several of the tactics below are the ones a pricing page most wants to reach for.

Two halves. The first is **what backfires**: tactics that damage more than they earn, each with the mechanism, the evidence as it actually exists, and honest cost accounting. The second is **the folklore register**: the consolidated list of claims this library refuses to cite, merged from every research lane, with what each one actually traces to.

The organising idea behind both halves is a single asymmetry. **A pricing-page A/B test measures the conversion and nothing else.** It cannot see the refund in week three, the cancellation in month four, the review that was not written, or the friend who was not told. Direct response — where most of these tactics were formalised — was built for one-shot mail order, where there *was* no month eleven. A subscription business inherits the entire downstream cost of every persuasion device it runs, and the instrument that would have warned it does not exist on the page.

---

## Anti-patterns

### Urgency and scarcity spend trust to buy a click

**Mechanism.** Scarcity cues are increasingly read as manipulation attempts rather than as information. The buyer does not merely fail to be persuaded — they update their model of the seller. For a subscription, where lifetime value is downstream of trust, that revision is charged to a period the test cannot see.

**The evidence, precisely.** Tuncer et al. (2023), **n=202 participants**, three site versions — countdown timer, stock progress bar, control — measured with NASA-TLX, a website-trust benevolence scale, and UEQ-short. Findings: countdown timers produced significantly higher frustration than no scarcity cues; limited-time cues degraded pragmatic quality, functioning as visual clutter; stock progress bars did not raise frustration but reduced hedonic appeal. And the load-bearing result: **both cue types negatively affected perceived vendor benevolence, with users reading them as opportunistic ploys.**

**The sharpest edge of that finding is the one usually dropped in retelling: the benevolence damage occurred for *honest* scarcity cues too.** It is not a finding about deception. It is a finding about the device.

Supporting work: Kristofferson et al. (2017) on timers triggering annoyance and aggression; Mathur et al. (2019) classifying scarcity cues as dark patterns; Luguri & Strahilevitz (2021) on increased mistrust and impulsive buying — all reached through a single academic review `[secondary]`.

**The cost accounting, honestly.** Tuncer et al. measured **perceptions, not purchases**. The trust cost is well demonstrated; the size of the revenue consequence is not. Meanwhile the *conversion* side of the trade is poorly sourced in the opposite direction: an oft-repeated claim that real-deadline checkout timers lift completion "up to 9.3%" is `[unsourced — the publisher presents this and every adjacent figure without attribution]`, and the widely circulated "23–41% lower repeat-purchase rates among buyers who detect fake urgency" is likewise `[unsourced — verifying would require the merchant cohort data behind it]`. **So the honest position is: the trust cost is measured and the conversion gain is not.** That is the reverse of how this tactic is usually sold.

**Where a deadline is legitimate.** When the constraint exists independently of the marketing — a real cohort start, a real price change with advance notice, a real capacity ceiling. If you announce a price increase, honour it; announcing and not raising is the same lie as a resetting timer, discovered one month later.

**The existence proof on the other side.** 37signals states outright that "Urgency is overrated, ASAP is poison" and has built a durable software business with no sales team and essentially no paid advertising for two decades. That is proof the urgency layer is *optional*, not proof it does not work — 37signals had twenty-five years of accumulated audience doing the job urgency would otherwise do. A founder without that audience is choosing between a slow trust-compounding strategy and a fast trust-spending one, and should choose consciously rather than by aesthetic reflex.

**What to do instead:** audit the page for any element implying a constraint you cannot document, and remove each one. If you keep a timer, make it survive a refresh and reflect a real event.

### Dark patterns now carry regulatory, not just reputational, risk

**Mechanism.** The negative option — a one-off purchase that quietly becomes a subscription — converts extremely well precisely because it does not ask. That is also exactly what makes it actionable.

**The evidence: FTC v. JustAnswer LLC and CEO Andrew Kurtzig, filed 2026-01-13**, Northern District of California, **two counts under ROSCA**: failure to "clearly and conspicuously disclose the subscription fee," and failure to "obtain express informed consent." The complaint alleges the conduct affected "hundreds of thousands of consumers between January 2022 and the present."

The complaint quotes JustAnswer's own on-screen copy verbatim, which makes it a stronger evidentiary source than any marketing page — it is sworn and dated. `[secondary — justanswer.com returned HTTP 403 on every path on 2026-08-12; the complaint text was reached through ppc.land, regulatoryoversight.com and allaboutadvertisinglaw.com, all fetched 2026-08-12.]`

The chatbot, named "Pearl," during signup:

> "OK, got it. Here's a secure form to join JustAnswer for $5 (fully-refundable). While you're filling this out, I'll tell the Veterinarian about your situation and then connect you two."

The payment-form disclosure, sitting **between the card fields and the button**:

> "By clicking 'Confirm now' I agree to the Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, to be charged the one-time join fee, and a $39 monthly membership fee today and each month until I cancel."

The complaint's own characterisation of the legibility problem:

> "The material terms were still difficult to see in comparison with the much larger text on the payment form"

and of the consent checkbox, the

> "omission of any language regarding the price consumers would pay for the subscription".

The advertised offer, as characterised in the complaint, was expert help for **"as little as $1 or $5"**; the actual recurring charge was **"$28–$125" per month** by category (Mechanics "$47/month"; Legal "$79/month" as of Oct 2024; all categories "$65/month" as of Nov 2025).

**The mechanism stack, named so you can recognise it in your own draft:** intent interception (buy the moment of need at auction rather than build a brand) · disclosure-as-sunk-cost (take the story before naming the price) · the "someone is waiting" device (a live expert is briefed *while you enter your card*) · nominal-fee anchoring (a $1/$5 number occupies the price slot; the real number arrives in small print) · negative-option continuity.

**The cost accounting.** Read this as a market signal rather than only as a scandal. JustAnswer had every incentive and all the data, and concluded the one-off could not be monetised at a price it wanted — so it faked recurring. **The demand is proven; honest monetisation of it is the open problem, and the dishonest route now has a docket number.** Note also the adjacent signal: at least one company in Hormozi's portfolio drew an FTC investigation amid high sales volume and customer complaints `[secondary, an opinion piece — treat the specific claim as unverified reporting]`, and dark-pattern research is increasingly cited in regulatory contexts.

### Fabricating social proof on a pre-launch product

**Mechanism.** Social proof works because the reader treats it as evidence about the world rather than as a claim by the seller. A fabricated testimonial, an invented user count or a logo wall of companies who are not customers converts the strongest element on the page into the most fragile one: it is verifiable by anyone who tries, and a single verification failure retroactively discredits every other claim, including the true ones.

**The evidence, stated honestly: no quantified conversion study of fabricated social proof was located in any lane, and none should be expected — nobody runs that experiment and publishes it.** What the research does supply is three converging facts.

First, the **enforcement environment**. The JustAnswer complaint above is a live federal action over misrepresenting what the buyer was agreeing to, and the same regulatory posture covers fabricated endorsements. Second, **what real pages actually do**: across a census of **33 live pricing pages fetched 2026-08-12**, proof is specific, attributed and checkable — "Trusted by over 40 million people and 50,000 organizations" (Grammarly), "[337K+] ★★★★★ reviews" (Todoist), "Trusted by more than 40,000 companies" (Linear). ⚠ **The Todoist figure is bracketed because the identical token "337K+" appears in the same census attached to an unrelated metric at an unrelated company (16Personalities, "Tests taken today", `patterns/09-trust-and-proof.md`). One of the two is almost certainly a transcription error and neither can be resolved without re-fetching, so the number is not quotable; the *form* of the claim is what this passage is illustrating.** Third, **published paywall guidance from operator case studies** is explicitly to "use real store reviews rather than written testimonials, since that is what the published cases used."

**The cost accounting.** The honest trade is not "fake proof versus real proof" — a pre-launch product has no real proof, so the trade is **fake proof versus a different device**. The research supplies two that do not require customers you do not have. The first is the **damaging admission**: state a real limitation openly, before the price, which raises the credibility of every other claim because a source that volunteers costs is read as not selling, and which self-selects buyers who have already accepted the limitation. Example register: *"This won't help if what you need is [X]. It's built for people who [Y]."* No controlled test of the technique itself was found; treat it as plausible-but-unmeasured, and note the adjacent "94% of consumers are likely to be loyal to a brand offering complete transparency" figure is `[unsourced at primary level]` and should not be quoted. The second is **founder-visible specificity**: name what exists, in numbers you can defend, however small.

The bracket to carry into a COLD deliverable: `[social proof — no customers yet; the page ships with a damaging admission in the proof slot until the first three real testimonials exist]`.

### Unpredictable usage units, and the redenomination trap

**Mechanism.** When you meter in an abstract unit you mint yourself — credits, tokens, compute units — you control the exchange rate between that unit and the work the customer wanted. That control is an option you will eventually exercise, and customers know it. Worse, a unit whose consumption the buyer cannot predict converts every use of the product into a small act of budget anxiety, which taxes exactly the behaviour that produces retention.

**The evidence: Cursor's public failure and apology.** Before June 2025, Cursor sold **500 requests/month** — a countable unit, easy to reason about, and economically wrong, because as the company later put it, *"the hardest requests cost an order of magnitude more than simple ones."* In the week of 2025-06-16, Pro moved from request-based to compute limits: *"all users will get at least $20 of model inference at API prices per month."* Users reported all-day frontier-model capacity shrinking to about an hour, and reported overages such as "$350 in a week" `[secondary]`. The change also **removed the request counter from the UI**.

On **2025-07-04** Cursor published an apology and refunds: *"We will refund any unexpected charges you may have incurred for usage over the past 3 weeks"*, window *"between June 16 and July 4"*, with the statement *"Our recent pricing changes for individual plans were not communicated clearly, and we take full responsibility."*

Two further details matter. They let existing subscribers keep the old model — *"Existing subscribers could retain the previous 500-request structure if preferred"* — which is the correct handling of a unit migration and almost nobody does it. And as of **2026-08**, the public unit has retreated to **multipliers** ("3x Pro limits on Agent", "20x Pro limits on Agent") with the dollar meter demoted to opt-in overage. **Cursor moved the honest unit from the front of the page to the back**, because a multiplier cannot be exceeded into a charge.

The structural claim behind this: a currency you mint and can silently devalue will eventually be devalued. Airlines are said to devalue frequent-flyer miles roughly **15% annually** `[unsourced — no publisher, sample or date is recorded for this figure anywhere in this library; use the analogy, not the number]`; one published critique alleges Cursor raised effective per-unit rates **20×+ through credit redenomination in early 2026** `[secondary, and the specific multiple is contested — the publisher, Software Pricing Partners, sells the consulting that competes with the "just ship credits" default]`. Adjacent failures in the same corpus: Salesforce Agentforce abandoned $2/conversation for flexible credits after backlash; Perplexity's "unlimited Pro Search" cap was **cut from 600 to 200 per week in early 2026** — an unlimited claim that had a number behind it all along, and the number moved.

**The cost accounting, and the honest counterweight.** Credits persist for a reason: they sit between seat-based access and outcome pricing, offering more transparency than legacy licenses and more feasibility than outcomes, because most products cannot define or verify an outcome. Outcome-based pricing sat at **5% adoption (n=240, Apr–May 2025)** and is only *expected* to reach 25% by 2028. Corroboration from a party with the opposite commercial interest: Metronome sells usage-billing infrastructure and therefore profits when companies adopt credits, yet its own 2025-09-22 analysis calls cost-plus credits *"born of necessity rather than preference,"* a **bridge, not a destination** — with two quoted customers: *"We don't love credits, but we didn't have time to define outcomes. This was the fastest way to ship"* and *"Our finance team likes it. Our customers don't know what a credit does."* On the buyer side, **78% of IT leaders reported surprise AI-related charges in the past year** (Zylo, *2026 SaaS Management Index*, n=218 — the publisher this file omitted until 2026-08-13, recorded at P-198 in `patterns/07-risk-reversal.md`).

**So the anti-pattern is not "credits." It is the unpredictable unit and the silent redenomination.** The operating rules that follow: denominate in the noun the customer came to buy; ship a live meter before you need it (a persistent "7 of 20 analyses used — resets March 14", an 80% warning carrying the exact number and date, a top-up offer inside that warning); and put the burn/allowance terms under change control with a notice cycle, a grandfather period, and a dated changelog. **Announced price changes are survivable; discovered ones are the Cursor apology.**

### Promising a refund and delivering an invisible account credit

**Mechanism.** A customer who cancels mid-period believes one of two things is happening: either they keep access until the period ends, or they get money back. Stripe's default does neither in the way the customer imagines. Cancelling with a mid-period date computes a proration, and that proration lands as a **credit on the customer balance** rather than as a refund to the card. Nothing arrives on the statement. The customer sees a cancellation, sees no refund, and concludes they were charged for a service they no longer have — which is the precise fact pattern that produces a chargeback rather than a support ticket, because the customer believes they have already asked and been ignored.

**The evidence, from the platform's own documentation.** All verbatim from `docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/prorations`, accessed **2026-08-12**.

> "Negative prorations aren't automatically refunded and positive prorations aren't immediately billed, although you can do both manually."

The rest of the machinery constrains what a refund policy can even promise. Proration on Stripe is **strictly time-based**, computed as "Unused time on original plan" against "Remaining time on new plan", and "By default, Stripe calculates prorations down to the second" — granularity can be coarsened to day, hour, week or month, but the *basis* stays time. Consumption is not a basis the platform supports: **"Usage-based billing isn't subject to proration."** To prorate by units consumed you must compute the figure yourself and pass `add_invoice_items` with a negative `unit_amount`, or run custom proration scripts. Setting `cancel_at_period_end` generates **no proration at all** and the customer simply keeps access to the end of the paid period. Setting `cancel_at` mid-period **does** trigger proration, and "if you cancel the subscription for a date outside the current period, you can't disable the proration."

**The cost accounting.** The invisible credit is the worst of the available outcomes on every axis. The revenue is deferred rather than kept, because the balance will be consumed by a future invoice or written off. The customer's belief is that they were refused, so the goodwill the credit was meant to buy is not merely unearned but inverted. And the dispute that follows costs the transaction plus the dispute fee plus a mark against processor standing, which at a sub-$30/mo price point exceeds several months of that customer's revenue — the same arithmetic P-192 uses to rule out the anti-guarantee.

**The second failure mode, for products that meter.** The natural fairness basis for a product selling discrete generated units is *units consumed*, and the platform's native basis is *time elapsed*. Those diverge hardest in exactly the abuse case: a buyer subscribes, generates everything they wanted in the first 48 hours, cancels on day three, and is owed roughly 90% back under time proration having consumed 100% of the value. **A consumption fence must therefore be built in application logic and disclosed at checkout, because Stripe will not enforce it and the card networks will not infer it.**

**What to do instead.** Make `cancel_at_period_end` the default self-serve path, say on the page and in the confirmation what the buyer keeps and until when, and issue any refund you decide to grant as an **explicit, separately-granted refund to the original payment method** with a message naming the amount. That construction stays inside platform defaults, avoids the invisible-credit failure mode entirely, and removes the need for custom proration arithmetic in the highest-stakes part of the billing system. The patterns are **P-310** (cancel at period end, refund explicitly) and **P-311** (fence in application logic, disclose at checkout) in `patterns/07-risk-reversal.md`.

### Badging two tiers at once destroys the signal

**Mechanism.** A badge works as a recommendation because it is scarce and singular — it tells the visitor which rung the seller believes fits most people, and lets them stop comparing. Two badges is self-evidently incoherent: two tiers cannot both be most popular. The buyer resolves the contradiction the only way available, by reclassifying the badge from *recommendation* to *decoration*, and once reclassified it does no routing work anywhere on the page.

**The evidence: it happens in the wild, at scale.** In the census of **33 live pricing pages fetched 2026-08-12**, **Grammarly shows "Most popular" on both Pro and Enterprise**, and **Dropbox shows "Best Value" on both Standard and Advanced**.

**Preserve the caveat the research attached:** both were read from a single automated transcription and could reflect a responsive or multi-section page rather than one visible grid. **Worth a manual eyeball before citing as a mistake.**

**The cost accounting.** There is no published effect size for double-badging — and note from the folklore register below that there is no defensible effect size for *single* badging either. So the honest statement is narrow and still sufficient: badging is the cheapest routing device on the page, its entire mechanism is singularity, and shipping two costs you the device while gaining nothing that has ever been measured. There is a second, separate honesty constraint: **the badge is honest if the tier is in fact the most popular and manipulative if it is not**, and no source located addresses verification. `[unsourced — would need a published policy or an audit of badge accuracy across a sample of pricing pages.]`

**One further situational note:** on a two-tier ladder the badge is redundant regardless — the layout already tells the buyer which is which.

### Pushing annual when the commitment was already captured

**Mechanism.** An annual push is a second commitment ask. If the buyer already cleared a large commitment elsewhere in the funnel, the push reopens a price objection that was settled, at a moment when the remaining decision was supposed to feel like nothing.

**The evidence: Oura is the anomaly of its category and it is deliberate.** Observed **2026-08-12** on the live membership page: **$5.99 USD/month against $69.99 USD/annually.** $5.99 × 12 = $71.88, so the annual saving is **$1.89, or 2.6%.** There is no "Best value" badge, no strikethrough, and no savings percentage. The hero CTA quotes the **monthly** price: *"Take control of your health for just $5.99/month."*

The structural reason is that Oura already captured the large commitment at the ring purchase (**$349+ hardware**), so the membership's job is to be small enough to feel like nothing. A user holding a $349 ring will not cancel a $5.99 membership, because cancelling strands the asset. Pushing annual would re-raise a price objection already cleared at hardware checkout.

Every company in the same consumer-subscription set that sells an ongoing *practice* — Headspace, Calm, Strava, Duolingo, Blinkist — pushes annual hard. The one that had captured commitment elsewhere declines to.

**The cost accounting, from the other side of the same trade.** The annual push does work when the subscription *is* the primary purchase decision, and it has a real published effect: demoting the monthly plan behind a "view all plans" link lifted yearly take-up **+15–20%** at Mojo — a vendor-published case study with **no sample size, no duration and no significance level**, so read the direction and discount the magnitude per test 2 below. But **the counter-metric in that same case study was reported as "only a minor increase in cancellations" and was never quantified.** *Minor* is not a number. Annual-forcing tests systematically under-report the refund, cancellation and support-cost tail — which is the general form of the anti-pattern in the next section.

**The operating rule:** push annual only when the subscription is the primary purchase decision, and define the counter-metrics before you call it a win — refund rate, day-30 and day-90 cancellation, and billing support contacts. If day-90 cancellations on annual exceed monthly's day-90 churn, the default is wrong. **And push it with de-risking (longer trial, honest renewal disclosure, dated reminders) rather than with pressure (timers, deleted monthly, guilt).**

### Optimising a shallow metric and calling it a win

**Mechanism.** Every persuasion element has a proxy metric that moves faster and more visibly than revenue: clicks, add-to-carts, trial starts, paywall views. Because the proxy is upstream, it responds to changes that *reduce* the downstream number — most reliably to changes that increase curiosity or exposure rather than intent. Reporting the proxy as the result is not usually a lie; it is the metric that was instrumented.

**The evidence, four published specimens.**

1. **Price visibility, measured on clicks, twice, in opposite directions.** Microsoft's Office Online store redesign that *displayed* the price got **64% fewer Buy-button clicks**. The authors' own post-hoc read: *"when the price is not shown there are more clicks because some users are looking for this crucial bit of information"* — and their own conclusion is the lesson: *"it is entirely possible that no more users purchased the product … an excellent example of a case where the experimenters should have done the additional work to measure what should have been the OEC, actual purchases."* The mirror image: PriceCharting *removed* the price from a CTA and reported **+620.9% clickthrough** (1 week, 99.9% significance, **no sample size**) — again on clicks, never on purchases. **A price-visibility test scored on clicks measures curiosity, not demand.**

2. **A published "win" that never reached significance on money.** Royal Discount's famous 15px → 20px bold price test is cited everywhere as "+10.21% revenue." **The source itself says significance was not reached on the revenue goal**; only the upstream Add-to-Cart metric (+36.54%) moved convincingly. A proxy-metric win, relabelled as a revenue win in downstream retellings.

3. **A null hidden inside a win.** FitnessAI moved the paywall before onboarding: paywall views **+50%**, install-to-trial **2×**, revenue **2×** — and **trial-to-paid stayed the same.** The mechanism is *exposure, not persuasion*: more people saw the paywall and the same fraction of triallists converted. Any claim that an earlier paywall "converts better" conflates the two.

4. **Winning engagement while losing the money.** MSN Video post-rolled its ad instead of pre-rolling: the 6-week return rate rose **just over 2%** and ad impressions fell **more than 50%** — net negative. This is one of the very few published tests with a genuine long horizon (2-week cohort, 6-week tracking), and it flipped sign at that horizon. Paywall softening and trial extension routinely win the engagement metric and lose revenue the same way.

**The cost accounting.** Consider what the base rates imply. Only about **one-third** of well-designed Microsoft experiments improved their key metric; **75% of 150,000 QualPro ideas over 22 years had no impact or hurt**; **3 of 21 people** correctly guessed the winning design in the MSN Real Estate contest. Against those, GoodUI advertises **92% success and 23% median impact across the 26 case studies it sells for $289**, and VWO's public library is, as far as the research could tell, **100% wins across ~17 case studies** — losses appear only in a post specifically about learning from bad results. Nobody is lying. You are reading the right tail of a distribution whose body is null. And **2 of ~45 harvested case studies publish a sample size; 4 publish a significance level.** You are usually being handed a numerator with no denominator.

**Three further reasons published lifts are inflated**, all documented on pricing tests specifically. **Early stopping**: Airbnb's max-price-filter test crossed **p ≈ 0.05 with +4% on day 7** and finished **null**, because *"users often take a long time to book, so the early converters have a disproportionately large influence in the beginning of the experiment"* — every purchase-latency business has this property. **Low power inflates surviving effect sizes**, which is why the literature is full of +620.9% and +125%. **Seasonality and concurrent changes**: Wistia's celebrated pricing restructure (sales 2×, revenue +46%) is reported alongside an **88% cut in ad spend in the same period** — a traffic-mix change large enough to produce the entire revenue effect on its own.

**The operating rules.** Score price tests on purchases, never on clicks. Never redesign the whole pricing page in one jump — BigCommerce's single-jump redesign lost **−4% trials and −25% demos**, and the diagnosed second iteration recovered **+15% / +45%**; the redesign-as-one-jump is the single most-published *losing* pattern in the corpus. Fix the sample size in advance and stop reading the p-value until the test is over. And note the one place where *not* testing is itself the negative result: a mandatory PSD2/3DSecure v2 checkout change shipped untested took credit-card subscriptions to **~zero for a couple of weeks** because an error-tracking script blocked the 3DS prompt, detected only via three customer complaints.

### Flat pricing that inverts on power users

**Mechanism.** A flat fee against a variable cost-to-serve is a bet that usage does not have a heavy tail. It does. And flat pricing does not degrade gradually as usage rises — it crosses zero and keeps going, so a small number of accounts consume the margin of a large number of others.

**The evidence.** `[derived — a published stress test, not a measurement of any real company's book.]` At **$0.04 cost-to-serve per task**, which is the example's own assumption rather than a market rate:

| User | Tasks/mo | Cost | Flat $30/mo | Pure usage @ $0.08 | Hybrid: $15 base + overage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light | 100 | $4 | **86.7%** GM | 50% GM | 73.3% GM |
| Median | 500 | $20 | **33.3%** GM | 50% GM | 42.9% GM |
| Power | 2,500 | $100 | **−233%** GM | 50% GM | 48.7% GM |

Source: Digital Applied, 2026-06-30. **The $0.04 is this published stress test's own assumption. It is a generic example, not a market rate, and it is specifically not Ledgerly's cost-to-serve**, which is $0.060 per receipt before the cost levers and $0.0186 after them.

**Read the bottom-left cell carefully.** The light user contributes about $26 of margin on the flat $30 plan and the power user destroys about $70, so a single power user at 2,500 tasks consumes the margin contributed by **roughly three** light users. The ratio matters less than the sign: the power user's contribution is negative rather than merely thin, which is what "inverts" means and what no volume of light users can be relied on to absorb. Pure usage pricing is margin-stable but revenue-unpredictable and hostile to buyers; **hybrid is the only column simultaneously acceptable at every row**, which is the arithmetic reason each annual monetization survey reports hybrid rising against its own prior-year base — 41% from 27% (n=240, 2025) and 37% from 25% (n=230, 2026). Note that the two surveys' *levels* are not comparable, because the 2026 respondent mix skews far more enterprise, so read each rise within its own survey and do not draw a trend line across them.

**The corroboration from production.** From **2026-04-04** Anthropic barred Claude Pro and Max flat plans from powering third-party agent frameworks, with the reported rationale that a single agentic session could consume **$1,000–$5,000 of API cost** — multiplied across thousands of power users, the flat-rate maths broke. Cursor and Replit both revisited pricing in 2025 for the same reason. **Agentic usage has no stable pricing answer yet; these are defensive reactions, not designs.**

**The cost accounting, including the version of this that looks like the fix.** A flat "N analyses for $X" package with a hard cap is the −233% column's cousin: it protects margin and converts your best customers into blocked customers. The two things to write into terms on day one: that the allowance covers interactive use by the account holder, with programmatic or resold access routed to a metered API; and that burn/allowance terms are under change control.

**The counterweight, so this is not read as "always meter."** In the consumer literature, flat-rate bias and prospective accounting both point the other way — at a sub-$30/mo consumer price point, metering a conscious user action taxes the behaviour that produces retention, and **37% of sub-$5M-ARR companies still use flat fees (n=230, 2026)**, because comprehensibility can beat theoretical optimality. **The rule is not "never flat." It is: never flat without knowing your usage distribution's tail, and never flat where per-use cost is real.**

### Modelling revenue instead of shipping it

**Mechanism.** At small N the metrics that feel most decision-grade are the ones that require the most extrapolation, so effort flows to the model rather than to the product. The model then produces a number precise enough to act on and wrong by an unbounded margin, and — because it is unfalsifiable at that sample size — nothing ever corrects it.

**The evidence: the arithmetic of your own ignorance.** The precision of a churn estimate is governed by the **number of churn events observed**, not the number of customers. Because `LTV ∝ 1/churn`, the relative error in LTV is approximately the relative error in churn, and for small `p` the relative standard error collapses to `≈ 1/√k` where `k` is observed cancellations. `[derived — author's arithmetic in L20 §5 at 10% true monthly churn and $8.50 ARPU × GM, not a cited benchmark.]`

| Subscriber-months observed | Expected churn events | 95% CI on monthly churn | Implied LTV | LTV spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 3 | 0% – 20.7% | $41 – ∞ | **unbounded** |
| 100 | 10 | 4.1% – 15.9% | $54 – $207 | **3.8×** |
| 250 | 25 | 6.3% – 13.7% | $62 – $135 | **2.2×** |
| 1,000 | 100 | 8.1% – 11.9% | $71 – $105 | **1.5×** |

**With 30 subscriber-months of history — "I have 15 customers and it's been two months" — the 95% interval on churn includes values arbitrarily close to zero, so the implied LTV has no upper bound. A CAC:LTV ratio computed on that data is not a weak estimate; it is not an estimate.**

And the bias is not only noise. Fader & Hardie show that ignoring heterogeneity produces a **systematically downward-biased** estimate of the residual value of a customer base, because high-churn-propensity customers leave first and the surviving cohort is progressively enriched with low-churn customers, so cohort-level retention rates typically *increase* over time. Two consequences: computing churn over your first few months and extrapolating **understates** the survivors' value; computing it from long-tenured survivors **overstates** new cohorts. These are two different populations. Skok & Reiss name three further assumptions in the canonical `LTV = (ARPA × GM%) / churn` that "typically don't hold true in the real world" — constant churn, linear expansion, constant gross margin — plus the terminal failure mode that with negative churn **LTV can become infinite**. For a consumer subscription there is a fourth they do not cover: monthly and annual plans produce churn events on totally different clocks and **cannot be averaged into one number**.

**The cost accounting.** A ratio starts to carry decision-grade information at roughly **100 observed churn events** and directional information at about **25**. Below ~10 it is decoration. At 10%/month churn, 25 events ≈ 250 subscriber-months. **Use payback period instead** — `CAC / (ARPU_monthly × GM%)` — because it uses only data you have already observed and requires zero extrapolation of the churn curve.

**And the substitution that actually pays at this size.** The single highest-return action for a product under 100 customers is **not** a pricing change or a model: it is the eight Tier-0 events. Without `churn_type` on subscription expiry you will spend months optimising a price to fix what is a failed-payment problem in **roughly a third of cases at sub-$10 price points**. Without `paywall_viewed` you have no denominator and therefore no pricing metric at all. Without `holdout_group` on offers, **every retention campaign you ever run will report success** — because ~20% of churned monthly consumer subscribers return unprompted within a year, and an un-held-out win-back will claim credit for all of them.

### A worked example of citation laundering — the Hotjar 2.8%

This one deserves its own section, because it is the clearest specimen the research produced and because the failure mode it demonstrates is now general.

The claim is *"Hotjar's 2025 analysis of ~3,000 SaaS pricing pages found average pricing-page conversion of 2.8%, top quartile 6.5–12%."* It is specific. It names a credible publisher, a plausible sample size, a recent year, a mean and a quartile. Every surface feature of a real statistic is present. It has none of the substance.

**Here is what source-chasing actually found.** Hotjar's own pricing-page article — now redirected to `contentsquare.com/blog/pricing-page-optimization/` — was fetched specifically to test this claim. **It contains exactly one statistic, a Portent page-speed figure, and no such study.** A `site:hotjar.com` search for pricing-page conversion benchmarks returns only individual customer case-study anecdotes and **Hotjar's own explicit statement that a standard pricing-page conversion benchmark cannot be pinned down** — the publisher supposedly behind the number says the number cannot exist. The only page found "citing" it, leadsuitenow.com, **returns 403 to fetchers and offers no outbound citation of its own.**

**Where it does circulate** is a cluster of **2026-dated AI-written SaaS-pricing SEO posts** — 925studios, influenceflow, orbix, acceleroi and others — **citing each other**. None cites Hotjar directly. Each is, to the next one, a source.

**And here is the part that makes this dangerous rather than merely wrong: search-engine answer boxes now return it as confirmed.** A researcher who checks the claim the ordinary way — by searching for it — receives a confident affirmation assembled from the citation ring. The verification step returns a positive. This is the laundering: a hallucinated or invented citation enters the SEO corpus, accumulates cross-references that look like independent corroboration, and is then re-served by summarizers as a settled fact. **The claim's confidence increases monotonically with the number of times it is repeated, and its evidentiary basis never changes from zero.**

Three lessons, and they generalise past this one number:

1. **A search summary confirming a statistic is not verification.** It is frequently the laundering mechanism itself. Only fetching the named primary counts.
2. **Cross-references are not independence.** Five posts citing the same fabrication are one fabrication, not five sources. Check whether any of them cites the *primary* rather than each other.
3. **"The publisher's own page contains no such study" is a stronger finding than "I could not find it."** Chase the claim to the named publisher and read what they actually published. In this case they published a statement that the benchmark cannot be pinned down.

## How to tell folklore from evidence

Four tests, in this order. They are drawn from what actually worked in this research, not from general scepticism.

**1. Chase the citation to a primary source, and fetch it.** Not the post that cites it — the thing itself. This single step killed the Hotjar 2.8% (the publisher's own page contains no such study), the "+90% first-person CTA" (real test, dead post, only a blocked Wayback capture), the "30–40% badge lift" (the named research firm has no such paper), and the "1% price = 11% profit" (a 1990s McKinsey study of non-software businesses, re-attributed). Note what happens when the primary *is* reached and disagrees: the "53% Adapty trial-to-paid" collapsed to 25.6% on direct fetch, and the "up to 46% local payment methods" collapsed to +12% revenue / +7.4% conversion with a disclosed method. **If the chase terminates in a 403, a 404, or another blog post, you have found folklore.** Record it as `[unverified-at-source]` and do not use the number.

**2. Demand a sample size and a date, and refuse the claim without both.** Across ~45 harvested A/B case studies, **2 published a sample size and 4 published a significance level.** A statistic without an n is a numerator with no denominator, and a statistic without a date cannot be checked for staleness — which is how NN/g's 2010 "80% above the fold" survived its own author's 2018 correction to 57%, and how Baymard's 2016 form-field counts are still quoted as current against the published 2024 figure of 11.3. **The date test is not pedantry: AI-pricing figures from 2024 are already stale, and the most-cited AI margin band originates in 2020.**

**3. Check whether the publisher sells the conclusion.** This does not make a finding false, and it is not an excuse to dismiss vendor data — the largest and most useful datasets in this library are vendor-published. It changes what you require of it. Adapty sells paywall A/B infrastructure and publishes that experimenters earn up to 40× more; Superwall sells paywall tooling and publishes that better paywalls convert 37% better; Price Intelligently sold pricing consulting and published that pricing is 4× acquisition; Paddle/ProfitWell sells pricing consulting and publishes that 8 of 10 per-user pricers are wrong. **The test that separates them is methodology disclosure.** Superwall discloses 40M+ opens, a date range, a ≥50-open threshold and an exclusion rule, and earns more trust than vendors who disclose none. Price Intelligently's most-quoted numbers disclose nothing and could not be reached. **When a vendor's finding is exactly the finding their product needs, require the method; if the method is absent, treat the number as marketing.** Weigh the corroborating case especially: when Metronome, which profits from credit adoption, calls credits "a bridge, not a destination," that is evidence *against* interest and worth more than either party's house thesis.

**4. Treat any statistic that appears only on 2026-dated SEO pages as fabricated until proven otherwise.** This is the newest and now the most necessary test. The genre — "Pricing Page Conversion Statistics 2026: A 4,200 A/B Test Study", "we analysed N thousand pricing pages" — is overwhelmingly AI-generated, publishes no dataset, and cross-cites within its own cluster. **The tell is a specific-sounding figure whose only carriers are recent content-farm pages that reference each other and never the primary.** Because search summarizers now re-serve these as confirmed, the burden of proof has to invert: the default verdict is fabricated, and only a fetched primary with an n and a date overturns it.

**A fifth test, for the numbers that survive the first four: ask what the effect is measured *on*.** A great many real, well-sourced, correctly-dated figures are measured on clicks, trial starts or paywall views rather than on money, and are then reported as revenue results — the Microsoft −64%, the PriceCharting +620.9%, the Royal Discount +10.21% that never reached significance on revenue, the FitnessAI 2× that was exposure rather than persuasion. **A sound citation attached to the wrong metric is as misleading as a fabricated one, and it is much harder to spot because everything about the provenance checks out.**
