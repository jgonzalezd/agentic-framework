# Layer 12 — Experimentation

**Layer slug:** `experimentation` · **Loaded at:** Phase 8

This layer governs how a pricing change gets tested and how a published pricing result gets read. It decides what to test first, which metric is primary, when to stop, and how much of a headline lift to believe. The thing that goes wrong most often is not a badly designed test — it is a *confidently reported* one. Of roughly 45 published pricing and checkout case studies harvested for this library, **2 report a sample size and 4 report a significance level** (L09 harvest, accessed Aug 2026). Almost every number circulating in the pricing-page genre is a numerator with no denominator, and a founder who plans against those numbers is planning against the right tail of a distribution whose body is null.

## How to use this layer

Read **P-281** first — it is the discount function you apply to every other number in this library, including the numbers in the other eleven layers. Then read **P-282** and **P-283** together, because the choice of primary metric decides whether a test is runnable at all, and at low traffic that choice is the whole design. **P-284** (stopping) and **P-289** (base rates) are load-bearing in both regimes and cost nothing to obey.

In the **COLD** regime — no traffic, no instrumentation, or no customers — the operative patterns are **P-294** (attach a falsifier), **P-296** (non-inferiority monitoring on forced changes), and **P-291** (counter-metrics). The rest of the layer is read as a reason *not* to propose tests. In the **HOT** regime the full set applies, and the sample-size arithmetic in `references/experimentation.md` decides which entries in the test queue survive contact with the traffic.

Situational: **P-286** (price-visibility tests), **P-287** (redesigns), **P-293** (funnel-position tests), **P-295** (benchmark comparison). Apply them only when the run touches that specific move.

## Patterns

### P-281 — Ask for the denominator before you believe the lift

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: A conversion lift is a ratio of two ratios. Without the sample size behind it you cannot tell whether the reported effect is a measurement or an artifact of noise, and underpowered tests systematically publish inflated effects, because the only results that clear significance in a small sample are the ones noise pushed far from zero. Publication then filters again: vendors write up the wins.
- **works-when**: reading any published lift, any competitor teardown, any vendor case study, or any number a stakeholder brings to the meeting.
- **fails-when**: the source is a large-N observational benchmark rather than an experiment. There the denominator is published and enormous and still tells you nothing causal — see P-295. Also fails as an excuse for paralysis: a direction without a denominator is still worth more than a guess, it is just worth nothing as a forecast.
- **observed-effect**: Of ~45 published A/B case studies harvested across VWO, RevenueCat, GoodUI, Conversion.com and Growth.Design, **2 report a sample size** and **4 report a significance level** (L09 harvest, accessed Aug 2026). The two most-cited large numbers in that set — PriceCharting **+620.9%** clickthrough and Flos USA **+125%** checkout conversion — publish no sample size. GoodUI's one fully public pattern is the counter-example that shows what disclosure does to a headline: pattern #1 "Remove Coupon Fields", 10 tests, verdict "Likely Better" at 2.5 evidence, **Sales median +0.8%**, and a formal meta-analysis of the 9 sales tests giving **+0.5% combined at p = 0.1335** — not significant (goodui.org/patterns/1/, accessed 2026-08-12).
- **evidence**: `medium` — this is a census of one lane's harvest of ~45 studies, not a random sample of the published literature. The direction of the finding is not in doubt; the exact proportion would move under a different harvest.
- **sources**: L09 case-study harvest (exp-platform.com, vwo.com, revenuecat.com, goodui.org, conversion.com, growth.design) ; https://goodui.org/patterns/1/
- **how-to**: For every external lift you are about to act on, write down four things: sample size per variant, duration, significance level, and the exact metric. Where a field is missing, write `n/p`. If three or four fields are `n/p`, the study may rank an idea and may not forecast an outcome — carry that distinction into the deliverable. Never let an undenominated percentage become the expected value in a business case; use it to order a queue, then size the opportunity from your own funnel.
- **example-copy**: "Published lift: +34% purchases (GoodUI Datastory #11, ticketing checkout; sample, duration and significance paywalled). Used to rank this idea, not to forecast it."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's audit cites Paltalk's **+6.74%** purchase lift for putting all plans above the fold (53 days, 50/50 split of all incoming traffic, sample not published). At Ledgerly's ~2,000 monthly visitors and an assumed 0.5% visitor→paid rate, a true +6.74% effect moves 10 new paying customers a month to 10.7 — a difference Ledgerly could not detect in a decade. The Paltalk figure earns the change a place on the page, not a line in a forecast.

### P-282 — Declare the deep metric primary before the test starts

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Interventions that lower the perceived cost of the *next click* frequently move the abandonment one step later rather than removing it. Scored on a progression metric, that reads as a win. GoodUI quantified how much of a shallow win survives to the sale, and roughly a quarter of the variance is the honest answer.
- **works-when**: always, for any pricing, paywall or checkout experiment.
- **fails-when**: never on principle — but low-traffic sites face a real bind, because deep metrics need far more traffic to detect. That bind is resolved by P-283, not by quietly promoting a shallow metric to primary after the fact.
- **observed-effect**: GoodUI correlation study, 2023-11-30: adds-to-cart → sales **R = 0.4983** (44 tests); all progression metrics → sales **R = 0.5085** (119 tests); checkout visits → sales **R = 0.6085, p ≈ 0** (533 tests). Conversion.com's independent dataset: adds-to-cart → orders **R = 0.4349** (~200 tests), with agreement rising from **35%** agnostic of significance to **66%** when filtered for significance on orders. GoodUI's stated conclusion: *"the closer the metric to a sale/order, the higher the correlation."* The purest cautionary case is Thomasnet's registration wall: **+1135% signups, −18% visits to company profile pages, and the team rejected the test** (published 2021-02-19), with GoodUI noting the baseline registration rate was "incredibly low to begin with," which manufactured the spectacular relative number.
- **evidence**: `strong` — a 533-test correlation on the deepest proxy plus a second independent dataset agreeing on direction and magnitude.
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/blog/do-adds-to-cart-or-progression-metrics-correlate-with-sales-in-a-b-tests/ ; https://goodui.org/blog/why-did-thomasnet-reject-a-registration-wall-a-b-test-with-a-record-1135-to-signups/
- **how-to**: Name the primary metric in writing before the variant is built, and make it completed paid conversions or revenue per visitor. Record every progression metric as diagnostic — they explain *why* a result happened and they never decide *whether* it happened. If the primary metric is not powered, say so in the test plan rather than substituting a shallow metric silently; the substitution is what turns an honest null into a published win.
- **example-copy**: "Primary metric: paid subscriptions per 1,000 pricing-page visitors. Diagnostics (not decision metrics): pricing-page → checkout rate, plan-card click rate, checkout completion rate."
- **worked-example**: A Ledgerly test of "Solo" vs "Solo — for the freelancer who files once a month" scored on plan-card clicks would need roughly a **+34% relative** move to clear a quarter of traffic (see the arithmetic in `references/experimentation.md`). Scored on paid conversions it would need **+130%**. Declaring clicks primary makes the test look runnable; it does not make the answer mean anything, because a click win of that size carries R ≈ 0.50 to the sale.

### P-283 — When the deep metric is unpowered, climb to the nearest defensible proxy and state the discount

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Proxy metrics trade inferential strength for statistical sensitivity. A metric with a higher baseline rate needs a far smaller sample to detect a given relative effect, so moving up the funnel buys power. The cost is measurable: each rung further from the money lowers the correlation with revenue.
- **works-when**: the revenue test would take longer than a quarter, the proxy sits as close to payment as your funnel allows, and you write the correlation discount into the conclusion.
- **fails-when**: the intervention plausibly moves the proxy in the opposite direction to revenue. Price-visibility changes are the canonical case (P-286), and so is anything that widens the top of the funnel by lowering qualification — Thomasnet's +1135% signups against −18% downstream visits.
- **observed-effect**: The ladder, from GoodUI's 2023-11-30 correlation study: checkout visits → sales **R = 0.6085, p ≈ 0** (533 tests); all progression → sales **R = 0.5085** (119 tests); adds-to-cart → sales **R = 0.4983** (44 tests); Conversion.com adds-to-cart → orders **R = 0.4349** (~200 tests). No published correlation was located for plan-card clicks or CTA clicks specifically. GoodUI's own framing: an R around 0.50 means a shallow win explains roughly a quarter of the variance in whether the deep metric moved.
- **evidence**: `medium` on the correlations themselves — the 533-test and 119-test pools are large, but the ladder this pattern prescribes climbing is entirely GoodUI's, published by the vendor whose product is the pattern library, and only the adds-to-cart rung has an independent replication (Conversion.com's R = 0.4349), which is not enough to rate the whole ladder `strong`. `anecdote` on any specific rung not covered by the 533-test or 119-test pools.
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/blog/do-adds-to-cart-or-progression-metrics-correlate-with-sales-in-a-b-tests/
- **how-to**: Use checkout-page visits, not plan-card clicks, as the proxy — that is the one measured at R = 0.61 across 533 tests rather than R ≈ 0.50. Write the conclusion in the form "the proxy moved +X%; at R = 0.6085 this is consistent with a revenue effect anywhere from negative to +X%, and we are not claiming the revenue effect." Then set a date to re-read realised revenue over the following quarter as an uncontrolled check. Never let a proxy result close a question; let it re-rank a queue.
- **example-copy**: "Result: pricing-page → checkout visits +18% (proxy metric; correlation with sales R = 0.6085 across 533 tests, GoodUI 2023). Revenue effect not measured and not claimed."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly cannot power a paid-conversion test on any realistic change. At an assumed 5% pricing-page → checkout rate it can detect roughly a **+34% relative** move in a quarter, against **+130%** on paid conversion — about 4× the sensitivity. That is the trade written out: 4× more sensitive, and worth R ≈ 0.61 of the answer.

### P-284 — Fix the sample size and the end date before launch, and never stop at the first crossing

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Monitoring a running test continuously and stopping at the first crossing of p < 0.05 is a multiple-comparisons procedure with a true false-positive rate far above 5%. Purchase-latency businesses make it worse: early converters are systematically unrepresentative of the cohort, so the early estimate is biased as well as noisy.
- **works-when**: always. This is the highest-value discipline in the layer and it costs nothing but patience.
- **fails-when**: never, with one legitimate exception — a pre-registered sequential design with an alpha-spending schedule lets you peek by planning for it in advance. There is also one asymmetric case: stopping early on a **guardrail breach** (payments failing, refunds spiking) is a different decision from stopping early on a win, and it is allowed.
- **observed-effect**: Airbnb's max price filter test ($300 → $1000) crossed **p ≈ 0.05 with a +4% effect on day 7** and finished **null** — "the final effect size was practically null." Airbnb's stated cause: *"users often take a long time to book, so the early converters have a disproportionately large influence in the beginning of the experiment."* The same post records that a dummy experiment broken down by 100 countries should be expected to yield ~5 spurious non-neutral results by construction (Airbnb Engineering, Medium, 2014).
- **evidence**: `strong` — standard practice across the industrial experimentation literature, and stated here with a published day-by-day trajectory on an actual price test.
- **sources**: https://medium.com/airbnb-engineering/experiments-at-airbnb-e2db3abf39e7
- **how-to**: Compute the required sample per variant before building the variant, convert it to a date at current traffic, and write both into the test plan. Do not look at significance before that date. Run whole weeks so day-of-week effects cancel, and for at least one full billing cycle when the metric is subscription revenue. Pre-register any subgroup you intend to read; a subgroup finding you did not pre-register is a hypothesis for a fresh test, never a result.
- **example-copy**: "Test window: 2026-09-01 to 2026-12-01 (13 whole weeks). Required n: 15,600 visitors per variant. Significance will not be read before 2026-12-01. Guardrails checked daily: payment success rate, refund requests."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's monthly billing cycle means the shortest honest window for any revenue-metric test is one full cycle plus the refund window — call it 45 days — before the first read is even meaningful. Against a required 15.6 months for a +50% effect (see `references/experimentation.md`), the 45-day figure is not a stopping rule, it is a floor beneath an already-unrunnable test.

### P-285 — Spend the testing budget on structure, not cosmetics

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: The size of an effect determines whether it is detectable at all. Structural levers — what is bundled, what is metered, what is free, how the plan is billed, where the paywall sits — change the buyer's decision. Cosmetic levers — endings, fonts, badge wording, button colour — change the rendering of a decision already made. At any given traffic level the structural change is detectable and the cosmetic one is not.
- **works-when**: you have a finite number of test slots per year, which is every product below large scale.
- **fails-when**: you are transacting at volumes where a 1% effect is both detectable and material, and the structural questions are already settled. Then cosmetics are the remaining surface.
- **observed-effect**: Adapty's experiment win-rates for LTV uplift by lever, across 105,000 paywalls in a 16,000-app / $3B cohort (2026) — **mobile in-app purchase, not web, exactly as flagged in sibling patterns P-291, P-292 and P-293 for the same publisher family**: localization **62.3%**, trial structure **59.6%**, plan duration **58.7%**, number of plans **57.1%**, price changes **45.5%**, **visual/copy only 34.6%**. Two of the top four levers are things a web pricing page does not have in the same form — a store paywall's trial structure and plan duration are configured as store products — so the ranking cannot be carried across as a ranking. From the behavioural literature: choice overload pooled to **d ≈ 0.02** across ~50 experiments (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, *JCR* 2010); charm pricing failed to reproduce in preregistered replications (Fenneman et al., *PLOS ONE* 2022, N = 266 / 4,788 decisions, just-below 47.1% vs rounded 45.9% vs control 45.3%, n.s.; Escher et al., *Frontiers* 2026, two preregistered studies, N = 701 total from 284 and 417, no effect on purchase intentions); the attraction/decoy effect produced **11 reliable effects from 91 attempts** (Yang & Lynn 2014). Against those, Wistia's packaging change — all tiers get all features, tiers differ only on monthly upload volume — reported **sales 2× and revenue +46%**, though confounded by a simultaneous 88% ad-spend cut and publishing no sample size.
- **evidence**: `medium`, and it is worth saying plainly what the ordering rests on once the cohort caveat is applied. The Adapty ranking is large-N but vendor-published, uncontrolled for confounds, and **mobile in-app purchase carried onto a web pricing page in the worked example below**, so it cannot be the quantitative basis for a web test order by itself. What survives the caveat is the cosmetic half of the case, and it is the stronger half: choice overload pooled to d ≈ 0.02 across ~50 experiments, charm pricing failing two independent sets of preregistered replications, and the decoy effect reproducing 11 times in 91 attempts — three independent research groups, none of them vendors, all finding cosmetic effects too small to detect at any traffic a small product commands. The structural side rests on undenominated case studies plus the arithmetic of detectability. **So the ordering is carried by a well-replicated null on cosmetics and a power argument, with the Adapty ranking as a directionally consistent mobile analogue rather than as the evidence.**
- **sources**: https://adapty.io/blog/free-trial-vs-direct-purchase-subscription-apps/ `[mobile IAP]` ; https://adapty.io/blog/high-performing-paywall-2026/ `[mobile IAP]` ; https://conversion.com/case-study/wistia/ ; Scheibehenne et al. *JCR* 2010 ; Fenneman et al. *PLOS ONE* 2022 ; Escher et al. *Frontiers* 2026
- **how-to**: Order the test queue by expected effect size, and put every cosmetic candidate below every structural one regardless of how cheap the cosmetic change is to build. For anything with a published effect under about 5%, ship the version the literature mildly favours and stop thinking about it — do not spend a slot you could spend on packaging or the value metric. When a stakeholder proposes a price-ending test, show them the two preregistered nulls.
- **example-copy**: n/a — this pattern governs the queue, not the page.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's queue has "test $9 vs $9.99 on Solo" and "test metering on receipts vs metering on connected accounts". The first has a published effect indistinguishable from zero in two preregistered replications and would need a sample Ledgerly will not have this decade. The second changes what the buyer is choosing between and is the only one worth a slot — and even it will be settled by interviews rather than by a split test.

### P-286 — Score a price-visibility test on purchases, never on clicks

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Hiding or de-emphasising the price removes the information a user needs to self-disqualify, so more unqualified users click through. The click metric rises while the purchase metric may be flat or down. Showing the price does the opposite: fewer clicks, better qualified.
- **works-when**: your funnel has a click step between the price and the purchase — CTA → cart, CTA → signup, paywall → plan picker.
- **fails-when**: the click *is* the conversion, in a single-step purchase, where the distinction collapses.
- **observed-effect**: Microsoft's Office Online store redesign, which displayed the product price where the control did not, produced **−64% Buy-button clicks** (control had more than twice the clickers). The authors' own conclusion: *"it is entirely possible that no more users purchased the product … an excellent example of a case where the experimenters should have done the additional work to measure what should have been the OEC, actual purchases"* (Kohavi, Crook & Longbotham, KDD, 2009; per-test sample not published). In the opposite direction, PriceCharting removed the price from a CTA and reported **+620.9% clickthrough at 99.9% significance over 1 week**, at a $2 price point, **with no sample published and clicks as the metric** (VWO case study, accessed Aug 2026).
- **evidence**: `medium` — the same direction at two independent companies, but **both measured the proxy**, so the revenue direction is untested in both.
- **sources**: https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf ; https://vwo.com/resources/case-studies/pricecharting-ab-tested-cta-increased-conversions
- **how-to**: Before launching any test that adds, removes, moves or restyles a price, write down the purchase-level metric and confirm you have both the instrumentation and the volume to move it. If you cannot power the purchase metric, do not run the test — you will get a large, significant, meaningless click result. If you must ship on the click metric, declare it a diagnostic and re-measure revenue over the following quarter.
- **example-copy**: n/a — this pattern governs the metric, not the page. The copy decisions it constrains live in `04-price-points.md` and `05-page-anatomy.md`.
- **worked-example**: A proposal to move Ledgerly's prices above the fold is exactly this test. Ledgerly can detect a change in pricing-page → checkout visits and cannot detect a change in paid conversions. Given Microsoft's documented −64%/purchases-unmeasured result, the honest plan is to ship the change on the priors in `05-page-anatomy.md`, instrument checkout visits as a diagnostic, and record the purchase-level question as unanswerable at this scale.

### P-287 — Never ship a whole pricing-page redesign without a diagnostic layer underneath it

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: A wholesale redesign changes dozens of things at once. If it loses you learn nothing about which change caused the loss; if it wins you cannot carry the learning forward. Redesigns also silently delete small elements that were load-bearing, and nobody remembers deleting them.
- **works-when**: heatmaps, scrollmaps and session recording are running from day one, so a loss is still diagnosable.
- **fails-when**: the redesign is your only shot and there is no diagnostic layer — then a loss is a pure write-off. Also fails when the goal was a publishable insight rather than a better page; for that goal, isolate one variable (and accept you cannot power it — see P-288).
- **observed-effect**: BigCommerce's pricing-page redesign v1 lost **−4% trial conversion and −25% demo conversion**. Diagnosis came from heatmaps and scrollmaps, which showed a small "Learn more" payment link had been deleted and that mobile users behaved differently; v2 then delivered **+15% trials, +45% demos**. No sample size or duration published (VWO, accessed Aug 2026). Independently, Microsoft's MSN Search Box test changed button size, button label and the treatment of "Popular Searches" at once and produced an explicit statistical draw: *"neither variant was statistically significantly better than the other"* (2009).
- **evidence**: `medium` — one detailed published loss-then-recovery plus an independent published null from bundled changes.
- **sources**: https://vwo.com/blog/leverage-bad-ab-test-results/ ; https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf
- **how-to**: If a redesign is unavoidable, run it with heatmaps and session recording from launch and treat the round as a diagnostic rather than a decision. Diff the old and new page element by element and list every element you removed — the losing element is usually something small nobody thought mattered. Rebuild v2 as the old page plus only the changes the diagnostics support.
- **example-copy**: n/a — governs process.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's audit recommends 9 page changes. Shipping them together is correct at this traffic (P-288), but the removal list must be written down first: if the top-up pack line, the "cancel anytime" microcopy or the receipts-per-month allowance disappears in the rebuild, the diff is the only record that it ever existed.

### P-288 — Bundle changes deliberately at low traffic, and give up attribution knowingly

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Orthodox CRO isolates one variable per test, which is correct for learning and ruinous for shipping at low traffic, because single small changes produce effects too small to detect. Bundling a coherent set of changes raises the expected effect into detectable range. The price is that you never learn which change did the work.
- **works-when**: traffic is limited, the changes all push the same direction on the same objection, and the goal is a better page rather than a publishable insight.
- **fails-when**: you need attribution for a future decision, or the bundled changes could plausibly cancel each other. Bundling opposing changes is how you manufacture a null (P-287, MSN Search Box).
- **observed-effect**: GoodUI's grouped-change results: Examine.com, 7 pricing-screen changes → **+28% sales** (2018-01-03); Designlab, 13 course-landing-page changes → **+49% enrollments** (2018-11-14); KensingtonTours, 12 landing-page patterns → **+42% leads** (2019-08-19); Bomgar, 6 landing-page changes → **+30% signups** (2018-05-10); Thomasnet, 6 signup-form changes → **+17% signups** (2019-07-26). **None disclose a sample size, confidence interval or significance level.** The published counterweight from the same source: Menufy.com, 4 checkout changes → a "highly significant" **+1% sales** (2018-10-30), with the author noting the test "brings us down a little closer to reality."
- **evidence**: `medium` — five published grouped wins and one published grouped near-miss, none with disclosed samples.
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/blog/7-pricing-screen-ui-changes-for-28-sales-on-examine-com/ ; https://goodui.org/blog/4-checkout-ui-changes-for-a-highly-significant-1-sales-on-menufy-com/
- **how-to**: Assemble 5 to 10 changes that all attack the same objection on the same screen, ship them as one variant, and measure revenue. Write down the attribution you are giving up, so nobody later cites the bundle as evidence for one element inside it. Calibrate to Menufy: 4 sensible checkout improvements bought +1%, and that was the outcome that reached significance.
- **example-copy**: "This is a bundled change (9 elements). If it wins we will not know which element did it, and no single element in it may be cited as evidence."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's 9 page changes ship as one variant. Even bundled, the arithmetic in `references/experimentation.md` says a paid-conversion read needs a **+130% relative** effect within a quarter, which no page bundle delivers. So the bundle ships on priors, with pricing-page → checkout visits recorded as the diagnostic and a 90-day revenue re-read as an uncontrolled check.

### P-289 — Plan for roughly 1 idea in 3 to work

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: The published base rate of experiment success is low even at organisations with world-class idea generation and unlimited traffic. Planning as if two-thirds of tests will fail changes what you build: cheaper tests, more of them, and no political capital staked on any one.
- **works-when**: you can lower the cost per experiment enough to run many.
- **fails-when**: your traffic supports only a handful of tests a year — then the base rate argues for *fewer, bigger* bets informed by qualitative research rather than a high-throughput testing programme. This is the Ledgerly case and it is the common one.
- **observed-effect**: Microsoft: *"evaluating well-designed and executed experiments that were designed to improve a key metric, only about one-third were successful"* (Kohavi, Crook & Longbotham, 2009). QualPro: **150,000 business-improvement ideas over 22 years, 75% had no impact or actively hurt performance** (Holland et al. 2005, via Microsoft 2009). Netflix "considers 90% of what they try to be wrong" (Moran 2007, via the same). Quicken Loans, after five years of testing: correct outcome guesses "about 33% of the time." MSN Real Estate design contest: **3 of 21 people** picked the winning design. Against these, **GoodUI publicly advertises a 92% success rate and 23% median impact across the 26 Datastories it sells for $289** (accessed Aug 2026), and its own pattern-driven self-audits report **67% (14 of 21) in Q1 2017** and **71% across 51 tests by January 2019**. Both sets of numbers can be honest; they measure different populations — all experiments run, versus experiments a vendor chose to write up and sell.
- **evidence**: `strong` — multiple independent large programmes over decades with published methodology.
- **sources**: https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf ; https://goodui.org/datastories/ ; https://goodui.org/blog/beating-chance-with-conversion-patterns-our-2017-q1-win-rate-report/
- **how-to**: Budget for a hit rate near one in three and design the programme around throughput rather than around any single test succeeding. Never let a failed pricing test become a referendum on the person who proposed it, or you stop hearing proposals. When someone calls a change a no-brainer, note that 3 of 21 professionals picked the winner in Microsoft's own documented contest.
- **example-copy**: n/a — governs planning.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly can run at most one meaningful pricing change per quarter, and at a one-in-three base rate that is roughly one win per year from testing alone. That arithmetic is the argument for settling the price level by interview and reserving the testing capacity — such as it is — for the single structural question that matters most.

### P-290 — Discount your own impact estimate before you rank the queue

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Every prioritisation framework (ICE, PIE, RICE) requires an impact estimate. Practitioners are measurably poor at producing one, and experience barely helps, which means a prioritised backlog is ordered largely by noise plus a systematic optimism bias.
- **works-when**: always, when ranking a pricing-experiment backlog or forecasting a change's effect for a stakeholder.
- **fails-when**: n/a — but note the study's own limit: 1,391 guesses against only **13** completed tests.
- **observed-effect**: GoodUI Experiment Estimation Study, published 2026-05-05: **1,391 guesses from 100+ professionals against 13 completed A/B tests.** Median absolute error **2.7 percentage points**; median signed error **+0.7pp**; **56% of all guesses overestimated** the real effect; directional accuracy **~64%**. By experience: 101–500+ experiments run gave **2.4–2.7pp** error versus **2.9–3.5pp** for less experienced, with overlapping confidence intervals — about **1pp** of improvement across an entire career. Having seen a similar experiment before helped slightly: **2.5pp vs 3.2pp**. Aggregating 5 estimators cut absolute error from **~2.8pp to ~2.3pp** but left the optimism bias unchanged at **+0.8pp**. Of the 13 underlying tests, **10 were genuinely positive and 3 negative or neutral**.
- **evidence**: `medium` — 1,391 observations sounds like a large sample and is not one for this purpose: the 1,391 guesses are made against only **13** completed tests, so the study has 13 independent outcomes, not 1,391, and every error statistic inherits that. It is also a single organisation, and that organisation sells the alternative to guessing. The direction — practitioners overestimate, and experience barely helps — is the usable finding; the specific 2.7pp and +0.7pp figures should be read as this one study's estimates rather than as calibrated constants.
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/blog/experiment-estimation-study/
- **how-to**: Stop ranking pricing experiments by predicted impact, because your estimates carry ~2.7pp of error against effects that are frequently smaller than that. Rank by cost to implement and by external repeatability evidence instead. Where you must state a number, shave roughly a percentage point off it to correct the documented optimism, and say that you have done so.
- **example-copy**: "Estimated effect: +3% (adjusted down 1pp from the initial +4% estimate, per the documented +0.7pp median optimism bias in practitioner forecasts; GoodUI, 1,391 guesses, 2026)."
- **worked-example**: The Ledgerly audit's instinct is that moving the top-up pack below the plans is worth "maybe 5%." Applying the correction gives ~4%, and the sample-size arithmetic says a 4% effect on paid conversion is undetectable at 2,000 monthly visitors within any planning horizon. The estimate is therefore not a forecast; it is a reason to ship the change and stop measuring it.

### P-291 — Pair every primary metric with a counter-metric and require it to stay flat

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Most pricing wins are transfers rather than creations — conversion borrowed from retention, revenue borrowed from refunds, volume borrowed from qualification. A single-metric test cannot see the transfer, so it reports the borrowing as income.
- **works-when**: any pricing change that alters who buys, what they commit to, or how much information they had when they bought.
- **fails-when**: the counter-metric needs a longer horizon than the test, which is usual. Then the counter-metric becomes a scheduled post-launch read (P-292), not a stopping condition.
- **observed-effect**: Mojo demoted the monthly plan behind a "view all plans" link and reported **+15–20% yearly take-up** with "only a minor increase in cancellations" — **the cancellation figure is never quantified** (RevenueCat, accessed Aug 2026). Microsoft's MSN Video post-roll test raised the 6-week return rate **just over 2%** while cutting ad impressions **more than 50%**, a net loss (2009). MSN's home-page ad test generated "tens of thousands of dollars per day" with **no statistically significant change in visit frequency** — a published null on the counter-metric, which almost nobody publishes. Thomasnet's registration wall produced **+1135% signups against −18% profile visits** and was rejected. Cohort context for the delayed tail: RevenueCat's 115,000-app 2026 cohort reports annual-subscriber year-1 cancellation at **72%**, with **35%** of those cancellations occurring in month 1 — mobile IAP data, not web.
- **evidence**: `medium` — several independent documented cases in the same direction; the counter-metric is more often acknowledged than measured.
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/paywall-tests-grow-app-revenue/ ; https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf ; https://goodui.org/blog/why-did-thomasnet-reject-a-registration-wall-a-b-test-with-a-record-1135-to-signups/ ; https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps/
- **how-to**: Before launch, name the counter-metrics and their acceptable bands: refund rate, chargeback rate, day-30 and day-90 cancellation, support contacts about billing, and downstream engagement. Write the bands as numbers. A test that wins on the primary and breaches a counter-metric band is not a win pending investigation — it is a loss pending an explanation.
- **example-copy**: "Guardrails: refund requests ≤ 2 in the window, day-30 cancellation on new annual subscriptions ≤ the trailing monthly rate, billing support contacts ≤ 3. A breach halts the rollout regardless of the primary metric."
- **worked-example**: If Ledgerly demotes the $5/50 top-up pack to push Solo customers up to Books, the counter-metric is not conversion — it is whether Books customers start requesting refunds in month 2 because they bought an allowance they do not use. At ~40 paying customers, 3 refunds is a 7.5% refund rate and is visible without any statistics at all. That is the one measurement Ledgerly's scale actually favours.

### P-292 — Read the result at the revenue horizon, not at checkout

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Short tests capture the reaction of existing users to *change*, not the steady-state preference of future users. Subscription changes in particular resolve at the first renewal, the refund window and the cancellation spike, all of which sit outside a typical test window.
- **works-when**: subscription and recurring-revenue products, annual-plan changes, trial-structure changes, and anything that alters commitment length.
- **fails-when**: one-off transactional purchases with no renewal and a short refund window, where checkout genuinely is the horizon.
- **observed-effect**: Microsoft's MSN Video test is the only published pricing-adjacent experiment in the harvest with a genuine long horizon — a 2-week cohort tracked for 6 weeks — and it is the one that flipped from a **+2% engagement win** to a **net loss** on ad impressions (2009). Test durations in the published record are far shorter: Ben ran ~2 weeks on a 2.20% → 2.59% subscription conversion change; PriceCharting ran 1 week; Paltalk ran 53 days. **No paywall or pricing case study in the L09 harvest reports a 90-day revenue read or a post-launch holdout.** Structural context from RevenueCat's 115,000-app 2026 cohort (mobile IAP, not web): annual cancellations cluster at **35% in month 1** and **9–14% in month 12** just before renewal.
- **evidence**: `medium` — the mechanism is well-documented and the absence of long-horizon reads in the published record is verifiable; only one published test actually demonstrates the flip.
- **sources**: https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf ; https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/average-subscription-renewal-rates-by-app-category/
- **how-to**: For any change to plan length, trial structure or commitment, schedule the real read at one full billing cycle plus the refund window, and a second at 90 days. Keep a small holdout where you can afford one, so the second read has a comparison. Where you cannot hold out, write the pre-change trailing rate down before you ship, because a post-hoc comparison with a number nobody recorded is not a comparison.
- **example-copy**: "Revenue read scheduled: 2026-10-15 (one billing cycle + 14-day refund window) and 2027-01-01 (90 days). Pre-change trailing 90-day paid conversion recorded 2026-09-01 at [value] for comparison."
- **worked-example**: If Ledgerly introduces an annual Books plan, the checkout number will look good immediately and mean nothing. The read that matters is how many of the first cohort of annual buyers are still there at day 90 and how many asked for a refund in the first 30 days. With ~40 customers those are countable events, not statistics.

### P-293 — Separate exposure from persuasion in any funnel-position test

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Moving a paywall or a pricing page earlier in a flow increases the fraction of users who ever see it. Total conversions rise because the top of the monetisation funnel widened, while the conditional rate at the paywall is unchanged. Reported as one number, that reads as "the paywall converts better," which is a different and unsupported claim.
- **works-when**: any test that changes where in the journey the price appears, or which users reach it.
- **fails-when**: the position change also changes the content of the offer, in which case exposure and persuasion are confounded again and you need both splits plus a diagnosis.
- **observed-effect**: FitnessAI moved the paywall before onboarding and added a video: paywall visibility **+50%**, install-to-trial **doubled**, **trial-to-paid "stayed the same"**, revenue doubled — no sample, duration or significance published. Rootd moved the paywall to the very start of onboarding and reported revenue **+500%**, again with no sample, duration or significance (RevenueCat, accessed Aug 2026). Observational context, explicitly not randomized: hard-paywall apps show **10.7% median D35 download-to-paid vs 2.1% for freemium** across a 115,000-app cohort, with **year-1 payer retention essentially identical at 27% vs 28%** (RevenueCat 2026, mobile IAP).
- **evidence**: `medium` — two independent apps in the same direction with no denominators, plus a large-N observational correlation that cannot establish causality. Note the cohort line: all of it is app-store data and none of it is a web checkout.
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/paywall-tests-grow-app-revenue/ ; https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps/
- **how-to**: Split the funnel into two reported rates — the share of users who reach the price, and the share of those who pay — and report both, every time. Add a retention counter-metric at day 30 and day 90, because earlier exposure selects for less-informed buyers. Treat the freemium-versus-hard-paywall benchmark gap as a hypothesis about your own product, not as an established effect, and never carry an app-store figure into a web-checkout deliverable without saying you are doing it.
- **example-copy**: "Reported as two rates: (a) visitors reaching the pricing page, (b) pricing-page visitors who pay. A change in (a) is exposure. A change in (b) is persuasion. We will not report their product as a single conversion improvement."
- **worked-example**: If Ledgerly moves its price behind the first receipt scan instead of showing it on the landing page, the number that will move is (a) — how many visitors ever see a price. Reporting a combined "+X% conversion" would hide whether the pitch got better or simply got seen by more people, and only the second is true at Ledgerly's likely effect size.

### P-294 — Attach a single falsifying observation to every recommendation you cannot test

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: A recommendation with no falsifier is indistinguishable from an opinion, and it survives indefinitely because nothing can dislodge it. A named falsifier converts a prior into a claim with an expiry date, which is the only form of accountability available in a COLD regime.
- **works-when**: COLD regime — no traffic, no instrumentation, or no customers. This is the mandatory substitute for a test queue, and Phase 8 requires one falsifier per recommendation from Phases 6 and 7.
- **fails-when**: the falsifier requires instrumentation the product does not have, or a sample it will not reach in the window. A falsifier that cannot arrive is a decoration. It also fails when the falsifier is written so loosely that any outcome satisfies it.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` — no study measures the effect of attaching falsifiers to pricing recommendations. The supporting argument is the documented forecasting record: practitioners' directional accuracy on experiment outcomes is **~64%** and median absolute error **2.7pp** across 1,391 guesses (GoodUI, 2026), and Microsoft's own designers picked the winning design **3 times in 21**.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — a method borrowed from experimental discipline, not a measured intervention.
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/blog/experiment-estimation-study/ ; https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf
- **how-to**: For each recommendation write four fields: the prior it rests on, one observation that would show it wrong, the single event or field that makes that observation visible, and a check-after date. The falsifier must be a countable thing rather than a rate wherever the customer count is small, it must be cheap to observe, and it must be something you would actually act on. Full method and worked Ledgerly examples in `references/experimentation.md` › The COLD-regime falsifier method.
- **example-copy**: "Falsifier — if 3 of the first 10 Books customers process fewer than 100 receipts in their first full month, receipts are not the metric they are buying and the fence is wrong. Visible once monthly per-account receipt counts are stamped. Check after 2026-11-01."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly is COLD at the point of sale. Every one of the audit's 9 recommendations therefore carries a falsifier of this shape, and the recommendations that cannot be given one are marked as unfalsifiable in the deliverable rather than quietly kept.

### P-295 — Never compare two benchmarks whose numerator, denominator and window you have not read

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: "Conversion rate" has no standard definition. Depending on the source it means any page goal, a trial start, a demo request, a purchase within six months, or a download-to-paid rate at day 35. Lining these numbers up produces a funnel that does not exist.
- **works-when**: always, before quoting or acting on any external benchmark — including the benchmarks in this library's own `benchmarks.md`.
- **fails-when**: never. This is the discipline most of the surrounding literature lacks.
- **observed-effect**: Three headline numbers that cannot be compared with each other: Unbounce reports a SaaS landing-page **median 3.8%** across 41,000 landing pages, 57M conversions and 464M pageviews (Q4 2024), where **any page goal counts, so an ebook download weighs the same as a demo request**; ChartMogul reports a **median 8%** free-to-paid across 200 self-reported B2B products (Jan 2026), defined as conversion **within six months**; Baymard reports **70.22%** cart abandonment as an average across **50 separate studies** with no stated common definition (updated 2025-09-22). Within a single vendor's own pages the instability persists: RevenueCat publishes year-1 realised LTV per payer for North America as both **$26.07** and **$32**, and "conversion rate" in that dataset variously means download→paid at D35, download→trial at D30, trial→paid, or paywall-view→purchase — figures that differ by an order of magnitude.
- **evidence**: `strong` — this is an observation about the sources themselves and is verifiable in each.
- **sources**: https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/saas-conversion-rate/ ; https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/ ; https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate ; https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps/
- **how-to**: Before adopting any benchmark, extract three things and write them next to the number: the numerator event, the denominator population, and the attribution window. Prefer medians to averages. Then benchmark against your own prior quarter, which is the only comparison with a controlled definition. Where you must cross a cohort line — app-store data onto a web checkout, B2B onto consumer — say that you are doing it and why, per the skill's Rule 4.
- **example-copy**: "Benchmark used: free-to-paid median 8% (ChartMogul, 200 self-reported B2B products, Jan 2026). Numerator: paying customers. Denominator: free signups. Window: 6 months. Not comparable to our 30-day dashboard figure."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's ~40 paying customers against ~2,000 monthly visitors is not a "2% conversion rate" comparable to anything, because 40 is a stock accumulated over months and 2,000 is a monthly flow. The first job of Ledgerly's measurement plan is to define one rate with a numerator, a denominator and a window, and record it — until then there is no baseline to test against and no benchmark that can be applied. Note the scope of this warning, because the library has confused it before: it is about **conversion-rate denominators**. A billing-event count is a different quantity with a different derivation — Ledgerly's ~31 charge events a month follow from its billing mix (P-296 here, P-252 in `checkout`) — and deriving an alarm threshold from that flow is the correct application of this pattern rather than a violation of it. What P-295 forbids is using the 40 itself as though it were a monthly flow.

### P-296 — Run a non-inferiority check on the changes you are forced to ship

- **layer**: `experimentation`
- **mechanism**: Mandatory changes — compliance, payment-provider migrations, SDK upgrades, tax-display rules — get shipped without tests because there is "no decision to make." There is still an implementation to validate, and payment-path failures are silent: no error reaches you, revenue simply stops.
- **works-when**: any change touching the payment path, third-party scripts, or the checkout DOM.
- **fails-when**: the change is legally required on a hard deadline with no rollback. Then instrument heavily and monitor rather than split-test.
- **observed-effect**: Analytics-Toolkit shipped a mandatory PSD2 / 3D Secure v2 implementation untested: *"we did not have any other new payment subscriptions via credit cards for a period of a couple of weeks."* The root cause was an error-tracking script interfering with the 3D Secure prompt and blocking it from appearing. It was detected only after **3 customer complaints over several days**. Customer LTV was **$360–$1,680** over 2 years and the author concluded that "taking the risk of shipping without testing was underestimated and it was not worth it" (2020). Survey context: **17%** of cart abandoners cite site errors or crashes (Baymard, n = 1,026 US adults, 2025).
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — one detailed, honestly reported incident, but the failure mode is generic and the mechanism is well understood.
- **sources**: https://blog.analytics-toolkit.com/2020/the-cost-of-not-ab-testing-case-study/ ; https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate
- **how-to**: Frame mandatory changes as non-inferiority tests: ship to a fraction of traffic and require the payment-success metric to stay within a defined margin before rolling forward. Where a split is impossible, put a hard alert on completed payments per hour with a threshold that fires within hours rather than days. Treat every third-party script injected into the checkout as a suspect and test the flow end to end with a real card after every deploy.
- **example-copy**: "Rollout gate: completed payments per 24h must stay within 30% of the trailing 14-day mean for 3 consecutive days before proceeding past 25% of traffic. Alert fires at 6 hours with zero completed payments."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's charge-event flow comes from its billing mix, not from its customer count: 30 of the 40 paying customers are billed monthly and 10 annually, so `30 + 10/12 = 30.83` — **roughly 31 charge events a month, about one a day and about seven a week**. A "payments per hour" alarm is still meaningless at that rate, because the expected count in a given hour is near zero and the alarm would fire on ordinary silence rather than on failure. The conclusion the pattern reaches is unchanged by the higher rate; only the reasoning is corrected. The scale-appropriate version is a synthetic check: a scripted or manual real-card purchase after every deploy that touches checkout, plus a standing alert on empty days. At about seven payments a week, a single empty day is unremarkable and **seven consecutive empty days is not a variance story at all** — it is roughly seven expected payments that did not arrive, so the alarm is firing on a rail that has stopped. The tighter 48-hour threshold in P-252 in `checkout` is the same derivation run for a faster detection window, and both come from the ~1/day flow rather than from the 40, which is a stock of customers (P-295).

## Interactions

**P-282 (deep metric primary) versus P-283 (climb to a proxy).** These are in direct tension and the tension is the point. P-282 is the rule; P-283 is the documented exception with its price attached. What decides between them is the sample-size arithmetic, not preference: if the deep metric reaches power inside a quarter, P-282 wins absolutely. If it does not, P-283 applies and the conclusion must carry the correlation figure. What is never allowed is running under P-282's framing and reporting under P-283's data.

**P-285 (structure over cosmetics) versus `04-price-points.md`.** The price-points layer contains real, sourced patterns about endings, framing and anchors. P-285 does not refute them; it deprioritises *testing* them. Ship the version those patterns favour and spend the test slot elsewhere. A conflict only arises if someone reads a price-points pattern as a reason to run a price-ending experiment, which the two preregistered nulls in P-285 rule out at any traffic level a solo product has.

**P-288 (bundle at low traffic) versus P-287 (never redesign in one jump).** These look contradictory and are not. P-288 bundles changes that all attack the same objection on the same screen, with a written removal list. P-287 forbids the *undiagnosed* wholesale rebuild. The separating condition is whether you can name every element you removed and whether a diagnostic layer is running. A bundle you can enumerate is P-288; a redesign you cannot is P-287's failure case.

**P-291 (counter-metrics) versus P-284 (do not stop early).** P-284 forbids stopping on a win; P-291 requires halting on a guardrail breach. The asymmetry is deliberate and it is not a violation of P-284: stopping early on a win inflates the false-positive rate on the thing you are trying to learn, while stopping early on damage costs you only the test. Write both rules into the plan so the distinction is decided before there is an incentive to blur it.

**P-293 (exposure versus persuasion) versus `11-expansion-and-retention.md`.** The retention layer's benchmarks are drawn from the same app-store cohorts as P-293's evidence. When both layers are cited in one deliverable, the cohort warning must be stated once and carried: hard-paywall-versus-freemium conversion, trial-length gradients and reactivation rates are all mobile in-app-purchase artifacts, and Phase 2's regime declaration is where that gets flagged.

**P-295 (read the definition) governs every other layer.** Any pattern in this library whose `observed-effect` carries an external benchmark is subject to P-295 before it is applied. In practice this most often bites on `benchmarks.md` in Phase 2 and on `11-expansion-and-retention.md` in Phase 7.

## Where this layer is thin

**Almost nothing here is a true price-*level* test.** The published evidence is overwhelmingly about presentation — layout, plan visibility, paywall length, framing, checkout friction. Experiments answering "should this cost $29 or $49" are nearly absent from the public record, plausibly because of the legal, fairness and legacy-pricing objections practitioners raise and because the required samples are out of reach for most companies. Any confident claim in this library about optimal price points is unevidenced by the experimentation record and rests on the derivation methods instead.

**The denominator census is a census of one harvest.** "2 of ~45 report a sample size" is the count from the L09 lane's harvest across VWO, RevenueCat, GoodUI, Conversion.com and Growth.Design, accessed August 2026. It is not a systematic review, and three sources refused automated retrieval entirely (CXL's pricing-experiments article, Unbounce, PostHog's Groove story). The direction of the finding is robust; the exact ratio would move.

**Two of the most useful negative results rest on dead primary pages.** Groove's null on `.00` versus `.99` versus `.97` price endings (`blog.groovehq.com` → Helply, now 404) and Buffer's reported ~$25K MRR loss from mobile price changes (`data.buffer.com`, ENOTFOUND) are both `[SECONDARY — primary offline]` and neither should be quoted as a verified figure. That both *failure* write-ups vanished while win-libraries persist is itself a data point about publication bias, and it is the reason this layer treats the absence of published nulls as informative.

**GoodUI's decision-relevant numbers are paywalled.** Every repeatability score, shallow median and deep median for the pricing, plan, checkout and signup patterns sits behind a $60/month subscription, verified individually against patterns #2, #17, #113, #114 and #115, which render the literal placeholder `X.X%`. What is public is the power data, and it is sobering: **#113 "More Or Fewer Plans" sits at 10.7% of its 90% cumulative power target at 2% MDE from 3 tests**, and **#114 "Less Or More Visible Prices" at 54.4% from 7 tests**. The two questions a founder most wants answered — how many tiers, and how prominent the price — are the two the largest meta-analytic dataset has least power on.

**Nothing here measures long horizons.** Microsoft's MSN Video design (2-week cohort, 6-week tracking, 2009) is the only test in the harvest with a genuine retention horizon, and it is the one that flipped from a win to a net loss. No paywall or pricing case study in the record reports a 90-day revenue read or a post-launch holdout, which means P-292 is a well-argued discipline with almost no published demonstration behind it.

**The largest datasets are observational and most of them are mobile.** RevenueCat (115,000+ apps) and Baymard (50 studies) carry the most statistical weight anywhere in this library and neither is randomized. Roughly 95% of the subscription benchmark data is app-store in-app-purchase behaviour, where trials require no card entry and cancellation is an operating-system flow. Applying any of it to a web checkout crosses a cohort line that Phase 2 must declare.

**B2B is badly underrepresented, and its one detailed pricing-page result is a loss.** The B2B entries in the harvest are lead-generation tests rather than pricing tests. BigCommerce's pricing-page redesign — the single detailed B2B pricing-page experiment available — lost.

**Nobody has measured what a founder at 2,000 monthly visitors should do.** The entire literature is written by and for organisations with traffic. The arithmetic in `references/experimentation.md` is derived from standard two-proportion power calculations rather than from any published study of low-traffic pricing decisions, because no such study was located. Treat that section as sound statistics applied to an unstudied situation, not as evidence.
