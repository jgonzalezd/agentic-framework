## Ladder arithmetic

**Two rungs, exactly 2× apart: $9.99 → $19.99.**

**The 2× spread is the whole design.** There is no subscription to position against, so **the two-rung one-off ladder does the work a monthly/annual toggle usually does.** Lite at $9.99 exists mainly to make Lifetime at $19.99 read as obviously correct — **doubling the price for "forever" is the cheapest possible upsell arithmetic a buyer can do in their head**, and it resolves in under a second.

**The effective entry price is half the sticker price, and the free tier is the mechanism.** The free tier trades an email for a **"50% off membership code"**, so the real entry price for a converted lead is **~$5.00 (Lite)** or **~$10.00 (Lifetime)** — derived from the stated 50%. **The free tier is not a product fence; it is a lead-capture instrument with a discount attached.**

At the discounted price the ladder becomes **$5.00 → $10.00** — still exactly 2×, and now positioned at genuine impulse-purchase level. **The spread survives the discount because the discount is proportional.**

**Against the corpus's verified bands:** $19.99 sits **inside the $9.95–$36 band** for a one-off self-serve digital deliverable (`one-off-report-market.md`), and at the discounted $10.00 it sits near the floor set by UnderstandMyself's $9.95. **This is a well-calibrated price for a no-human artefact, arrived at from a completely different category.**

**Refund arithmetic.** A **7-day money-back guarantee on a $19.99 one-time payment with zero marginal cost** is nearly free to offer and removes the last objection on a purchase whose value cannot be assessed before access. Compare `one-off-report-market.md`, where three comparable products offer **no refund at all** and leave the lever unclaimed.

**Does any add-on invert the ladder?** No add-ons exist.

**Does a lower tier plus add-ons reach a higher tier?** No stacking path — Lite cannot be topped up into Lifetime on the evidence captured. **Note the gap this leaves:** a Lite buyer who later wants Lifetime has no documented upgrade credit, so the two rungs may be mutually exclusive purchases. `Not captured in research.`

## What they do that most pages don't

**They run no subscription at all.** In a corpus where every other product either sells recurring billing or wishes it could, Nomads.com prices a one-off as terminal and lives on it.

**They say the words "then never again."** The phrase does what a paragraph of billing FAQ does in `notion.md` and `vercel.md`, in three words, for a buyer who has been trained to expect a trap.

**They set the real price with a discount code the free tier hands out.** The listed price is $9.99/$19.99; the price a converted lead pays is $5.00/$10.00. **The sticker price is the anchor and the email is the transaction.**

## What transfers, and what doesn't

**Operator-driven:** Levels runs an unusually low cost base (`[anecdote]`, levels.io: "my sites get 4 billion requests a year but I only pay $244/mo to host them on my own VPS"), which makes a $10 effective price viable in a way it would not be for a company with staff.

**Genuinely universal, and this is one of the most directly copyable pages in the corpus for a small product:**

1. **A one-off ladder is viable, and 2× is the right spread.** $9.99 Lite / $19.99 Lifetime is the easiest arithmetic a buyer can do, and the cheap rung's job is to make the expensive one obvious rather than to be good value. (Compare the same logic applied to *paid trials* in `photoai.md` at 7.8× and `midjourney.md` at 1.5×.)
2. **Fence the free tier on breadth, not volume.** Showing the shape of the data and withholding the depth avoids every question a meter creates — rollover, expiry, top-ups, unused-balance resentment.
3. **Make the free tier a lead-capture instrument.** An email for a 50%-off code sets the real entry price at half the sticker, keeps the anchor high, and gives you a channel. **The listed price is positioning; the emailed price is the transaction.**
4. **Say "billed once, then never again" if it is true.** Two independent products in this corpus decided that *not being a subscription* was a headline feature. In a market the buyer has learned to distrust, it is.
5. **Attach a short money-back guarantee to any zero-marginal-cost one-off.** Seven days is enough, the refund rate is a rounding error, and it removes the last objection on a purchase whose value cannot be judged in advance.
