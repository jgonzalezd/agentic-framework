## Ladder arithmetic

**The one-off ladder: $39 → $119 → $149.** Introductory → Study Plan is **3.05×**; Study Plan → Interview Preparation is **1.25×**.

**Crucially, these rungs are not volume tiers — they are different deliverables.** The research records no duration difference; what changes is **what you walk away with.** "Introductory Call" produces a conversation; **"Study Plan" and "Interview Preparation" produce named artefacts with outcomes attached.** The 3× step from $39 to $119 is paid for **specification**, not for time. **This is the single most transferable pricing structure in the decision-help neighbourhood for any product that produces a deliverable rather than a session.**

**The one-off/recurring crossover, and it does not invert.** Recurring runs "$120–$270/month". Against the one-off rungs:

- At the **low** end of recurring ($120/mo) versus the **entry** one-off ($39/call): **3.08 calls per month** buys the same spend. If a monthly mentorship includes fewer than ~3 introductory-grade calls, repeat one-offs are cheaper.
- At the **high** end of recurring ($270/mo) versus the **mid** one-off ($119/call): **2.27 Study Plans per month** buys the same spend.

**So the arithmetic does not force the upgrade — and MentorCruise does not pretend it does.** The recurring product is not sold as "cheaper per call". It is sold as **a different thing: an ongoing relationship with a specific human.** That is why the ladder holds without an inversion guard.

**Does any add-on invert the ladder?** No add-ons exist.

**Does a lower tier plus add-ons reach a higher tier?** Buying repeat one-offs can certainly exceed a monthly subscription in spend without reaching what the subscription is. **This is the correct shape for a marketplace whose recurring product is qualitatively distinct**, and it is exactly the property `justanswer.md` lacked — there, the recurring product was the *same* answer, so it could only be sold by default rather than by choice.

**Free-first-call arithmetic.** A free call with **every** mentor the buyer meets means the risk reversal scales with search effort rather than being consumed once. Against a $39 entry price, the company gives away **100% of the entry transaction** to establish the relationship — affordable only because the marginal cost falls on the mentor's time, not the platform's.

## What they do that most pages don't

**They sell the one-off honestly and keep an upgrade path.** This is the only researched model in the neighbourhood that does both. `justanswer.md` refuses the one-off and disguises the refusal; `betterhelp.md`, `noom.md`, `rosebud.md` and `abby.md` refuse it cleanly with no one-off SKU at all; `intro-co.md` sells only the one-off and does not try to convert; `talkspace.md` inverts it, making the one-off a *subscriber* benefit. **MentorCruise is the only one that serves a buyer who says "I only need this once" and still has somewhere for them to go.**

**They name the deliverable instead of the session.** "$119/call — Study Plan" tells the buyer what they will hold afterwards. "One 60-minute call" tells them what they will spend.

**They give away the first unit of the thing being sold, not a trial of something adjacent.** A free call is a sample of a call. A 7-day trial of a subscription is a sample of a subscription — a different and weaker promise when what the buyer wants is one answer.

## What transfers, and what doesn't

**Marketplace-driven:** the free first call is affordable because the giveaway lands on the mentor's time rather than the platform's cost base. A single-operator product giving away its first unit pays the full cost itself — though for a zero-marginal-cost digital deliverable that objection largely disappears.

**Genuinely universal, and this is the most directly applicable page in the neighbourhood for a product that produces an artefact:**

1. **Name the deliverable, not the session.** "Study Plan" at $119 versus "Introductory Call" at $39 is a **3× price step bought entirely with specification.** If your product produces something the buyer keeps, the name of that thing is your price ladder. See `one-off-report-market.md` for the same lever applied without any human at all — "over 2,500 words each", "available in a PDF format".
2. **Serve the one-off buyer honestly and put the upgrade beside it, not behind it.** The upgrade should be **offered by satisfaction, not extracted by default.** The two companies that tested this structurally landed on opposite sides of an FTC complaint.
3. **Make the risk reversal a sample of the thing itself.** A free first call beats a free trial of a subscription, because it answers the question the buyer actually has.
4. **If your experts are not famous, quantify the platform instead.** "97% satisfaction rate", "42,000+ Matches made", "4.9 out of 5" — three numbers doing the work one recognisable face does at `intro-co.md`.
5. **Say the cancel promise twice, in the buyer's words.** In a category with a trust problem, repeating it is positioning.

**Calibration point:** **"$39/call" is the verified market floor for a single human answer.** Anything priced above roughly $36–$39 without a human attached is competing against a real person, and — per the cross-cutting one-off analysis — loses that comparison.
