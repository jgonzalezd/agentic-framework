# Layer 02 — Packaging models

**Layer slug:** `packaging` · **Loaded at:** Phase 4

This layer governs the decision that precedes the ladder: **what shape the purchase takes.** Subscription, one-off, credits, hybrid, pure usage, freemium, free-core, lifetime, outcome-based — these are different businesses, not different presentations of the same business, and the tier ladder is built *inside* the model rather than the other way round. The thing that goes wrong most often is choosing the model by analogy ("we're SaaS, so we do a subscription with three tiers") and discovering the cost consequence a year later. For any product with real marginal cost per use, that consequence is specific and severe: **a flat price does not degrade gradually on heavy users, it inverts** — and the inversion is invisible in aggregate revenue right up until a handful of accounts are consuming the margin of dozens. The second failure is the opposite one: bolting on the full apparatus of credits, rollover, expiry, top-ups and overage at a scale where the operational overhead costs more than the model captures.

Where a model deserves more than a pattern, it gets a full pack in **`references/models/`** — read that pack at Phase 4 rather than expanding these patterns. This layer decides *which* model; the pack says how to run it.

## How to use this layer

Read **P-019 first** — it frames the model choice and the five questions that decide it. Then run **P-021 and P-022 before evaluating any model**, because the COGS floor and the usage-distribution stress test are what disqualify models rather than preferences. They are cheap, they are arithmetic, and skipping them is how flat pricing gets shipped onto a skewed usage distribution.

**P-023 → P-031** are the model patterns themselves, each with its fit and failure conditions. Read the two or three that survive P-022 and ignore the rest. **P-020 is the default answer** for any product with per-use cost and skewed usage, and it carries the layer's best-evidenced effect — read its caveats as carefully as its numbers.

**P-032 → P-036** are the mechanics every metered model needs and every model pack assumes: expiry, rollover, top-up pricing, the degraded lane, and the option of refusing the machinery altogether. **P-037 and P-038 are survival conditions, not optimisations** — the terms fence and the change-control discipline. Both must be written before launch, because both are unenforceable if introduced after the behaviour they govern.

In a **COLD** regime, P-021 and P-022 are the only patterns here you can actually execute with confidence, and they are also the highest-value ones — they need no traffic, only instrumentation. Everything else is a prior. In **HOT**, the distribution's top 5% is the single most decision-relevant dataset in this layer.

## Patterns

### P-019 — Choose the monetization model before the tier ladder, and test it against five questions

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: The model determines what can be fenced, what can be metered, what expansion looks like, and where the margin cliff sits. A tier ladder built before the model is decided is a set of price points in search of a structure, and every subsequent decision inherits the mismatch. The five selection questions exist because most model failures are not economic — they are acceptance, evolution, capability or implementation failures that a purely financial comparison never surfaces.
- **works-when**: You are designing pricing from scratch, adding a new purchase shape (Phase 7), or auditing a product whose ladder arithmetic keeps failing in ways that patching cannot fix.
- **fails-when**: You are repricing an installed base. Then the model is largely fixed by migration cost, and the honest deliverable is a migration plan rather than a model recommendation. Changing model on live customers is the expensive half of every pricing change and belongs in the model pack's *Migrating into and out of it* section.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the selection procedure itself. Ramanujam and Tacke name five models in *Monetizing Innovation* (Wiley, 2016) — **subscription, dynamic pricing, market-based/auction, alternative metric (pay-as-you-go), freemium** — and five selection questions: (1) will target customers *accept* this model; (2) how does it hold up as the market evolves; (3) does it fit our company stage and resources; (4) what is the competitive context; (5) can we actually implement it — measure usage, bill it, explain it. Their second-order claim is the one that matters here: **how you charge matters more than how much**, illustrated by Michelin charging fleets per kilometre driven rather than per tire. **The book is pre-AI (2016);** Ramanujam's own current position adds outcome-based pricing as a distinct sixth model, which is a material update the book does not contain and which is sourced to interviews rather than to data. His accompanying claim that AI products capture a far larger share of the value they create than traditional SaaS carries specific percentages in circulation, and **those percentages are recorded in this layer's Folklore table and must not be quoted**: `04-price-points.md` rules every value-capture percentage untraceable and states that no published study relates transacted prices to measured customer ROI. Market shape as of the most recent surveys: **hybrid 37% (from 25% prior year, n=230, fielded Apr–May 2026)**; flat-fee **37% among companies under $5M ARR**; seat-based **15% (from 21%, n=240+, Apr–May 2025)**, still 29% above $150M ARR; outcome-based **5%** with 25% expecting it by 2028.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(a widely-taught framework with no controlled evidence, plus two annual surveys whose levels do not replicate — see P-020)*
- **sources**: https://review.firstround.com/its-price-before-product-period/ · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026 · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-b2b-monetization · https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/pricing-and-scaling-your-ai-product-madhavan-ramanujam
- **how-to**: Shortlist two or three models against the five questions, in writing, before drawing a single tier. Kill any model that fails question 5 — if you cannot meter it, bill it and explain it this quarter, it is not a candidate this quarter. Then run P-021 and P-022 against the survivors and let the arithmetic choose. Read the surviving model's pack in `references/models/` before designing the ladder.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's shortlist is **subscription-with-allowance**, **hybrid (allowance plus overage)**, and **flat subscription**. Auction and dynamic pricing fail question 1 outright — they read as hostile in a small consumer subscription and Ledgerly has neither liquidity nor supply constraint. Outcome-based fails question 5: "correctly categorised" is not machine-verifiable today (P-016). That leaves three, and P-022 chooses between them.

### P-020 — Default to hybrid: a base fee with an included allowance, then metered overage

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: Hybrid is the only shape that is simultaneously acceptable at every point of a skewed usage distribution. The base fee makes revenue predictable for you and the purchase legible for the buyer; the included allowance makes the median user feel unmetered, which is what they are actually buying; and the overage protects margin against the power tail without a hard wall that converts a paying customer into a support ticket. It is not a compromise between subscription and usage — it is the structure that removes the specific failure mode of each.
- **works-when**: Usage is skewed (it essentially always is) and you cannot yet charge for a verified outcome.
- **fails-when**: Your buyer has zero tolerance for a variable line item — some procurement-gated enterprises genuinely do not — or usage variance is genuinely low, in which case the second dimension is complexity with no purpose. It also fails at very small scale where the billing machinery costs more than the tail it protects against (P-036).
- **observed-effect**: **The arithmetic case is the strongest evidence in this layer.** Same $0.04 cost-to-serve, three package designs, three user profiles (Digital Applied, 2026-06-30): a light user at 100 tasks/mo yields **86.7% GM on flat $30 / 50% on pure usage at $0.08 / 73.3% on hybrid ($15 base + overage)**; a median user at 500 tasks yields **33.3% / 50% / 42.9%**; a power user at 2,500 tasks yields **−233% / 50% / 48.7%**. Hybrid is the only column acceptable at every row. **On adoption, honour the demotion:** Growth Unhinged's 2025 edition (published 2025-06-04, survey fielded Apr–May 2025, n=240+) reports hybrid moving **27% → 41%**; the *same author's* 2026 edition (published 2026-05-13, n=230, fielded Apr–May 2026) reports hybrid at **37%, "up from 25% twelve months prior"** — i.e. it says hybrid was 25% in mid-2025 when the mid-2025 edition said 41%. **The levels are mutually inconsistent because the self-selected sample turns over each year; only the slope replicates, at roughly +12 to +14 percentage points per year, two years running.** Cite the slope. Never cite "41%" as a market fact. Both surveys are self-reported online surveys of a newsletter readership that skews toward pricing-engaged B2B companies, and the author sells pricing advisory.
- **evidence**: `strong` *(for the margin arithmetic, which is reproducible from the inputs) / `medium` (for the adoption trend, where only the slope survives replication)*
- **sources**: https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/ai-unit-economics-pricing-margins-services-2026-framework · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-b2b-monetization · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026 · pack: `references/models/hybrid.md`
- **how-to**: Set the included allowance so that a clear majority of users never reach it — the allowance is a comfort feature, not a revenue feature, and an allowance most people hit is a hard cap with extra steps. `[the "80–90% of users never reach it" figure that circulates as the standard instruction is unsourced: no study, no cohort and no dataset was located for it, so do not prescribe a percentile from it. Size the allowance instead from your own usage distribution — put the allowance between p50 and p90 of measured monthly consumption, state which percentile you chose, and record it as a choice rather than as a benchmark. Until that distribution exists the allowance is a guess and the deliverable should say so.]` Set the overage rate at or slightly above your 2× floor (P-021) so overage is margin-accretive rather than break-even, and above your entry tier's in-plan rate so the ladder cannot invert (P-034). State the overage price on the pricing page, in dollars per named unit, next to the allowance — never in a docs page.
- **example-copy**: "100 receipts a month, then $0.10 each. No surprises: set a monthly cap in Settings."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly is currently subscription-with-hard-cap plus a mispriced top-up, which is hybrid's failure mode rather than hybrid. Converting it properly: Solo $9 including 100 receipts, overage at $0.10 per receipt bought self-serve at the moment of exhaustion, with a user-set monthly spend cap. The overage rate derivation is in P-034; the reason it must not be $0.08 is in the same pattern.

### P-021 — Compute the cost-to-serve floor on real traffic before naming any package

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: For any product with a per-use marginal cost, the package is downstream of the unit economics, and there is a price below which the unit is structurally unprofitable regardless of volume. Computing it takes an afternoon and it disqualifies more package designs than any customer conversation will.
- **works-when**: Always, for any product with a per-use inference, compute, storage or human-review cost.
- **fails-when**: You measure a happy-path prompt in isolation and miss the multi-call reality — **the naive estimate typically understates true cost-to-serve by a wide margin**, producing a floor that is 2–5× too low, because the visible generation is one of several calls. Also fails when you compute the floor *before* pulling the cost levers, which overprices you into a cut you then cannot explain.
- **observed-effect**: The published method and its worked example (Digital Applied, 2026-06-30):

  ```
  Cost per task = (input tokens  × input rate  per MTok ÷ 1,000,000)
                + (output tokens × output rate per MTok ÷ 1,000,000)

  input :  3,000 tokens × $5  / 1,000,000 = $0.015
  output:  1,000 tokens × $25 / 1,000,000 = $0.025
                                cost/task = $0.040
  ```

  **The published default floor is 2× cost-to-serve, which produces a 50% gross margin at every usage level: $0.040 → $0.08 per task.** Measure on real traffic including retries, failed generations you do not charge for, evaluation and guardrail calls, embedding and retrieval calls, and any second-pass critic model. Cost levers to pull *before* setting the floor: **model routing ≈ 5× price spread** between tiers of the same family (e.g. $1/$5 versus $5/$25 per MTok; Red Hat's AI infrastructure team claims up to **70% compute cost reduction** from routing alone); **prompt caching ≈ 90% discount** on cached input reads; **batch/async processing ≈ 50% discount**. Margin context, so you know what you are aiming at: average AI product gross margin **52% in Jan 2026, up from 45% (2025) and 41% (2024)** (ICONIQ Growth, via SaaS Mag 2026-05-15 — sample and methodology not public); LLM-native ~**65%** (Bessemer, 2025); the investor-accepted **50–60%** band originates in a16z's *The New Business of AI* (**2020**) and is still the most-cited number in 2026; median operator **target** is **50%**, with only 12% targeting 80%+ (n=230, 2026-05-13). Monitoring ratio: **IER = AI product revenue ÷ inference cost**, with published thresholds of **8:1 target for AI-infused SaaS** (warning below 5:1) and **4:1 for AI-native** (warning below 3:1); the industry average implied by ICONIQ's ~23% inference share of AI product revenue is about **4.3** — i.e. the average company is below the healthy line.
- **evidence**: `strong` *(the arithmetic is a definition and reproduces exactly; the benchmark bands are `medium` at best — the 50–60% band traces to a 2020 essay and margins are climbing year on year, so the "correct" band is a moving target)*
- **sources**: https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/ai-unit-economics-pricing-margins-services-2026-framework · https://www.thesaascfo.com/how-to-calculate-the-inference-efficiency-ratio/ · https://www.saasmag.com/ai-cogs-saas-gross-margin-compression/
- **how-to**: Instrument token counts per customer-visible action in production and take the **p50 and the p95** — the p95 user decides whether your packaging survives, so price the package against p95 rather than p50. Pull caching first (cheapest single change, and it dominates for any product with a long fixed system prompt), then routing, then batching, and recompute the floor after. Recompute quarterly: model prices fall fast enough that a floor set twelve months ago is meaningfully wrong.
- **example-copy**: n/a (internal)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's cost-to-serve, computed the honest multi-call way. Assume three passes per receipt — extract, categorise, validate — each 2,000 input and 400 output tokens against a frontier model at $5/MTok input and $25/MTok output. *(Token counts are assumed for this worked example; on a real product they are measured on production traffic, never assumed.)*

  ```
  input  per pass : 2,000 × $5  / 1,000,000 = $0.010
  output per pass :   400 × $25 / 1,000,000 = $0.010
  per pass                                  = $0.020
  three passes                              = $0.060 per receipt
  2× floor                                  = $0.120 per receipt
  ```

  Now the levers. Cache the fixed 1,500-token system prompt at ~90% off cached reads and route the extract and validate passes to a cheap model at $1/$5 per MTok:

  ```
  cached input, frontier : 1,500 × $5 /MTok × 0.10 = $0.00075
  variable input, frontier :  500 × $5 /MTok       = $0.00250   → $0.00325
  cheap pass  : (1,500 × $1 ×0.10 + 500 × $1)/MTok = $0.00065
              + output 400 × $5 /MTok              = $0.00200   → $0.00265  × 2 = $0.00530
  frontier pass : $0.00325 + (400 × $25/MTok = $0.01000)        = $0.01325
  total                                                          = $0.01855 ≈ $0.0186 per receipt
  2× floor                                                       = $0.037 per receipt
  ```

  The levers move the floor from **$0.12 to $0.037 per receipt** — a difference that changes which ladder is viable at all. This is why the floor is computed *after* the levers, not before.

### P-022 — Stress-test the package against the usage distribution, because flat pricing inverts

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: The floor tells you whether a *unit* is profitable. It says nothing about whether a *package* survives, because a package prices the median while serving the whole distribution. Under a flat price, margin is a declining function of usage with a zero crossing somewhere in the tail — and past that crossing it does not level off, it goes deeply negative. One power user can destroy the margin contributed by two or three light users, and the aggregate revenue line shows nothing until the ratio shifts.
- **works-when**: Any product with per-use marginal cost, at any scale. The test needs the usage distribution, not traffic.
- **fails-when**: You run it on the mean instead of the distribution. The mean is exactly the statistic that hides this failure; the p95 is the one that reveals it.
- **observed-effect**: The published stress test, at **$0.04 cost-to-serve** (Digital Applied, 2026-06-30):

  | User | Tasks/mo | Cost | Flat $30/mo | Pure usage @ $0.08 | Hybrid: $15 base + overage |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Light | 100 | $4 | **86.7%** GM | 50% GM | 73.3% GM |
  | Median | 500 | $20 | **33.3%** GM | 50% GM | 42.9% GM |
  | Power | 2,500 | $100 | **−233%** GM | 50% GM | 48.7% GM |

  Read the bottom-left cell carefully: **flat pricing does not fail gradually — it inverts.** A single power user at 2,500 tasks returns **−$70** of margin against a light user's **+$26**, so one power user destroys the margin contributed by roughly **2.7** light users. Pure usage is margin-stable but revenue-unpredictable and hostile to buyers. Hybrid is the only column acceptable at every row, which is the arithmetic reason hybrid adoption keeps climbing (P-020). The failure is not hypothetical: from **2026-04-04** Anthropic barred Claude Pro and Max flat plans from powering third-party agent frameworks, with the reported rationale that a single agentic session could consume **$1,000–$5,000 of API cost**.
- **evidence**: `strong` *(the arithmetic reproduces exactly from the stated inputs; the three user profiles are illustrative rather than a measured distribution, so substitute your own)*
- **sources**: https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/ai-unit-economics-pricing-margins-services-2026-framework · https://www.pymnts.com/artificial-intelligence-2/2026/third-party-agents-lose-access-as-anthropic-tightens-claude-usage-rules/ · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026
- **how-to**: Build the same three-by-three grid with your own p10, p50 and p95 usage against each candidate package. Any cell below zero disqualifies that package unless you can name the mechanism that prevents that cell from being reached — an allowance, a rate limit, a degraded lane, or a terms fence. State the mechanism explicitly in the deliverable; "we don't expect many users like that" is not a mechanism.
- **example-copy**: n/a (internal)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's current ladder against the **pre-lever** cost of $0.060/receipt from P-021, at full utilisation:

  | Rung | Price | Allowance | Revenue | Cost at full use | GM |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Free | $0 | 5 | $0 | $0.30 | — (marketing cost) |
  | Solo | $9 | 100 | $9 | $6.00 | **33.3%** |
  | Books | $19 | 400 | $19 | $24.00 | **−26.3%** |
  | Top-up | $4 | 50 | $4 | $3.00 | **25.0%** |

  Books at full utilisation is the −233% cell in miniature: **a Books customer who actually uses their allowance loses Ledgerly money.** Read across the distribution instead of at the cap: a Books user at 50 receipts returns 84.2% GM, at 150 returns 52.6%, at 400 returns −26.3%. The whole tier's profitability depends on customers not using what they bought, which is a business model, but not a good one.

  After the P-021 levers ($0.0186/receipt), the same table reads: Solo **79.3%**, Books **60.8%**, top-up 4.3× cost, free user **$0.093/month**. **The cost levers, not a price increase, are what make Ledgerly's existing ladder viable** — which is the correct sequencing and the finding that goes at the top of the deliverable.

### P-023 — Subscription: sell the relationship, and only when usage is predictable

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: A recurring fee for continued access buys revenue predictability and a relationship deep enough to cross-sell into. Its economics rest on an assumption inherited from zero-marginal-cost software: that the cost of a customer is roughly independent of how much they use the product. Where that assumption holds, subscription is the cheapest model to run and the easiest to explain. Where it does not, subscription is a bet on the usage distribution that you have not priced.
- **works-when**: Usage is predictable and reasonably uniform; you want relationship depth and cross-sell; churn is manageable; and AI or compute is a *feature* inside a product whose value is not the compute.
- **fails-when**: Power users' cost-to-serve exceeds the fee (P-022), or the product resolves a finite problem — a buyer who is done in six weeks and finds themselves in month eleven writes the review that defines you. Also fails as an "unlimited" claim governed by an unpublished fair-use policy: the moment agentic or automated usage arrives, the policy tightens, and the tightening is the news.
- **observed-effect**: Live examples of AI-as-a-feature inside a subscription: Notion at $20/user/mo Business with AI bundled; Slack folded AI into all plans after selling it as a $10/user add-on; Atlassian Rovo bundled with credit limits (all 2026). The add-on-then-bundle pattern is documented across the top-500 tracking: AI add-ons launched at **$4–$10/user** and were later bundled with the core price raised **$2.50–$5/user** (Notion, Slack, Loom — PricingSaaS via Growth Unhinged, Jan 2026, methodology partly paywalled). Prevalence at the small end: **flat-fee 37% among companies under $5M ARR** (n=230, Apr–May 2026). Cadence: **~75% of B2B software companies changed pricing or packaging in the prior year**, across two independent survey waves (n=240 in 2025, n=230 in 2026).
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026 · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-saas-pricing-changes · https://www.productgrowth.blog/p/ai-pricing-credits-vs-seats-vs-outcomes · pack: `references/models/subscription.md`
- **how-to**: Choose a flat subscription only after P-022 shows no negative cell, or after you have named the mechanism that prevents the negative cell. If AI is a feature rather than the product, keep the 80% margin target rather than accepting an AI-native 50–60% band — accepting the compressed band when you are AI-*augmented* is leaving money on the table.
- **example-copy**: "$9 a month, cancel any time."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly is AI-native, not AI-augmented: the categorisation *is* the product. A pure flat subscription is therefore refused, and the refusal is stated with the arithmetic from P-022 rather than as a preference.

### P-024 — Pure usage: margin-stable, revenue-unpredictable, and hostile to consumers

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: Billing exactly what is consumed at a marked-up rate holds gross margin constant at every usage level — the one thing no other model does. The cost is borne entirely on the buyer's side: an open-ended meter is unbudgetable, and unforecastable spend is the most-reported procurement complaint in the category. It also publishes your markup where the underlying rates are public.
- **works-when**: A developer or API audience that already thinks in your unit and prices you against other APIs; or cost variance is so enormous that no allowance is honest.
- **fails-when**: Consumer or prosumer buyers, who cannot forecast a bill and will not sign up for one. Also fails commercially where sophisticated buyers can reverse-engineer your margin off public model rates and negotiate you toward infrastructure cost. And it fails as a *thesis*: pure usage never won. OpenView's data showed usage-based adoption rising 34% → 45% → 61% (2020–2022) and its house thesis was that usage-based companies command premium valuations; TechCrunch's read of the same report noted the public-market growth advantage had narrowed to **4% by Q1 2023**, OpenView subsequently wound down, and the successor data shows the market landing on **hybrid**, not pure usage.
- **observed-effect**: Usage-based adoption **61% (2022), 45% (2021), 34% (2020)**, plus 15% actively testing — private SaaS, respondent count not disclosed in the secondary write-up (OpenView 2nd edition, survey Jul–Aug 2022, via TechCrunch 2023-02-02). Survivors of pure metering in 2026 are API products: OpenAI API, Amazon Bedrock, Anthropic API. Buyer-side evidence: **78% of IT leaders reported surprise AI-related charges in the past year** (Zylo 2026 SaaS Management Index, n=218 IT leaders — Zylo sells SaaS management software). **Companies now offering customers a *choice* of pricing model: 29%, up from 21%** (n=230, Apr–May 2026).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(adoption is documented across two vendor-published survey generations; the "hostile to consumers" conclusion is an inference from the surprise-charge data plus the market's convergence on hybrid)*
- **sources**: https://techcrunch.com/2023/02/02/usage-based-pricing-is-rising-but-not-replacing-other-models/ · https://blog.hubspot.com/website/ai-credits-buyers-guide · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026 · pack: `references/models/usage-based.md`
- **how-to**: If you must meter purely, ship a spend cap and a spend alert as *product features* and say so on the pricing page — for a buyer who has been burned, the cap is a stronger purchase argument than the price. Show a typical annual total per usage profile rather than only a per-unit rate.
- **example-copy**: "Set a monthly spending cap. We'll pause rather than bill past it."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly rejects pure usage: a freelancer will not sign up for an open meter on a bookkeeping chore at $9-scale money, and the model's one advantage — constant margin — is bought at a conversion cost Ledgerly cannot afford at 2,000 monthly visitors.

### P-025 — Credits: a bridge, not a destination — and know the six ways they fail

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: An abstract prepaid unit lets multiple heterogeneous features share one meter and lets you ship before you have chosen a value metric or built outcome telemetry. That is a real and legitimate benefit. But every one of the model's failure modes is a consequence of the *abstraction*: an exchange rate is a thing the buyer must study, a thing you can devalue, and a thing that publishes your cost structure to anyone who bothers to compute it.
- **works-when**: AI genuinely *is* the product; you have three or more features with incompatible natural units; and you need to ship fast without outcome telemetry. Also the honest answer for the ~95% of products that cannot define or verify an outcome and whose alternative is a flat price that inverts.
- **fails-when**: You have one dominant countable action — then a credit is strictly worse than counting the action (P-006 in Layer 01). The six documented failure modes (Software Pricing Partners, 2026-04-16 — **SPP sells the consulting that competes with the "just ship credits" default, so read it as an interested but specific critique**): (1) **credits expose your cost structure**, because they correlate to publicly-priced tokens, and a sophisticated buyer reverse-engineers your markup in an afternoon; (2) **credits become incomprehensible at scale** — *"what starts as '1 credit = $1' evolves into a matrix of exchange rates, consumption rules, product-specific ratios, and exception policies that nobody fully understands"*; (3) **credits defer the pain of paying and then deliver it all at once**, which is a worse emotional shape than metered billing; (4) **credits create an exchange-rate risk you cannot resist exploiting** — a currency you mint and can silently devalue will eventually be devalued, and customers know it; (5) **prepaid credits mask pricing problems**, booking revenue immediately while hiding usage skew inside an aggregate balance until renewal; (6) **credits cap your revenue at infrastructure margins**, training you to accept cost-plus economics as your ceiling.
- **observed-effect**: **Adoption, with the demotion honoured:** the PricingSaaS 500 Index — a census of published pricing pages for a defined 500-company universe — recorded companies offering a credit model going from **35 at end-2024 to 79 during 2025 = +126% YoY** (via Growth Unhinged, 2026-01-07). **That is a small-base growth rate: 79/500 is 16% absolute adoption, and the 126% reads far more dramatic than the level does.** PricingSaaS is a commercial pricing-intelligence vendor, the full dataset is paywalled, the raw company list is not publicly auditable, and the index's inclusion criteria are not published. Named new adopters: Figma, HubSpot, Salesforce, Adobe, Cursor, Lovable. Independent survey figure: **29% use AI credits, 33% plan to within 6–12 months** (n=230, Apr–May 2026). Illustrative exchange rates in the wild: Microsoft Copilot Studio **2 credits per generative answer, 10 for tenant graph grounding**; Gamma **~50 credits per 10-slide deck, 2–40 per AI image, 5–10 per refinement**; HubSpot capacity packs at **1,000 credits/month for $10**, price locked for the contract duration (all 2026). **The counterweight, from a party with the opposite commercial interest:** Metronome sells the usage-billing infrastructure credits require and therefore profits when companies adopt them, yet its own 2025-09-22 analysis calls cost-plus credits *"born of necessity rather than preference,"* a **bridge, not a destination**, quoting its own customers: *"We don't love credits, but we didn't have time to define outcomes. This was the fastest way to ship"* and *"Our finance team likes it. Our customers don't know what a credit does."* **When the vendor selling the shovel says the hole is temporary, believe them.** Salesforce Agentforce is the public reversal: **$2 per conversation** abandoned for flexible credits after customer backlash, now reported to run three concurrent pricing models.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(adoption is measured on a named but unauditable index; the critique is specific, mechanism-led and corroborated by an opposed interest, but is not a controlled comparison)*
- **sources**: https://softwarepricing.com/blog/credit-based-pricing-ai/ · https://metronome.com/blog/the-rise-of-ai-credits-why-cost-plus-credit-models-work-until-they-dont · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-saas-pricing-changes · https://newsletter.pricingsaas.com/p/how-to-use-credit-models-12-examples · pack: `references/models/credits.md`
- **how-to**: Do not adopt credits to solve a metric you have not tried to choose. SPP's summary instruction is the right test: *"find the value metric that credits were invented to avoid choosing."* If you adopt them anyway, adopt them as a stated bridge with a written exit condition — the metric or outcome you intend to convert to, and the telemetry you will build meanwhile.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly refuses credits and the refusal is stated in the deliverable so it does not read as an oversight. Ledgerly has one dominant action, and a credit would trade its single strongest asset — a unit that is simultaneously the buyer's unit of value and a countable thing — for a flexibility it does not need.

### P-026 — If you ship credits, publish the burn table and the translation sentence

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: The dominant objection to credits is that the buyer cannot forecast consumption. A visible "what a credit buys" table converts an abstract currency into a forecastable one, and the accompanying translation into whole outputs answers the question that actually gates the purchase — not "what does this cost" but "is this enough for me".
- **works-when**: Your burn rates are stable enough to publish and few enough to fit in one table.
- **fails-when**: The table runs past roughly eight rows, or the rates change monthly — then publishing it advertises the complexity instead of resolving it, which is failure mode 2 of P-025 rendered visually.
- **observed-effect**: Gamma publishes both halves: the burn table (**~50 credits per 10-slide deck, 2–40 per AI image by quality, 5–10 per refinement**) and the translation (**1,000 credits ≈ 15–20 decks**), with Plus at ~$8/mo annual (≈28% off monthly) for 1,000 credits/month. Microsoft Copilot Studio publishes the rate without the translation (**2 credits per generative answer, 10 for tenant graph grounding**). Both observed 2026. **No conversion test of burn-table publication exists.** The census pattern the failure mode maps to: Perplexity's "4,000 bonus credits" against an undisclosed baseline was rated the weakest unit communication of seven AI pricing pages (fetched 2026-08-12) — precision vocabulary without a frame.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(consistent practice among the pages rated most legible; no measured effect)*
- **sources**: https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gamma-pricing · https://blog.hubspot.com/website/ai-credits-buyers-guide · L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Build the table, then add the sentence that translates the plan's allowance into whole outputs. Put both on the plan card, not in a docs page. If the table cannot be kept under eight rows, that is a signal to reduce the number of billable action types rather than to publish a longer table.
- **example-copy**: "1,000 credits ≈ 15–20 decks" — Gamma, 2026
- **worked-example**: Not applicable — Ledgerly has no credit system. Recorded here as the obligation Ledgerly would incur if it ever adopted one, which is part of the cost of that decision.

### P-027 — Freemium: budget the free tier like a paid channel, with a hard COGS ceiling

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: Classic freemium assumed near-zero marginal cost, so a free user was free. Any product with real per-use cost breaks that assumption: every free interaction is spend. The fix is not to kill free but to *budget* it — treat it as a marketing line with a monthly ceiling — and to gate on **usage intensity** rather than on capability, so the free user experiences the full quality of the product in a small quantity.
- **works-when**: Free users feed a real loop — content, referral, word of mouth, or habit formation that converts later — and you can name which loop.
- **fails-when**: You gate model quality or core capability, so the free experience never delivers the magic and nobody forms the habit. Or you leave the free tier uncapped and it eats the margin. The freemium unit-economics check is a definition, not a finding: `cost-to-serve(free user) ÷ conversion rate < acceptable CAC`. **At $1.00/month cost-to-serve and a 1% conversion rate you are paying $100/month of infrastructure per acquired paid customer.**
- **observed-effect**: Free-to-paid conversion, the best-sampled figures available (ChartMogul × ProductLed × Growth Unhinged, n=200 B2B software products, Jan 2026, defined as *"percentage of leads or free signups that convert to become a paying customer within six months"*): median all products **8%**; freemium good/great **3–5% / 8–12%**; ungated freemium (no account needed to try) **7–9% / 8–12%**; free trial without a card **4–6% / 10–15%**; free trial with a card **25–35% / 50–60%**, observed at **30%** and ">5× ones that don't", with only **20%** of trial products requiring a card. Per 1,000 website visitors: freemium **90 signups → 5.0 customers**; ungated freemium 70 → 5.6; free trial no card 45 → 3.6; **free trial card-required 35 → 10.5**. **These bands conflict with an earlier, larger cut** (n=1,000+, Aug 2023: free trial good/great **8–12% / 15–25%**, freemium **3–5% / 6–8%**) — the definitions differ and the canon never reconciles them publicly. Do not average them. Conversion benchmarks are contested further down-market: RevenueCat's dataset puts median freemium conversion at **2.1%** with hard paywalls at **10.7%** — **but RevenueCat measures consumer mobile in-app purchases, not web self-serve checkout, and the two differ in buyer, in price point and in the store that takes the payment.** **Use 2–5% for self-serve free→paid as a planning number and treat anything above 10% as requiring a stated definition.** State the crossing when you use that band: **its lower bound is set by app-store data imported into a web context, which is the transfer this library refuses elsewhere, so hold 2–5% as a prior and replace it with your own measured rate as soon as one exists.** Prevalence: **54.1% freemium, 38.2% free trial, 22.4% demo-only; median starter price $29, IQR $19–$49**, with price clustering at $19/$25/$29/$49 (SaaS Research Lab, 110 products, March 2026). AI-specific gating guidance: gate **usage intensity** (quotas, speed tiers) and **heavy compute modalities**, not intelligence — *"gating usage intensity was a more powerful monetization lever than gating model intelligence"* (Google AI's Vikas Kansal via Lenny's Newsletter, 2026-05-05, framework not data). **Cohort warning, in two parts. Every sample here except RevenueCat's is B2B software, typically $1M–$20M ARR at $50–$249 monthly ARPU; RevenueCat's is consumer mobile in-app purchase. Neither cohort is a web consumer subscription, so a product like Ledgerly sits outside all of them and every band above is directional.**
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the conversion bands are well sampled but mutually inconsistent across waves and cohorts; the COGS-ceiling method is arithmetic and therefore reliable)*
- **sources**: https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/ · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/free-to-paid-conversion-report · https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/freemium-tier-design · https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/why-saas-freemium-playbooks-dont · https://saasresearchlab.com/report/pricing-study · pack: `references/models/freemium.md`
- **how-to**: Compute fully-loaded cost per free user per month and set a monthly ceiling you are willing to spend on free, exactly as you would for a paid channel. Give free users the *full* quality of the product but a small quantity of it, and reserve your most expensive operations for paid tiers. Re-check the ceiling quarterly, because model costs move fast in both directions.
- **example-copy**: "Free forever: 5 receipts a month, same accuracy as every paid plan."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's free tier costs **$0.30/month per free user** at the pre-lever cost and **$0.093/month** after the levers (P-021). At a 3% conversion rate that is **$10.00** and **$3.10** of infrastructure per acquired paid customer respectively — the second is a perfectly acceptable CAC, the first is not obviously so at a $9 price point. The levers are what make Ledgerly's free tier affordable, and that is a packaging finding, not an engineering one.

### P-028 — Decide whether the free tier is a lead magnet or a product, and write it down

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: A free tier as **lead magnet** is a conversion funnel: its KPI is free→paid conversion and its design goal is to deliver the aha moment and then bind. A free tier as **product** is a distribution and defensibility asset: its KPI is reach and word of mouth, and its design goal is that free users stay happy indefinitely. The two demand opposite feature-allocation decisions, and the destructive failure is *drift between them* — a sequence of individually-winning A/B tests migrates the free tier from product to magnet one feature at a time, and the distribution asset is spent without anyone deciding to spend it.
- **works-when**: You can state in one sentence which asset the free tier is buying you — pipeline, or reach.
- **fails-when**: The free tier is unowned and each quarter's growth experiment gets to trim it.
- **observed-effect**: The architecture taxonomy (RevenueCat, fetched 2026-08-12, backed by their State of Subscription Apps dataset — **a consumer mobile-app subscription dataset, so the taxonomy transfers to web but none of its conversion levels do**): **Taster** — same full product, usage-limited, the limit is the ask (Loom's 5-minute recording cap, Zoom's 40-minute cap); **Split** — genuinely different feature sets for different segments, free users are a served segment (CapCut free basic editing versus premium tools); **Hybrid** — taster limits *plus* exclusive premium features (Slack: 90-day history plus Slack Connect). RevenueCat names the drift failure directly — *"slowly shift[ing] more and more of your offering to premium, one experiment at a time,"* where each test wins locally but *"the cumulative effect of chipping away at the free tier"* erodes what made free valuable — and prescribes a written **Bill of Rights** of capabilities that must always be free (Life360's list: the core map, location history, and place alerts). The governing allocation rule: free users should reach *"meaningful progress toward their goal — but not reach the full solution"*; Atticus Li's sharper version is free should deliver *"enough value to drive adoption, not satisfaction."* Detection thresholds for cannibalisation: free→paid **2–5% healthy, below 2% alarm**; free:paid user ratio **~50:1 healthy, above 100:1 too generous**. **The direct experimental evidence: Athenic raised its free-tier task limit from 100 to 500 and free→paid conversions fell 60%** (via Artisan Growth, `[aggregator — underlying source not independently reachable]`) — the cleanest demonstration that in a taster architecture the *limit*, not the feature set, carries conversion, and therefore that the limit is dangerous to loosen and hard to tighten back.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(a well-constructed taxonomy from a source with real subscription data; the one experiment behind the central claim is aggregator-sourced)*
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/freemium-tier-design · https://atticusli.com/blog/posts/freemium-trap-free-tier-cannibalizes-paid-growth/ · https://www.artisangrowthstrategies.com/blog/saas-pricing-experiment-results-real-tests-what-they-changed · pack: `references/models/free-core.md`
- **how-to**: Write one sentence naming the free tier's job and a short list of capabilities permanently off the table for gating, and put both under a named owner. Add a rule that any experiment touching the free tier is evaluated on the free tier's own KPI as well as on conversion. Review the list annually and deliberately, rather than letting it erode weekly.
- **example-copy**: "Always free, whatever else changes: photo capture, category suggestions, and CSV export of anything you've processed."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's free tier is a **taster** and should be declared one: 5 receipts a month at full accuracy, with the limit doing all the conversion work. Its Bill of Rights is the three lines above — in particular CSV export, because withholding a user's own data is the fence that produces the review that ends a small product. The counterfactual worth recording: raising the free limit from 5 to 25 would be the Athenic move, and the deliverable should carry it as an explicitly rejected experiment rather than an unconsidered one.

### P-029 — One-off and lifetime: viable, and the spread should be about 2×

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: A one-off purchase removes the recurring-commitment objection entirely and converts the entire pricing decision into a single yes. It buys conversion with the loss of every expansion mechanism, so it works where the product's value is a *state* the buyer wants to reach rather than a *flow* they consume. A lifetime price is the same trade with an unbounded cost tail attached, which is why it must be justified against a real cost-to-serve rather than against a payback-period fantasy.
- **works-when**: The buyer's need is finite or the value is access to a stock (a database, a directory, a one-time transformation); marginal cost per served user is genuinely near zero; and you can accept that revenue is a function of new customers only.
- **fails-when**: The product has real per-use marginal cost, in which case a lifetime price is an uncapped liability — the P-022 inversion with no renewal to reprice at. It also fails where the buyer expects updates indefinitely, which converts "lifetime" into an unfunded support obligation.
- **observed-effect**: The cleanest live one-off-only ladder is **Nomads.com**, observed 2026-08-12: **Lite Membership $9.99 and Lifetime Membership $19.99**, both *"Billed once. Then never again"*, with a *"7-day money back guarantee"* — a **2× spread**, no subscription option at all, and a free tier fenced on *breadth* rather than volume (limited access to city pages and rankings for 1,500+ cities, plus an emailed **50% off** membership code, which quietly makes the real entry price $5). The directionally interesting datapoint: the same product's lifetime price went **down** over time, from a reported "$100 lifetime cost" (2022, `secondhand`) to the observed $19.99 (2026) — no founder explanation was located, so the observation is recorded without a theory. Related indie datapoints, all self-reported and unaudited: Nomad List "$51K MRR"; Bannerbear "$36K MRR with 450 paying users" (≈$80/user/mo `derived`) after raising entry price from **$9/mo to $49/mo** and narrowing to a niche.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` *(one clean live ladder plus a handful of self-reported founder figures; no controlled comparison of one-off against subscription exists)*
- **sources**: L15 teardowns 2026-08-12 · https://nomads.com/pricing · pack: `references/models/one-off.md` · pack: `references/models/lifetime.md`
- **how-to**: If you run a one-off ladder, use two rungs at roughly a 2× spread — it is the easiest arithmetic a buyer can do, and the cheap rung exists to make the expensive one obvious. Pair it with a free tier fenced on *breadth* rather than volume. Before pricing anything "lifetime", compute the cost of serving one user for five years at your measured cost-to-serve and confirm the price clears it; if it does not, the product is a subscription wearing a one-off label.
- **example-copy**: "Billed once. Then never again." · "7-day money back guarantee." — Nomads.com, 2026-08-12
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly cannot sell lifetime access: at $0.0186 per receipt post-levers, a freelancer processing 100 receipts a month costs **$1.86/month, or $111.60 over five years** — so at the 2× floor a lifetime price would have to clear **$223.20**, and anything below that is a loss with no repricing event. Recorded as a rejected mechanism with the arithmetic shown. A *one-off* Ledgerly is conceivable in a different shape — a flat "process one tax year's shoebox" purchase — and that belongs in Phase 7 as a net-new mechanism rather than as a replacement for the subscription.

### P-030 — Outcome-based: the ceiling, and why almost nobody can reach it yet

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: Charging per verified business result is the tightest possible alignment between price and value and commands the highest prices in the category. It also transfers all execution risk to you, which is why it requires a success rate high enough that failures are a rounding error and a verification layer robust enough to survive dispute.
- **works-when**: The outcome is discrete, attributable to your system, machine-verifiable, and you can absorb cost variance on failed attempts. The stated success-rate criterion is **≥ ~80%**.
- **fails-when**: The outcome is fuzzy, contested or unverifiable; or your success rate is low enough that you eat inference on failures while booking nothing. **What counts as an "outcome" is actively contested and definitions are tightening, which mechanically reduces billable volume on existing contracts.**
- **observed-effect**: Live prices (2026): **Intercom Fin $0.99 per resolution**; "Fin for platforms" **$49/mo including 50 resolutions**, then $0.99 each; **Zendesk ~$1.50 per automated resolution committed, ~$2 pay-as-you-go**, narrowed in a **May 2026** restructure to *Verified* Resolutions confirmed by LLM evaluation; Sierra, Decagon and Ada on custom enterprise contracts $50k–$600k+/yr. Adoption **5% in mid-2025, with 25% expecting it by 2028** (n=240+, Apr–May 2025 — an *intentions* survey, and intention surveys in pricing are historically optimistic). SPP's existence-proof case study: the same product moved from cost-correlated pricing to outcome-based pricing and renewed at **$300,000/year instead of roughly $833/year** — a single unnamed case, to be read as an existence proof rather than a benchmark. **Vendor-interest warning: the richest comparison source is fin.ai, which is Intercom's, and Intercom sells the $0.99/resolution model it benchmarks; the $1M-to-$100M+ ARR growth figure for Fin is Intercom's own claim.**
- **evidence**: `medium` *(several live implementations with published rates; the threshold is one analyst's heuristic, the forecast is an intentions survey, and the strongest revenue case is a single unnamed anecdote from an interested party)*
- **sources**: https://fin.ai/learn/ai-customer-service-agent-pricing-comparison · https://www.productgrowth.blog/p/ai-pricing-credits-vs-seats-vs-outcomes · https://softwarepricing.com/blog/credit-based-pricing-ai/ · pack: `references/models/outcome-based.md`
- **how-to**: Build the verification before the billing — Zendesk's move to *Verified* Resolutions is the tell that the second-generation version of every outcome price adds a verification layer. If you cannot verify today, ship hybrid and instrument the outcome now, so that in twelve months you have the data to define one. Never charge for an interaction that includes failures; that is the Agentforce mistake and it produced a public reversal.
- **example-copy**: "You're charged when it works."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's outcome — a correctly categorised entry — is not verifiable today because correctness is judged by the freelancer. The deliverable states plainly that charging on receipts caps Ledgerly at cost-plus economics, and prescribes logging the post-processing edit signal now so the outcome becomes definable later.

### P-031 — Expire the included allowance; make purchased top-ups permanent

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: Expiring the included allowance protects capacity planning and preserves the subscription's recurring logic — the allowance is a lease that refills, not a balance that accumulates. Making *bought* units permanent removes the risk from the top-up purchase, which is the expansion transaction you most want to encourage. The asymmetry reads as fairness when both halves are stated together, and as a trap when the second half is discovered.
- **works-when**: You have both a subscription allowance and a self-serve top-up.
- **fails-when**: You expire *purchased* units too. That reads as confiscation and is the single most reliable way to make a metered product feel dishonest.
- **observed-effect**: **Midjourney's exact policy (2026): included fast GPU hours expire at the end of every billing cycle with no carryover, for monthly *and* annual subscribers alike, while purchased top-up hours at $4/hr never expire.** Runway runs the same asymmetry in credits: plan credits *"don't roll over — they reset within 24 hours of your billing date"* on Standard/Pro, while *"any additional credits you purchase never expire"* (observed 2026-08-12). Runway adds a second guard — a **minimum top-up purchase of 1,000 credits, exceeding the entire 625-credit monthly allowance of the Standard plan** — so a small customer cannot use top-ups as a permanent cheap tier. The harsher end of the spectrum for contrast: **Workday Flex Credits expire at the end of the contract year**; **PostHog rolls over half of unused credits, but only on a renewal at or above the previous contract's spend** (both via HubSpot's buyer guide, 2026-05-21).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(two independent live products running the identical asymmetry, plus two contrasting contract-scale policies; no measured effect)*
- **sources**: https://www.eesel.ai/blog/midjourney-pricing · L15 teardowns 2026-08-12 · https://blog.hubspot.com/website/ai-credits-buyers-guide
- **how-to**: State both halves of the policy in one sentence on the pricing page, so the asymmetry reads as fairness rather than as a trap discovered later. Draw down the expiring allowance first and the permanent balance second, and show which bucket is being consumed — doing it the other way round is the version customers write angry posts about. Set a minimum top-up size at or above your entry plan's full monthly allowance.
- **example-copy**: "Your monthly receipts reset each cycle. Receipts you buy are yours to keep."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's top-up pack becomes 50 permanent receipts that never expire, drawn down only after the monthly 100 are gone, with the consumed bucket named in the usage meter. The minimum pack is 50, which is half of Solo's allowance — below the Runway guard, and acceptable only because Ledgerly's top-up is repriced above the entry rate in P-034 so it cannot function as a cheap permanent tier.

### P-032 — Cap rollover at about 2× the monthly allowance

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: Some rollover removes the use-it-or-lose-it anxiety that suppresses usage and drives churn. Unlimited rollover lets a customer bank a year of capacity and then dump it, wrecking capacity planning and letting them downgrade while still consuming. 2× is where two independent vendors landed, and it looks like the equilibrium between the two failures.
- **works-when**: Monthly allowances with genuinely variable month-to-month demand.
- **fails-when**: You need hard capacity predictability, or the banked balance would let a customer cancel and keep consuming.
- **observed-effect**: **Two independent vendors converged on the same number.** ElevenLabs: rollover for up to **two months**, capped at two months' worth, on Creator/Pro/Scale/Business only, none on Free/Starter, with the total balance capped at **3× monthly quota** and the explicit clause *"Downgrading or cancelling forfeits unused credits."* Gamma: rollover **capped at 2,000 on a 1,000/month allowance**, and annual Plus does *not* front-load 12,000 credits — it drips 1,000/month. Both observed 2026. They diverge sharply on cancellation: **ElevenLabs forfeits, Gamma honours through period end** — forfeiture creates switching costs, honouring reduces purchase hesitation, and the choice reveals whether you are optimising retention or acquisition. Rollover can also substitute for top-ups entirely: ElevenLabs core sells no top-up at all and uses rollover to solve the same lumpy-usage problem with zero inversion risk. Runway sells rollover as a **top-tier-only feature**, which is the third option: charge for it rather than giving it away.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(independent convergence on the same rule by two vendors, plus a third using it as a paid fence; no measured effect on churn or usage)*
- **sources**: https://flexprice.io/blog/elevenlabs-pricing-breakdown · https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gamma-pricing · L15 teardowns 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Offer rollover on paid tiers only — it is a paid-tier benefit and a free-tier cost. Cap the accrual at 2× the monthly allowance and the total balance at 3×. Decide the cancellation clause deliberately: forfeit if retention is your problem, honour through period end if acquisition is. Say whichever you chose in the same sentence that announces rollover, so it is a disclosed term rather than a discovered one. Then work the example in words — six of them removes every ambiguity in "2×".
- **example-copy**: "Unused receipts roll over for up to two months. Example: a 100-receipt plan can hold up to 200."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly rolls over on Solo and Books only, accruing to 2× the monthly allowance (200 and 800), with the balance surviving to the end of the billing period on cancellation. At 40 paying customers, acquisition dominates retention, so Gamma's clause is chosen over ElevenLabs' — and the choice is recorded as reversible once churn becomes the binding constraint.

### P-033 — Convert the hard cap into a speed tier: build the Relax lane

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: The best mechanism in the AI-pricing lane is not a unit at all — it is what happens when the unit runs out. If exhaustion means *slower* or *cheaper* rather than *stopped*, the buyer surrenders something you value (priority, model choice) and receives unlimited volume in exchange. Your COGS ceiling holds because you control the scarce dimension, and the marginal cost at the margin approaches zero because the overflow runs on spare capacity or a cheap model. Nobody hits a wall, so nobody churns at a wall — and the paid tier's value becomes a thing the free user *feels continuously* rather than reads about.
- **works-when**: You have a materially cheaper serving path — smaller model, queued or batched execution, reduced depth — whose output is still genuinely useful.
- **fails-when**: There is no acceptable degraded output, in which case a clear block with a one-click top-up beats a silent failure. It also fails if you extend the degraded lane to the free tier: the cheapest tier should be the only one with a genuine hard stop, because that is where the upgrade pressure has to live.
- **observed-effect**: **Two companies arrived at this independently.** Midjourney's **Relax mode** — unmetered generation on Standard and above, slow queue — alongside metered fast GPU hours. Cursor's **Auto mode** — unmetered usage if you let them route the model — alongside a metered frontier-model pool. Both observed 2026. The teardown census's verdict: *"If there is one thing to steal from this lane, it is this."* The counter-example is instructive rather than damning: ChatGPT and Claude hard-block at the window, and "hit the limit" is one of the most common user complaints about both — which drives upgrades but also drives multi-tool subscriptions. Cost support for the mechanism: **batch/async processing runs at roughly a 50% discount and model routing at roughly a 5× price spread** (2026), so the degraded lane is genuinely cheap to serve rather than notionally so. **No conversion or churn effect has been published for the pattern.**
- **evidence**: `medium` *(independent convergence by two live products plus a documented cost mechanism; zero measured effect)*
- **sources**: L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12 · https://www.eesel.ai/blog/midjourney-pricing · https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/cursor-ai-pricing/ · https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/ai-unit-economics-pricing-margins-services-2026-framework
- **how-to**: Define the degraded path as a *product state* with its own label and its own quality expectation, not as an error. Name the lane after its trade-off so the constraint is baked into the label. At the moment of transition, show what changed and offer both the top-up and the upgrade with exact prices — this is your highest-converting expansion surface and hard-blocking wastes it. Fence the free tier out of the lane.
- **example-copy**: "You've used your 100 fast receipts. Overnight processing is still unlimited — or add 50 fast receipts for $5."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's Relax lane is overnight batch processing: exhausted Solo and Books users keep uploading, receipts are processed in a nightly batch on the cheap model, and the headline becomes **"100 fast receipts, then unlimited overnight receipts"** — which reads more generous than a competitor's flat 200 while costing less. At roughly a 50% batch discount on the already-routed cheap path, the overnight receipt costs a fraction of $0.0186. Free is fenced out of the lane and keeps its hard stop at 5, because that is where the upgrade pressure has to live.

### P-034 — Peg one flat top-up rate just above the entry tier's in-plan unit price

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: This is the arithmetic device that makes sub-plus-top-up safe. Because in-plan unit price *declines* as you climb, a single flat top-up rate pegged just above the *highest* in-plan rate — which is your cheapest, entry tier — is automatically never cheaper than any tier's marginal unit, so no inversion is possible at any tier, and it becomes progressively more punitive as customers grow, so upgrade pressure scales by itself. You do not need a per-tier overage table. The common mistake is pegging the top-up to your *top* tier's rate to feel generous, which hands entry customers a live arbitrage: they can buy units below their own plan rate.
- **works-when**: You sell a subscription with an allowance and any form of top-up or overage.
- **fails-when**: Your ladder's per-unit price is not monotonically decreasing to begin with — then there is no "highest in-plan rate" to peg to and the ladder needs fixing first (Layer 03's Check 1). Also fails if you publish the top-up rate but hide the in-plan rate, since the buyer cannot then see that the top-up is a premium rather than a penalty.
- **observed-effect**: **The one product in the indie lane that publishes both halves of the arithmetic is ElevenLabs Reception AI** (observed 2026-08-12, assuming 1 credit = 1 phone minute, which the page does not state):

  | Tier | Plan $/min (`derived`) | Flat overage | **Premium** |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Basic | $0.3667 | $0.40/credit | **+9%** |
  | Plus | $0.2750 | $0.40/credit | **+45%** |
  | Premium | $0.1980 | $0.40/credit | **+102%** |

  One flat rate set **~9% above the entry tier's in-plan unit price** lands by construction at roughly **1.5× the mid tier and 2× the top tier**. The independent corroboration at scale: **Zapier prices task overage at 2.5× the base rate on monthly plans and 1.25× on annual** (observed 2026-08-12), where the monthly multiplier does double duty as a term fence. **The second, stricter check** — the overage floor formula from the ladder-arithmetic literature — is `r_overage ≥ (Pₙ₊₁ − Pₙ) / (Qₙ₊₁ − Qₙ)`, the marginal rate of the next rung up; below that, the ladder's own next step is dominated by the top-up. On Zapier's 750→1,500 step: `($39.00 − $19.99) / (1,500 − 750) = $0.0253/task`, so any overage below $0.0253 makes the 1,500 rung unbuyable. **Both checks must run — they catch different failures.**
- **evidence**: `medium` *(one live product publishing both halves, one large corroborating implementation, and a formula that is a definition rather than a finding — but the Reception AI datapoint rests on an inferred credit-to-minute mapping and is currently one observation carrying the rule)*
- **sources**: L15 teardowns and sub-plus-top-up analysis 2026-08-12 · https://zapier.com/pricing · L16 ladder-arithmetic checks 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Compute every rung's in-plan per-unit rate. Take the highest — your entry paid tier — and set one flat top-up rate about 10% above it. Then verify it also clears the next-rung marginal rate. Publish both the in-plan rate and the top-up rate so the premium is visible and reads as the price of flexibility rather than as a fine.
- **example-copy**: "$5 for 50 extra receipts — $0.10 each, against $0.09 on your plan."
- **worked-example**: **Ledgerly's top-up is currently inverted, and this is the finding that outranks everything on the page.**

  ```
  Solo   : $9  / 100 receipts = $0.0900 per receipt   ← entry paid rate
  Books  : $19 / 400 receipts = $0.0475 per receipt
  Top-up : $4  /  50 receipts = $0.0800 per receipt   ← BELOW the Solo rate
  ```

  A Solo customer buys marginal receipts more cheaply than the receipts they already pay for, which makes the Solo allowance worth less than nothing and rewards staying on the cheap rung forever. Note that the top-up *does* clear the second check — the next-rung marginal rate is `($19 − $9) / (400 − 100) = $0.0333/receipt`, so Books is not dominated — which is exactly why both checks are required: one passes while the other fails.

  **The fix:** peg the top-up at ~1.1× the entry rate → **$0.10 per receipt, sold as $5 for 50.** That lands at **+11% against Solo and +111% against Books**, reproducing the Reception AI shape, and it clears the $0.0333 floor by 3×. It also sits comfortably above the post-lever 2× cost floor of $0.037 from P-021.

### P-035 — Reserve "unlimited" for the cheap path, and put it behind a named mode

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: An unlimited *slow* or *cheap-model* lane gives the no-anxiety experience that drives conversion while the expensive path stays metered. Because the user chooses which lane they are in, the constraint is a visible product feature rather than a hidden policy — which is the entire difference between a fair limit and a betrayal.
- **works-when**: You have a materially cheaper serving path whose output is still genuinely useful, and you can name it.
- **fails-when**: You apply "unlimited" to the frontier path and govern it with an unpublished fair-use policy. Then the policy tightens, and the tightening is the news.
- **observed-effect**: Working versions: **Midjourney's Relax mode** (unmetered on Standard and above, slow queue) alongside metered fast GPU hours; **Cursor's Auto mode** (unmetered) alongside a metered frontier pool. The failing version: **Perplexity's "unlimited Pro Search", where the real cap was cut from 600 to 200 per week in early 2026** — an unlimited claim that had a number behind it all along, and the number moved. All observed 2026. The category-wide correlate: discretion clauses are becoming standard — Anthropic's *"Price and plans are subject to change at Anthropic's discretion"* and *"we may limit your usage in other ways … at our discretion"*, OpenAI's *"These limits may vary based on system conditions"* — which is the honest legal correlate of publishing unnumbered limits (census fetched 2026-08-12).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(two working implementations and one documented public failure)*
- **sources**: https://www.eesel.ai/blog/midjourney-pricing · https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/cursor-ai-pricing/ · https://www.finout.io/blog/perplexity-pricing-in-2026 · L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Name the unlimited lane after its trade-off ("Relax", "Standard queue", "Overnight", "Auto") so the constraint is baked into the label. If you must publish a soft cap on an unlimited tier, publish the actual number — a stated 200/week is defensible; "unlimited" that turns out to be 200/week is not.
- **example-copy**: "Unlimited overnight receipts on every paid plan."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's unlimited claim attaches only to the overnight lane and is stated with the trade-off in the same phrase. The word "unlimited" never appears next to "fast receipts".

### P-036 — The safest top-up price is no top-up: right-size the machinery to your scale

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: Every metered mechanism carries permanent operational cost: a rollover policy, an expiry policy, a top-up price, unused-balance resentment, cancellation-forfeiture disputes, and a support surface for all of it. At small scale that cost is a real tax paid by one person, and the marginal revenue it protects may be smaller than the attention it consumes. Refusing the machinery is a legitimate design choice, not a failure of ambition.
- **works-when**: You are one or two people, tiers are few, and the power tail is small enough that a forced upgrade at the cap is acceptable.
- **fails-when**: The cap fires mid-job on a paying customer with no way forward — that is the churn event P-033 exists to prevent, and refusing top-ups without a degraded lane is the worst of both worlds.
- **observed-effect**: **Bannerbear, at $49/mo entry, deliberately sells no overage at all** — verbatim, *"It is not possible to go over your monthly image / video API credits limit"* — and has the simplest pricing page in the indie lane; hitting the cap means upgrading. **TinyWow at $20/mo ($15/mo yearly) runs no credit system whatsoever**, fencing its free tier on ads, CAPTCHAs and queue position while giving away every tool. **Clipdrop uses rolling 24-hour per-tool caps** rather than a monthly pool — a daily cap resets itself, bounds worst-case infrastructure cost per user per day, and generates no balance to argue about. **Fathom Analytics publishes a no-discount policy** — *"Never. Not even for Black Friday"* — removing an entire recurring decision and every discount-hunting support email. All observed 2026-08-12. Two live products **withhold the top-up rate from the public page entirely** (Descript, Runway), keeping the ladder comparison-shoppable while moving the overage conversation to the moment of maximum willingness to pay — **neither rate could be captured, so these are real gaps rather than estimates.**
- **evidence**: `anecdote` *(four indie products, each n=1, no counterfactual for any of them)*
- **sources**: L15 teardowns 2026-08-12 · https://www.bannerbear.com/pricing/
- **how-to**: Before adding any metered mechanism, write down what it costs you to operate for a year — the policy decisions, the edge cases, the support replies — and what you expect it to earn. If you cannot state the second number, do not ship the first. Prefer a rolling daily cap over a monthly pool at very small scale: it resets itself and drags none of the policy questions behind it.
- **example-copy**: "It's not possible to go over your monthly limit." — Bannerbear, 2026-08-12
- **worked-example**: For Ledgerly at 40 paying customers the honest sequencing is: fix the inverted top-up price (P-034) because it is free to fix and actively harmful, ship the overnight lane (P-033) because it removes the wall without any billing machinery, and **defer rollover entirely** until there is evidence that month-to-month variance is actually costing conversions. Rollover is the mechanism with the highest operational cost and the least evidence behind it at this scale.

### P-037 — Fence automated and third-party pass-through in the terms before it eats the flat plan

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: A flat or generously-capped plan priced for human interactive use is arbitrage bait the moment someone can point an automated loop at it. The exposure is unbounded and arrives suddenly rather than gradually, and a restriction written *after* the abuse starts is a takeaway rather than a term.
- **works-when**: You offer any flat or generously-capped tier over an expensive backend. Write it on day one.
- **fails-when**: You write the restriction only after the abuse starts. Then you are removing something a paying customer had, which is a materially worse conversation than declining something they never had.
- **observed-effect**: From **2026-04-04** Anthropic barred Claude Pro and Max flat plans from powering third-party agent frameworks such as OpenClaw, with the reported rationale that **a single agentic session could consume $1,000–$5,000 of API cost**, and multiplied across thousands of power users the flat-rate arithmetic broke; such usage was pushed to the metered API. Cursor and Replit both revisited pricing in 2025 for the same reason. The structural correlate in the census: discretion clauses reserving the right to limit usage are now standard across the category (fetched 2026-08-12).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(one dated, well-documented enforcement action plus two corroborating repricings; no measured effect, because the counterfactual is unobservable)*
- **sources**: https://www.pymnts.com/artificial-intelligence-2/2026/third-party-agents-lose-access-as-anthropic-tightens-claude-usage-rules/ · https://explainx.ai/blog/claude-usage-limits-2026-timeline-explained · L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Write the term on day one: subscription plans cover interactive use by the named account holder; programmatic, automated or resold access goes to a metered path. Then actually build the metered path, so enforcement is a redirection to a supported product rather than a door slammed on a paying customer. Monitor per-account request rate from launch — the top 0.1% of that distribution will tell you about this before your P&L does.
- **example-copy**: "Your plan covers receipts you upload yourself. For bulk or programmatic import, use the API plan."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly writes the term before launch and pairs it with a stub API plan priced per receipt at the top-up rate, so a user who wants to automate has somewhere to go. The monitoring hook is receipts-per-account-per-hour, which at 40 customers is a single query rather than a system.

### P-038 — Put pricing on a schedule, and never change the terms of a balance silently

- **layer**: `packaging`
- **mechanism**: Pricing decays: the product gains capability, costs move, competitors reprice, and the segment shifts — but the price only changes when someone schedules the conversation. Meanwhile, in any model where you mint the unit, you control the exchange rate between the unit and the work, which means every rate change is legible to customers as devaluation. Announced in advance with a grandfather period it is a price change; discovered by a user it is a scandal.
- **works-when**: Always for the cadence. The change-control half applies to any allowance, credit or burn rate.
- **fails-when**: You change prices so often that customers lose the ability to predict their bill — a real risk in credit-based models, and the direct tension nobody in the canon resolves. Frequent repricing and customer trust pull against each other, and the resolution is disclosure rather than frequency.
- **observed-effect**: Cadence: **~75% of B2B software companies changed pricing or packaging in the prior year**, across two independent survey waves (n=240, Apr–May 2025; n=230, Apr–May 2026). Across the top 500 SaaS/AI companies with transparent pricing, **more than 1,800 pricing changes in 2025 = 3.6 per company** (PricingSaaS via Growth Unhinged, Jan 2026, methodology partly paywalled). **Poyar's "pricing used to last 18 months, now it's down to 6" is his advisory opinion from experience with "100+ founders", not a study — do not cite it as data.** Campbell's 2020 version was "experiment with monetization in some manner every quarter". The failure case: **Cursor's June 2025 change removed the request counter from the UI while redefining "unlimited"; users reported all-day capacity shrinking to about an hour; Cursor issued a public apology on 2025-07-04 and refunded unexpected charges from mid-June to early July.** SPP alleges Cursor raised effective per-unit rates **20×+ via credit redenomination in early 2026** and draws the frequent-flyer analogy — **airlines devalue miles roughly 15% annually** — though that specific multiple is contested and SPP is an interested party. **Salesforce Agentforce abandoned $2/conversation for flexible credits after backlash.**
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the cadence behaviour is near-universal across two survey waves and a 500-company census; the specific cadence prescriptions are opinion, and the redenomination multiple is a contested claim from an interested source)*
- **sources**: https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-b2b-monetization · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026 · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/saas-pricing-out-of-date · https://softwarepricing.com/blog/credit-based-pricing-ai/ · https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/cursor-ai-pricing/
- **how-to**: Book a recurring quarterly slot that recomputes cost-to-serve, checks the margin ratio against its threshold, and examines the usage distribution's top 5% — those three numbers will tell you what to change before customers do. Ship one change per cycle so you can attribute the effect. Treat any allowance or burn table as a published price list under change control: announce at least one billing cycle ahead, grandfather existing subscribers, and publish a dated changelog. If a rate must rise because costs rose, say so explicitly — customers accept cost pass-through far better than an unexplained shrinking allowance.
- **example-copy**: "From 1 October, Solo includes 100 receipts a month instead of 120. Everyone signed up before today keeps 120 until 1 January."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's quarterly review recomputes cost per receipt at p50 and p95, checks Books' margin at the p95 usage level, and reads the top 5% of accounts by receipt count. The one change queued for the next cycle is the top-up reprice from $4/50 to $5/50 (P-034), announced one cycle ahead with existing top-up balances honoured at the old rate.

## Interactions

**P-021/P-022 (COGS floor and inversion) versus Layer 01's P-005 (align to value, not cost).** Deliberately in tension and the tension is productive. Layer 01 forbids letting the cost spreadsheet choose the metric; this layer insists the cost spreadsheet chooses the *package*. The resolution is order: metric on value grounds first, then package shape on arithmetic grounds. A product that lets COGS pick the metric ends up selling tokens; a product that ignores COGS when packaging ends up in the −233% cell.

**P-020 (hybrid) versus P-036 (refuse the machinery).** The layer's sharpest internal conflict. Hybrid is the arithmetically correct answer and it costs real operational overhead — a rollover policy, an expiry policy, a top-up price, a dispute surface. At Ledgerly's scale, Bannerbear's refusal is a defensible answer to the same arithmetic, provided the wall is softened by P-033 rather than left hard. The separator is not scale alone but whether you have a cheap serving path: **with a degraded lane, refusing top-ups is safe; without one, refusing top-ups is a churn machine.**

**P-033 (Relax lane) versus P-022 (inversion) versus Layer 03's cap arithmetic.** The degraded lane is the mechanism that lets you name a generous headline without owning the tail's cost, which means it can rescue a package that P-022 would otherwise disqualify — but only if the lane is genuinely cheaper to serve. If your "slow" lane runs the same frontier model on the same hardware, it is a queue with no economics behind it and the inversion is still there, hidden behind a nicer word.

**P-031/P-032 (expiry and rollover) versus P-034 (top-up pricing) versus Layer 03's ladder arithmetic.** These three compose into one system and break together. A permanent purchased balance (P-031) priced below the plan rate (P-034 failing) is not a generous top-up — it is a permanent cheap tier that dominates your own ladder, and rollover (P-032) then lets a customer accumulate it. Run the checks in order: monotonic in-plan rates first, then the top-up peg, then the permanence and rollover policies on top. The reverse order will make all three look individually reasonable while the ladder leaks.

**P-027/P-028 (freemium) versus P-021 (COGS floor).** For a zero-marginal-cost product these are independent decisions; for a per-use-cost product the free tier is a line item in the same budget as the paid tiers, and the free-tier limit is the single most sensitive number in the model. The Athenic result (100 → 500 free tasks, −60% conversion) and the freemium unit-economics check push in the same direction — small free allowances — for entirely different reasons, and it is worth knowing which reason is driving your number.

**P-025 (credits) versus Layer 01's P-006 (named unit) versus P-015 (second metric).** The escape ladder, in cost order: sell the countable noun; add a bounded depth dimension; only then abstract into credits. Most products that adopt credits skipped the middle rung. If you find yourself designing an exchange rate, go back and check whether a second named dimension would have carried the variance.

**P-030 (outcomes) versus everything else in this layer.** Outcome pricing is the ceiling, and every other model in the layer is a way of being paid for activity while you build toward it. That framing matters for Phase 8: the instrumentation that makes an outcome definable later is a decision made *now*, and it is cheap now and expensive to retrofit.

**P-038 (cadence) versus Layer 12 (experimentation) at low traffic.** The quarterly review is the right cadence at any scale, but at COLD scale the review is a *recomputation*, not an experiment — cost-to-serve, margin ratio, top 5% of usage. Presenting it as a test queue at 2,000 monthly visitors produces a plan nobody can run.

## Where this layer is thin

- **No controlled comparison between packaging models exists anywhere in the public record.** Every model recommendation here rests on either arithmetic (which is reliable but only as good as your inputs) or on revealed preference across live pricing pages (which is prevalence, not effect). Nobody has A/B tested subscription against hybrid on the same product, and the reason is obvious — you cannot, without running two businesses.
- **The AI-pricing evidence base is one year old and already self-contradicting.** The hybrid-adoption level, the gross-margin band, the credit-adoption rate and the outcome-pricing forecast are all moving. The 50–60% margin band still traces to a **2020** a16z essay, while measured margins climbed 41% → 45% → 52% across 2024–2026, which means the "correct" band may not be 50–60% in eighteen months. Re-derive rather than quote.
- **Inference deflation versus price stickiness is unresolved.** If cost really falls ~10× a year for equivalent capability, a price set at a 2× markup becomes a 10× markup within a year. **Nobody has published a norm for how much of that to pass through**, and the first mover to pass it through aggressively could reset the category's price points.
- **Agentic usage has no stable pricing answer.** Anthropic's April 2026 ToS fence, Cursor's repricings, Replit's — all are defensive reactions, not designs. The economics of "one user, thousands of automated calls" are still being discovered in production, expensively.
- **The Relax-lane pattern (P-033) — the best mechanism in this layer — has zero published effect data.** Two companies converged on it independently, which is decent evidence it works, and nobody has published a churn or conversion number for it. `[unsourced — would need a published before/after on degraded-lane introduction]`
- **The top-up peg rule (P-034) rests on one observation.** The Reception AI datapoint is strong and its mechanism is clearly general, but it is a single product, and the credit-to-minute mapping it depends on is *inferred* rather than published. Two live products (Descript, Runway) withhold their overage rates entirely, so the corroboration that would raise confidence does not exist publicly.
- **Rollover and expiry policy have no measured effects at all.** The 2× convergence between ElevenLabs and Gamma is the strongest signal available and it is two data points. Whether rollover reduces churn, whether forfeiture-on-cancellation actually creates switching cost, and whether purchased-unit permanence lifts top-up purchase are all unmeasured.
- **Every conversion benchmark in this layer is B2B, except the mobile one.** The B2B samples are n=200, n=230 and n=240 software companies, typically $1M–$20M ARR with $50–$249 monthly ARPU, and the one non-B2B source (RevenueCat) is consumer mobile in-app purchase rather than web. The single direct consumer figure available is one row (**B2C/hybrid free-to-paid, good 5–7% / great 8–12%**, n=200, Jan 2026) from inside a B2B study with an undisclosed number of B2C respondents. A sub-$30/month consumer product should treat all of it as directional.
- **Indie-scale evidence is thin and self-reported.** The one-off, no-top-up and no-credit patterns rest on four to six live products captured on a single day, with founder revenue figures that are unaudited. Several of the most canonical products in that category could not be captured at all — **remove.bg, the single most canonical credit-pack product, failed every fetch**, so its pack sizes, per-image rates and PAYG/subscription split are simply absent from this library.
- **A solo founder at ~2,000 monthly visitors cannot test any of this.** Model changes are not A/B-testable — they change the product. The honest posture is to run P-021 and P-022 (which need no traffic), fix arithmetic failures like P-034 (which are free and unambiguous), ship P-033 (which removes a wall without any billing machinery), and defer every mechanism whose only justification is a benchmark from a cohort you are not in.
