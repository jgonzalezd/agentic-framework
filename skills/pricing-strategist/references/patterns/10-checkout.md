# Layer 10 — Checkout

**Layer slug:** `checkout` · **Loaded at:** Phase 6

This layer governs everything between the moment the buyer clicks a plan and the moment the charge lands: how many screens sit in between, what the form asks for, whether an account is required to pay, which payment instruments are offered, whether the checkout is hosted by the provider or embedded in your page, how currency and tax are displayed, what happens when a card is declined, what the buyer sees after the charge, and where the line sits between a persuasive checkout and an illegal one. The thing that goes wrong most often is not friction. It is **cost surprise** — the single largest published cause of abandonment by a wide margin, and one that is caused by the *timing* of a disclosure rather than by the size of the number. The second most common failure is investing the whole checkout budget in payments infrastructure, which the same data ranks mid-table — 19 points of stated reasons, tied with trust and well behind cost transparency at 52 and friction at 35.

## How to use this layer

Read **P-238 and P-256 first**: the first is the largest fixable abandonment cause in the public record, the second tells you the order to spend effort in and will stop you starting anywhere other than the largest category. Then **P-240 → P-244** are the form and path patterns, which is where most of the remaining recoverable loss sits. **P-246 and P-247** are the infrastructure decisions and are usually one-time choices rather than iterative work. **P-250 is a boundary, not a tactic** — read it before writing any copy that sits near a recurring charge, and read it together with P-237 in `trust-proof`, which is the same boundary seen from the proof side.

Two cohort warnings that apply to the whole layer. **Baymard's corpus is e-commerce checkout, not SaaS self-serve purchase.** Shipping cost, delivery speed and returns policy — three of its top seven abandonment causes — have no SaaS analogue and must not be carried across. What transfers is field count, guest checkout, cost transparency and payment-form security. And **in a COLD regime none of this is testable**: at 2,000 monthly visitors and roughly **5 new paying customers a month**, the published effects here are between one and ten percent and every one of them is far smaller than what that traffic can detect. Note the figure carefully — Ledgerly's 40 paying customers are a stock accumulated since launch, not a monthly conversion count, and treating the stock as the flow overstates the detectable effect by about eightfold. Ship these on mechanism, and instrument completion rate per step as a diagnostic only.

## Patterns

### P-238 — Show the all-in total before the buyer invests any effort

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: A late-revealed cost violates the price the buyer already committed to mentally. The damage is not the money; it is the sense of having been misled after sunk effort, which is why the same dollar amount disclosed early converts and disclosed late abandons. The cost of the surprise scales with how much the buyer has already invested when it arrives.
- **works-when**: universally in transactional checkout. It is the single largest fixable abandonment cause in the published data.
- **fails-when**: nothing in this literature suggests the disclosure itself fails. The real objection is commercial rather than empirical — early cost disclosure suppresses top-of-funnel entry, and that is a genuine trade-off between lead volume and lead quality, not a refutation. It also fails as a *substitute* for fixing the number: 40% of abandoners are objecting to the amount, and a well-timed disclosure of an uncompetitive price still loses the sale.
- **observed-effect**: Baymard's 2025 abandonment survey (n=1,026 US adults, demographically matched to the US adult internet population, abandoners excluding "just browsing"): **extra costs too high (shipping, tax, fees) — 40%**, the top-ranked reason by a wide margin, and **couldn't calculate the total cost up front — 12%**. Combined, the two cost-transparency causes account for more abandonment than every UX-friction cause put together (forced account creation 18%, checkout too long/complicated 17%). Adjacent randomised evidence, much weaker: Zalora surfacing free returns and free shipping prominently on the product page gave **+12.3% checkout rate** (VWO case study, APAC fashion e-commerce, no sample, duration or significance published, accessed Aug 2026).
- **evidence**: `medium` — a large quantitative survey run annually for 14 years within a published methodology (25 qualitative rounds / 4,400+ participant-site sessions; 12 quantitative studies / 20,240 participants; 54 benchmark rounds across 344 sites), with cost transparency holding the top rank consistently. It remains the best-evidenced item in the layer, but every study behind it is Baymard's own, and this library does not grant `strong` to a single organisation however large its corpus. It is also self-report, and it is e-commerce.
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate ; https://baymard.com/research/checkout-usability ; https://vwo.com/success-stories/
- **how-to**: Put a total-cost line in the checkout summary on the **first** screen a price appears on, not on the payment step. For SaaS this translates directly into disclosing per-seat minimums, annual-only billing, onboarding or setup fees, overage rates and any payment surcharge **on the pricing page**. Where a cost genuinely cannot be computed early, show a live estimator rather than silence. If you must charge an extra, name it early and justify it in one line — the abandonment is caused by the timing of the reveal at least as much as by the amount.
- **example-copy**: "$9.00/month. That is the whole price — no setup fee, no per-export charge, and tax is included in the figure shown at checkout."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's Solo tier is $9/mo for 100 receipts with a $5 top-up pack for 50. The cost surprise available here is the top-up: a freelancer who processes 140 receipts in a busy month discovers a second charge they did not price in, so their real bill that month is $9 + $5 = **$14**, not $9. The fix is on the pricing page, not in the checkout — state the overage behaviour beside the tier ("over 100 receipts, we sell 50-receipt packs at $5; we never bill you without asking"), so the total a heavy month costs is knowable before the card is entered. At ~40 customers Ledgerly cannot test this; ship it on the mechanism.

### P-239 — Show the total, not the rate

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: A per-unit or per-period rate is not the price paid, and the buyer knows it. Displaying only the rate defers the arithmetic to the buyer, who either does it wrong, does not do it at all, or discovers the real number at the payment step and feels the reveal as a bait. Showing the all-in total removes an uncertainty that costs more than the number does.
- **works-when**: your displayed price is not the price charged — a monthly-equivalent figure on an annually-billed plan, a nightly rate with fees, a per-seat rate with a minimum, a tax-exclusive figure in a tax-inclusive market.
- **fails-when**: the total is genuinely high and the value case has not yet been made, in which case pushing the larger number earlier removes the chance to build the case first. This is why GoodUI frames the pattern bidirectionally ("less or more visible prices") rather than as a one-way rule, and why the pooled evidence is thin. It also fails when the total is shown *instead of* the rate rather than beside it — the rate is what makes tiers comparable.
- **observed-effect**: GoodUI pattern **#114 "Less Or More Visible Prices", 8 tests** — all medians `[paywalled — the site renders them as the literal string X.X%; verbatim-verified 2026-08-12]`. The free number that matters is the power line: **54.4% of the 90% cumulative power target at 2% MDE from 7 tests** — thin. The one strong real-world signal is a leak rather than a test: **Airbnb A/B tested hidden versus shown price totals and shipped the visible total** (screenshots 2023-03-31, implementation confirmed 2024-01-24), logged by GoodUI as **+0.5 repeatability** for the pattern; the format was later condensed onto a single line. Amazon is separately logged moving price and availability closer to the add-to-cart button.
- **evidence**: `medium` — 8 tests with low pooled power and no obtainable effect sizes, plus one very large-scale shipped implementation whose result is inferred from the rollout rather than published.
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/patterns/114/ ; https://goodui.org/leaks/airbnb-a-b-tests-hidden-vs-shown-price-totals/
- **how-to**: Wherever a rate is displayed, put the charge beside it in the same visual block: "$8/month, billed $96 today" rather than "$8/month" with the annual figure in a footnote. Do not invert it — the monthly-equivalent figure can stay the visual hero, provided the actual charge is legible secondary text directly beneath and not behind a tooltip. Do not spend a test cycle on font size or vertical position; that is precisely the sub-question GoodUI has only half the power it wants on.
- **example-copy**: "$7.50/month — billed once a year at $90. Your card is charged $90 today."
- **worked-example**: If Ledgerly adds an annual option at $90 for the Solo allowance, the card should read "$7.50/mo" as the hero with "billed $90 today, once a year" directly under it in the same block. The failure mode to avoid is the one the pattern's `fails-when` names: at 2,000 monthly visitors, Ledgerly will never be able to distinguish this from the alternative, so the argument for it is honesty and the Airbnb rollout, not a measured lift.

### P-240 — Count the clicks from plan choice to charge, and delete the ones that carry no information

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: Every screen between the decision and the charge is an opportunity to reconsider, to be interrupted, or to hit an error. A screen earns its place only if it collects something you genuinely need or tells the buyer something they genuinely need. Screens that exist because the billing stack was assembled that way carry no information and cost conversions.
- **works-when**: the checkout was assembled incrementally and nobody has ever walked it end to end and counted.
- **fails-when**: the deletion pushes work downstream into a support ticket, or removes a step the buyer needed in order to feel in control. The published wins here are also small, single-company, and almost entirely undocumented — see the evidence rating.
- **observed-effect**: Baymard's benchmark puts the average checkout at **5.1 steps** for a new user (2024). Individual published results, none with a disclosed sample, duration or significance: **PayU** — checkout form asking for mobile **and** email reduced to mobile only, **+5.8% conversions** (VWO, fintech checkout, accessed Aug 2026). **Ubisoft** — a multi-step "For Honor" buy flow with heavy scrolling simplified, conversions **38% → 50%** over roughly 3 months (VWO, gaming B2C). **Findsomeone.com.au** — the separate plan page and checkout page **merged** with fields exposed, **+8% revenue** (GoodUI Datastory #6, Oct 2014; sample and significance paywalled). Survey corroboration: **17%** of abandoners cite a too-long or too-complicated checkout (Baymard, 2025, n=1,026).
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — three single-company case studies in the same direction, none publishing a sample size, a duration or a significance level. The Baymard survey line is `medium` — large and methodologically published, but one organisation's corpus — and in any case it measures a stated reason, not the effect of removing a step.
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields ; https://vwo.com/blog/ab-testing-examples/ ; https://goodui.org/datastories/ ; https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate
- **how-to**: Buy your own product on a phone, on mobile data, with a real card, and count the screens. For each one, write the sentence explaining what it collects or communicates; delete the ones where you cannot write the sentence. Merge plan selection and payment where your billing stack allows it, since the buyer has already chosen. Then instrument completion rate per step so you can see which deletion did the work — this is the one checkout diagnostic that is affordable at low traffic, because it is a funnel count rather than an experiment.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's path is plan card → signup → email confirmation → payment. The email-confirmation screen collects nothing and communicates nothing the buyer needs *at that moment*; it exists because the auth library shipped it. Moving verification to after the charge removes a screen and an inbox round-trip from the purchase path. Ledgerly cannot A/B this at roughly 5 new paying customers a month, but it can count how many people who reach signup ever reach payment, which will tell it whether the screen is losing anyone at all.

### P-241 — Cut the form to about eight visible fields, and count what a new user actually sees

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: Perceived effort at checkout is driven by visible field count more than by anything else. A buyer forms an immediate judgement about how long this will take from the density of what is on screen, and forms it before reading a single label. The number that governs that judgement is what a *new* user sees by default, not what your schema contains.
- **works-when**: any transactional checkout, including SaaS self-serve purchase.
- **fails-when**: regulated purchases with mandatory data capture, or where removing a field simply relocates the work into a support conversation later. It also fails as a promise of a specific gain — Baymard publishes an ideal count and no elasticity curve, so "we will gain x% per field removed" is not something this evidence supports (see Folklore).
- **observed-effect**: Baymard's e-commerce UX benchmark: average visible checkout form fields **11.3 (2024)**, down from **11.8 (2021)** and **12.7 (2019)**; the 2019 study of the 60 largest sites gave 12.8, and the 2016 figure was 14.88 — a decline of roughly 14% every three years. Baymard's published target: **"most sites need only 8 form fields in total"**, and **6–8 for guest checkout including the card fields**. Distribution behind it: of **344** benchmarked sites, **65% score "mediocre or worse", 2% score "good", 0% score "perfect"**, and the average site has **32** distinct available improvements. Baymard's explicit position is that **form fields impact usability more than the number of steps** — splitting a long form across more steps is not equivalent to shortening it.
- **evidence**: `medium` — a benchmark of 344 sites with 7,900+ newly rated checkout UX scores, 30,000+ manually scored checkout elements and a published methodology tracked over a decade, but scored by one organisation against its own rubric with no independent replication of either the count or the 8-field target. Large and careful is not the same as corroborated.
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields ; https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-optimization-from-16-fields-to-8 ; https://baymard.com/research/checkout-usability
- **how-to**: Open your checkout as a logged-out stranger and count the inputs visible by default; that is the number. Delete company name, second address line and anything derivable (city and state from postcode, card brand from the number). Make phone optional and say in one line why you want it. For a SaaS purchase the honest floor is usually email, card number, expiry, CVC and postcode — five. Do not promise a per-field gain; measure your own completion rate per step instead.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's checkout currently asks for name, email, password, company name, country, card number, expiry, CVC and postcode — nine visible fields, above Baymard's target of eight and just under the e-commerce average of 11.3. Company name serves nothing for a freelancer buyer and country is derivable from the postcode plus the card BIN. Deleting both gets Ledgerly to seven. Deferring the password to the post-purchase screen (P-243) gets it to six, which is inside the guest-checkout target range.

### P-242 — Split what is left into semantic steps, and expect a small effect

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: Perceived effort is driven by what is visible at once. A single page of twenty fields reads as a wall; four pages of five fields read as easy, and each completed step invests the buyer further through commitment-consistency. The cost is added navigation, an extra page load per step, and one more place to drop out.
- **works-when**: the form is genuinely long, mobile traffic is significant, and the fields fall into natural semantic groups. It pairs with a progress indicator, which is what makes the multi-step structure legible rather than merely longer.
- **fails-when**: the form is already short — adding steps to a six-field form is pure friction, and this is the common misapplication. It also fails when each step reloads slowly, and it directly contradicts Baymard's position that fields matter more than steps: **splitting a long form does not shorten it**. Do P-241 first, and only reach for this if what remains is still long.
- **observed-effect**: GoodUI pattern **#9 "Multiple Steps", 14 tests**, pooled power **73.4% of the 90% target at 2% MDE**; medians `[paywalled — rendered as X.X%]`. The one fully published instance, **Evidence #66**: a 3-step checkout split into 4 steps (cart and billing details separated) produced **+5.5% sales with a confidence interval of −0.89% to +12%**, at over 1,000 conversions per variation (WiderFunnel client, published 2016-11-11); GoodUI logged it as **+0.25 certainty points**. Also on the pattern page: a donation form converted from single-step to four steps (amount → personal info → billing → occupation) showing **+5% revenue**. Companion pattern **#28 "Easiest Fields First"** carries tests at Aboalarm.de (Sep 2023, 138,384 visitors) and #536 (Jun 2024, 9,626 visitors); **#99 "Progress Bar"** carries five tests between 2,313 and 64,980 visitors.
- **evidence**: `medium` — 14 pooled tests with adequate-ish power but unobtainable effect sizes, and the single published result has a confidence interval that crosses zero. The honest reading of Evidence #66 is that it is compatible with a small loss.
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/patterns/9/ ; https://goodui.org/blog/evidence-66-a-possible-5-5-to-sales-from-an-extra-checkout-step/ ; https://goodui.org/patterns/28/
- **how-to**: Group fields semantically — contact, then billing, then payment — and give each group its own step with a three-to-four-step progress indicator that checks off completed steps and allows backward navigation. Order the steps easiest-first so the first commitment costs nothing. State the +5.5% honestly when you present the change: the interval runs from −0.89% to +12%, which is a plausible improvement rather than a demonstrated one.
- **worked-example**: After P-241, Ledgerly's checkout is six fields. This pattern does not apply — six fields is already one comfortable screen, and splitting it would add a page load between a freelancer and a $9 charge for no reason. Record the decision explicitly so it does not get revisited as "we should try multi-step".

### P-243 — Offer guest checkout, make it impossible to miss, and ask for the password afterwards

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: Forced account creation asks the buyer to commit to a relationship before they have committed to the purchase. There is a second, separate failure on top of the policy one: even sites that offer guest checkout hide it behind or beneath a login form, so the option exists and is not found.
- **works-when**: one-off or first purchases, and any flow where the account carries no immediate user-facing benefit.
- **fails-when**: subscription products where the account genuinely *is* the product and there is nothing to deliver without one. Even there, the sequencing argument survives: collect the password after the charge, on the confirmation screen, where you already hold name and email and the ask collapses to one field.
- **observed-effect**: **18%** of abandoners cite "the site wanted me to create an account" (Baymard, 2025, n=1,026 US adults). Separately, in moderated mobile testing, **60% of test subjects had serious trouble identifying, seeing and selecting the guest-checkout option** — a discoverability failure sitting on top of the policy failure (Baymard, 2013, per-finding n not stated). Note the widely-quoted 26% figure for this cause is a conflation of two different years and two different causes (see Folklore).
- **evidence**: `medium` for the 18% abandonment share — a large quantitative survey with a published method and a matched sample, but Baymard's alone, and one organisation cannot carry `strong`; `medium` also for the 60% discoverability figure, and weaker within that band — moderated testing, thirteen years old, sample not stated.
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate ; https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-checkout
- **how-to**: Give the guest path equal visual weight to the account path — a primary button of its own, not secondary text under a login form. Then move account creation to the confirmation page, where the ask is "set a password" rather than "register". If your product truly requires an account, create it silently from the email on the payment form and let the buyer set a password after they are inside. Quote 18%, not 26%.
- **example-copy**: "Buy now — you'll set a password on the next screen, once your receipts are already uploading."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly needs an account, because the receipts have to live somewhere. The available move is therefore not guest checkout but its sequencing equivalent: take email and card, charge, provision the account, and put the single password field on the confirmation screen next to the first upload control. That removes one field from the purchase path and puts the account ask at the moment the buyer has already decided.

### P-244 — Make the checkout a tunnel

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: Every link on a checkout page is an exit. Global navigation, search bars, app-install banners, category menus and footer link farms all offer the buyer somewhere else to go at the exact moment you want them going nowhere. Removing them turns the checkout into a one-way corridor.
- **works-when**: any flow where the buyer has already chosen and only needs to complete.
- **fails-when**: the buyer still needs to verify something — refund terms, what happens to their data, whether they can cancel — and the link they needed has been deleted. The fix is inline disclosure, not a link and not silence. Stripping the tunnel without adding the inline answers converts an exit into an abandonment.
- **observed-effect**: GoodUI pattern **#64 "Tunnel"** — medians `[paywalled]`, test count not published. The largest logged test: **#615 Online.metro-cc.ru** (2025-10-28, **206,500 visitors**, mobile — search bar and app links removed from checkout, measuring sales); result `[paywalled]`. Adjacent in the same free checkout list: **#624** (Dec 2025, 114,921 visitors — a floating "Confirm Payment" button in disabled and active states tied to terms acceptance, pattern #41 "Sticky Call To Action"). Baymard corroborates the demand side only indirectly: **17%** of abandoners cite a too-long or complicated checkout.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — the pattern's test count is not public and no effect size is obtainable; one very large test is logged with an unreadable result. The mechanism is obvious enough that this is shipped on reasoning, not on data.
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/tests/screen/checkout/ ; https://goodui.org/patterns/
- **how-to**: On the checkout and paywall screens, remove global nav, search, the footer link farm and any app-install prompt, leaving the logo (non-clickable, or returning to the cart), the progress indicator, the form and one support affordance. Replace every policy link with inline expandable text so the information stays reachable without a page exit. Keep the back path to plan selection — removing that is the version of this pattern that backfires.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's payment page currently inherits the marketing site's header, which includes Blog, Pricing and Login. Login is the dangerous one: a buyer who taps it lands in an auth flow with no route back to the purchase. Strip the header to a non-clickable wordmark, put the refund line and the "what happens to my receipt photos" line inline as expandable text, and keep one link back to the plan cards.

### P-245 — Remove or collapse the coupon field, and expect about one percent

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: An open, empty "Discount code" box tells the buyer that a better price exists and that they are not getting it. Some fraction leave the funnel to search for a code and do not come back. Collapsing the field behind a small link removes the exit ramp without removing the capability. The counter-force is that genuine code-holders get frustrated, which is why the effect is small and site-dependent.
- **works-when**: you run few or no public coupon campaigns, your traffic is not affiliate or deal-site driven, and the field currently sits open and prominent on the payment step.
- **fails-when**: coupons are a real part of your acquisition mix, or buyers arrive holding codes from email and partners. GoodUI's own dataset contains a clear loser. It also fails as a priority: this is hygiene, not a lever.
- **observed-effect**: **This is the one GoodUI pattern with fully public meta-analytic numbers** and it functions as their free sample. Repeatability **2.5 evidence for version B — "Likely Better"**. Expected medians for B: **Progression +6% (1 test), Sales +0.8% (9 tests), Revenue +15% (2 tests), ANY PRIMARY +1.2% (10 tests)**. The formal meta-analysis of the nine sales tests: **+0.5% combined effect, p = 0.1335** — positive and **not statistically significant**. Individual spread: test #42 **+6% progression / +2.6% sales**; test #121 **+24% revenue**; test #393 **−1.6% sales ("Likely Worse")**; the recent Obs.no / Obsbygg.no tests #634, #639, #640 (2026, samples 27k–188k) were all **insignificant**.
- **evidence**: `medium` — ten tests with real published numbers and a pooled meta-analysis, which is the most disclosure any pattern in this layer gets, but all ten tests and the meta-analysis are GoodUI's and no independent organisation has pooled them. The conclusion does not move with the rating: this is still the best-documented evidence in the layer for a **small and possibly zero** effect, which is the useful finding.
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/patterns/1/ ; https://goodui.org/tests/screen/checkout/
- **how-to**: If your checkout shows an open coupon input and you do not run coupon campaigns, collapse it to a small text link or delete it. Ship it as free hygiene and do not build a roadmap around it. Do not A/B test it yourself unless you have six-figure monthly checkout traffic, because a 0.8% effect is undetectable below that. Where you do run a promotion, the better move is to pre-apply it and show the discounted price early rather than asking for a code at the end (GoodUI pattern #69, test #167: showing a 50% discount on product pages rather than only at checkout gave **+33% add-to-cart and +24% sales** — a figure confounded with the size of the discount itself and not transferable to a 10% promotion).
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's payment page has an open "Promo code" box left over from a launch-week campaign that ended. Collapse it to a link. Expected gain: roughly a percent of 40 monthly conversions, which is 0.4 customers a month — worth the five minutes and worth nothing else. It is listed here mainly so it does not get proposed as a growth initiative.

### P-246 — Decide hosted versus embedded on inherited trust and maintenance burden, not on branding

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: Two separate things travel with the choice. The first is trust: a provider's hosted page carries security signalling the buyer already recognises, which an unknown brand's own page has to manufacture (see P-223 in `trust-proof`). The second is drift: payment providers ship conversion improvements — new methods, wallets, one-click, network optimisations — into their maintained components, so a frozen custom integration accumulates a widening deficit against them. Branding is the consideration people actually optimise for and the least important of the three.
- **works-when**: self-serve purchase where the checkout is not itself a differentiator — which is nearly every early-stage product.
- **fails-when**: the checkout genuinely is the product experience, or compliance requires bespoke handling. It also fails as a promise of the published number: Stripe's own caveat on the migration result is doing real work, because teams that migrate are teams actively investing in checkout, so the measured gain includes whatever else they changed.
- **observed-effect**: Stripe: businesses migrating from Card Element to Payment Element saw **+10.5% revenue on average** against a matched non-migrating cohort — **two matched groups of 5,000 businesses each**, matched on size, type, industry and geography with pre-migration metrics tracked (2023-04-28). **Link (one-click) added +14% conversion for businesses with a large returning-customer base** (A/B tested, sample not disclosed). Stripe states explicitly that the gains **"may include the impact of other unobserved changes that businesses made at the same time they upgraded their integration."** On the trust side, Baymard's finding across seven years of moderated testing is that the determining factor is the **visual robustness of the payment section** rather than the presence of a badge — a property a hosted page inherits and an embedded field in your own page must be given.
- **evidence**: `medium` — a large matched-cohort quasi-experiment, vendor-run by a party with a commercial interest in the conclusion, with a self-selection confound the vendor itself flags.
- **sources**: https://stripe.com/newsroom/news/payments-revenue-uplift ; https://baymard.com/blog/perceived-security-of-payment-form
- **how-to**: Treat the payment component as a dependency with a version and schedule upgrades against it. Read the 10.5% as an upper bound rather than an expectation. If you are an unknown brand taking a card on your own domain, prefer the hosted page or the provider's maintained embedded element and inherit the security signalling; if you keep the card field in your own page, you now owe it the visual treatment in P-223 because the provider's iframe cannot be restyled. Where you have returning customers, saved-credential or one-click flows are the highest-leverage single addition.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly takes payment through a provider component embedded in its own page. That is defensible — the purchase is $9 and a redirect to a different domain at that price point costs more in confusion than it buys in trust — but it means Ledgerly owes the card field a bordered panel, the accepted-card marks under the button and one line of microcopy, because none of that is inherited. If Ledgerly ever finds itself hand-maintaining the card form rather than using the provider's element, that is the moment to stop and migrate.

### P-247 — Surface the payment methods the buyer's country actually uses

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: A card-only checkout silently excludes buyers whose default instrument is a bank debit, a wallet or a local scheme. The lost conversion is invisible in your analytics because those buyers never start — there is no abandonment event to count, so the loss reads as low traffic quality.
- **works-when**: any geographically distributed customer base, especially outside the US.
- **fails-when**: single-market card-dominant audiences, where extra methods clutter the form for no gain; and where each method adds real settlement, refund or reconciliation overhead that a solo founder will end up doing by hand. It also fails as a first move, though not for the reason this layer used to give: Baymard's ranking puts payments at 19 combined, tied with trust and behind both cost transparency and friction, so it is mid-table rather than last (see P-256).
- **observed-effect**: Stripe: dynamically surfacing at least one relevant additional payment method beyond cards produced on average **+12% revenue and +7.4% conversion**, across **50+ methods × 200 buyer countries = 2M+ method-country pairs**, with a power analysis, an A/A validation test, hash-based randomisation across IP + user agent + merchant + date, and a causal-forest analysis (2023–24). **Business count and duration are not disclosed.** Demand-side corroboration: **9%** of abandoners cite "not enough payment methods" (Baymard, 2025, n=1,026) — the smallest single cause on the list. Ignore the "up to 46%" figures circulating from Stripe marketing pages; they appear in neither primary document (see Folklore).
- **evidence**: `medium` — the study design is the best in this lane and the analysis is genuinely careful, but it is vendor-run with a commercial interest in the conclusion, the sample of businesses and the duration are undisclosed, and the average spans 200 countries whose distribution is not yours.
- **sources**: https://stripe.com/blog/how-we-tested-the-conversion-impact-of-global-payment-methods ; https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate
- **how-to**: Turn on dynamic payment-method surfacing rather than hand-picking a global set, so the method shown matches the buyer's country and the form stays short for everyone else. Then verify on your own traffic before believing the headline. Put the accepted-method marks immediately below the pay button rather than in the footer, capped at four recognisable marks (GoodUI pattern #63; effect sizes paywalled).
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyer is a freelancer, and freelance work is geographically scattered in a way its own analytics will understate. The cheap version of this pattern is to look at where the *free tier* signs up from and compare it against where the paying customers are; a country that produces free signups and no purchases is the signature this pattern predicts. At 40 customers that comparison is noisy, but a country with 60 free users and zero payers is a finding rather than noise.

### P-248 — Show the price in the buyer's currency and state the tax treatment before the payment step

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: A price quoted in a currency the buyer does not hold is not a price; it is a homework assignment, and it comes with an unknown conversion fee attached. The same is true of a tax-exclusive figure shown to a buyer in a market where displayed prices are tax-inclusive by convention. Both are special cases of P-238: a cost the buyer cannot compute at the moment of decision.
- **works-when**: you sell in more than one country, or into any market whose display convention differs from yours.
- **fails-when**: localised prices drift out of alignment with each other and with your intent — store price tiers and FX rounding produce spreads you did not choose. It also fails when localisation is used to hide a price increase, which buyers detect and share.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the display choice itself. Adjacent: **12%** of abandoners cite being unable to calculate the total cost up front (Baymard, 2025, n=1,026). On drift, Adapty's *State of In-App Subscriptions 2026* (16,000 apps, $3B revenue, 500M+ transactions) found up to **4× price variation between countries**, with European prices running **29–39% above North America** — evidence that what you think you charge and what is displayed diverge without anyone deciding it should.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — the Baymard line is strong but measures a different thing, and the Adapty finding is about mobile store price tiers rather than about display convention. No source located measures the conversion effect of currency or tax display.
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate ; https://adapty.io/state-of-in-app-subscriptions/
- **how-to**: Display the charge in the currency the card will be billed in, and say so in one line. State the tax treatment explicitly — "$9.00/month, VAT added at checkout for EU customers" is worse than "€9.00/month including VAT" but far better than silence. Log a `price_shown` event carrying the displayed price, currency, country and store price tier (instrumentation #22 in Layer 11), because retroactively reconstructing what a given buyer actually saw is impossible once the price table changes.
- **example-copy**: "$9.00/month, charged in US dollars. If your card is in another currency your bank will convert it — we don't add a fee."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly prices at $9 and sells to freelancers who may be anywhere. It does not have the volume to justify localised price points, and inventing them at 40 customers would create four price tables to maintain and no learning. The correct move is the honest one-line disclosure above, plus the `price_shown` event so that when Ledgerly does have the volume, the question is answerable from history rather than from scratch.

### P-249 — Treat a checkout decline as a recoverable event, not a lost customer

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: A decline at the moment of purchase is not a decision. A meaningful share are soft — insufficient funds at that moment, a transient network failure, a false positive from a fraud model — and the buyer who sees a bare "payment failed" concludes the product is broken and leaves. The recovery is a matter of what the screen says and whether retrying is one tap.
- **works-when**: always. Declines are a permanent background rate rather than an incident.
- **fails-when**: the card is genuinely dead or the buyer has changed their mind. It also fails when retry logic is implemented aggressively enough to trip the issuer's own fraud heuristics, which makes the next attempt worse rather than better.
- **observed-effect**: **10%** of abandoners cite "credit card declined" (Baymard, 2025, n=1,026) — larger than "not enough payment methods" at 9%. On the recovery side, Stripe's Adaptive Acceptance (false-decline recovery) recovered **$6B of falsely-declined transactions in 2024**, with a **+60% year-over-year improvement in retry success rate**, **70% greater precision** and **35% fewer retry attempts** (Stripe blog, 2025-02-12). Related: **17%** of abandoners cite site errors or crashes — the same rank as checkout complexity, and larger than either payments cause.
- **evidence**: `medium` — the Baymard share is strong; the Stripe recovery figures are vendor-published aggregates over their own network with no independent verification and no denominator that lets you compute a rate.
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate ; https://stripe.com/blog/ai-enhancements-to-adaptive-acceptance
- **how-to**: Write the decline screen as a next action rather than a verdict: name what happened in plain language, keep every field the buyer already filled, and put "try again" and "use a different card" as the two visible buttons. Turn on your provider's adaptive-acceptance or network-level retry features, which are switches rather than projects. Log the decline with its reason code — at checkout the decline is a conversion event, and at renewal the same event is involuntary churn (P-264 in Layer 11), and you want them distinguishable.
- **example-copy**: "Your bank declined that card — this usually means a temporary hold rather than a problem with your account. Your details are still here; try again, or use a different card."
- **worked-example**: Declines are measured against charge *attempts*, not new customers, and Ledgerly attempts roughly 31 charges a month. A 10% decline rate is therefore about three failed payments a month. That is small enough to be invisible in a dashboard and large enough to be a tenth of Ledgerly's monthly billings. The affordable instrumentation is a log line per decline with its reason code; after two months Ledgerly will know whether its declines are one issuer, one country, or noise.

### P-250 — The dark-pattern boundary: the recurring charge must be disclosed where the decision is made

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: A subscription disclosure placed after the decision — in small print between the card fields and the confirm button, or behind a checkbox that names the terms without naming the price — satisfies a lawyer's definition of disclosure and none of a buyer's. Regulators treat the placement and the relative legibility as part of the claim, not as formatting. This is not a persuasion trade-off with an expected value; it is a boundary, and the enforcement record is the evidence.
- **works-when**: never, as a tactic. This pattern exists as a prohibition with an ID so it can be cited in an audit finding.
- **fails-when**: n/a. The rationalisations — "the terms are on the page", "the buyer agreed", "everyone in the category does it", "the trial is genuinely free" — all describe a page that is live and misleading today, and each of them appears in the enforcement record as a defence that did not prevent the filing.
- **observed-effect**: The **FTC's complaint against JustAnswer LLC and its CEO Andrew Kurtzig, filed 2026-01-13 in the Northern District of California**, which quotes JustAnswer's own on-screen copy verbatim. The advertised offer was expert help for **"as little as $1 or $5"**; the actual recurring charge was **"$28–$125" per month** by category (Mechanics "$47/month"; Legal, Oct 2024, "$79/month"; all categories, Nov 2025, "$65/month"). The chatbot copy during signup: *"OK, got it. Here's a secure form to join JustAnswer for $5 (fully-refundable). While you're filling this out, I'll tell the Veterinarian about your situation and then connect you two."* The disclosure, sitting **between the card fields and the button**: *"By clicking 'Confirm now' I agree to the Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, to be charged the one-time join fee, and a $39 monthly membership fee today and each month until I cancel."* The FTC on legibility: *"The material terms were still difficult to see in comparison with the much larger text on the payment form"*, and on the checkbox, the *"omission of any language regarding the price consumers would pay for the subscription"*. The counts are under **ROSCA**: failure to *"clearly and conspicuously disclose the subscription fee"* and failure to *"obtain express informed consent"*. Alleged to have affected **"hundreds of thousands of consumers between January 2022 and the present"**. `[secondary — justanswer.com returned HTTP 403 on every path on 2026-08-12; complaint material obtained via ppc.land, regulatoryoversight.com and allaboutadvertisinglaw.com, all fetched 2026-08-12. **Allegations, not adjudicated findings.**]` One carrier computes the headline arbitrage as **"$5 advertised vs. $130 annual subscription = 26×"** (ppc.land).
- **evidence**: `anecdote` on the regulatory record as it applies here — one enforcement action, reached through named secondary carriers, not adjudicated. The prohibition itself rests on the mechanism and the filing, not on a measured conversion effect. **No source in this corpus measured what hiding a recurring charge does to conversion, and the absence is not evidence of safety.**
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus; secondary carriers ppc.land, regulatoryoversight.com, allaboutadvertisinglaw.com, all fetched 2026-08-12. The governing rule text was not fetched in this corpus — `[unsourced — verify against the current ROSCA requirements and any negative-option rule in force before relying on the legal characterisation]`.
- **how-to**: Put the recurring amount, the billing period and the renewal behaviour **in the same visual block as the price the buyer decided on**, at the same type size as the price, before the card fields — not between the card fields and the button. If the entry price and the recurring price differ, both numbers appear together or neither does. Never let a nominal fee occupy the price slot while the real number arrives in small print. If your checkbox says the buyer agrees to terms, the price must be in the checkbox text. And apply the inverse where it is true: GoodUI pattern **#132 "One Time Payment Copy"** (2 tests, medians paywalled; test #501 Volders.de, 2023-11-02, **89,887 visitors**) exists because "one-time payment, no subscription" stated plainly is worth saying when it is literally true.
- **example-copy**: "$9.00 today, and $9.00 every month until you cancel. Cancel from Settings in one click — we do not ask why."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's temptation is the reverse-trial shape: "first 20 receipts free" with the $9/month starting silently afterwards. That is the JustAnswer structure with smaller numbers, and the smaller numbers are not a defence — the complaint is about disclosure, not about the amount. The compliant and better version puts both numbers in the same block on the plan card ("Free for your first 20 receipts, then $9/month") and repeats the recurring line above the card fields. Ledgerly is also the exact profile the enforcement record targets: an unknown brand, a low nominal entry price and a recurring charge the buyer did not come for.

### P-251 — Make leaving as short as joining

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: The purchase path and the cancellation path are the same commitment seen from two ends, and the asymmetry between them is the most legible signal a product sends about how it regards its customers. A two-click purchase against a support-ticket cancellation tells the buyer, correctly, that the business model depends on people failing to leave. It is also the specific asymmetry that regulators have been converging on.
- **works-when**: any recurring charge.
- **fails-when**: nothing here suggests it fails commercially — but note honestly that the corpus contains **no measurement** of what an easy cancellation path costs or earns. The argument is mechanism, symmetry and regulatory exposure, not a lift.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The nearest evidence is negative rather than positive: the ROSCA counts in P-250 concern consent and disclosure, and the adjacent product observation in the corpus is Talkspace, where deletion required contacting support and *"filling out various forms,"* which the reviewing source judged *"could've been made simpler"* `[secondary, helpguide.org, updated 2026-04-22]`, against BetterHelp's post-settlement **"shred"** button letting users *"permanently remove all conversations"* `[secondary, simplypsychology and healthline, 2026]`. On the retention side, the correct sequencing is that a cancel flow may offer a pause before a discount (P-267) — but the offer must be one screen, and the cancel button must remain visible on it.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — copy and product observation in one lane, no measurement, plus a legal characterisation this corpus did not fetch the rule text for.
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus, observed 2026-08-12; secondary carriers helpguide.org, healthline.com, simplypsychology.com
- **how-to**: Count the clicks from logged-in home to cancelled, and require the number to be no larger than the clicks from pricing page to charged. Put cancellation in Settings, not behind a support email. Allow at most one interception screen — the pause or save offer — with the cancel button visible on it and not disguised as the secondary action. Confirm the cancellation in writing with the date access ends, and keep the account and its data so returning is one tap (P-272 in Layer 11).
- **example-copy**: "Cancelled. You keep everything through 14 March, and your receipts stay where they are. Come back any time — one tap and your old plan is back at the same price."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's cancel path should be Settings → Cancel → one pause offer → confirmed, with an emailed confirmation naming the end date. At 40 customers the entire commercial cost of this is measured in single customers a year, and the alternative is building the exact structure P-250 describes at a smaller scale.

### P-252 — Alarm the payment path, because it fails silently

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: When a checkout breaks, nothing tells you. No error reaches your inbox, no support ticket arrives on day one, and the analytics show a plausible-looking dip. Revenue simply stops, and the median detection route is a customer complaint several days later. The failure mode is worst precisely where change is mandatory — compliance updates, SDK upgrades, provider migrations — because those ship without a test on the grounds that there was no decision to make.
- **works-when**: any change touching the payment path, third-party scripts, or the checkout DOM. Also as standing monitoring, independent of any change.
- **fails-when**: traffic is low enough that an hourly count is mostly zeros — at which point the alarm has to be daily, and the detection window widens correspondingly. That is a real limit at solo-founder scale, not a reason to skip it.
- **observed-effect**: Analytics-Toolkit shipped a mandatory PSD2 / 3D Secure v2 implementation untested; credit-card subscription starts went to approximately **zero for a couple of weeks**. The root cause was an **error-tracking script interfering with the 3D Secure prompt, blocking it from appearing** — that is, the monitoring tool broke the thing it was monitoring. It was detected only after **three customer complaints over several days**. Customer LTV at that business was **$360–$1,680** over two years; the author's conclusion was that "taking the risk of shipping without testing was underestimated and it was not worth it" (2020). Context: **17%** of abandoners cite website errors or crashes (Baymard, 2025, n=1,026) — equal to checkout complexity and larger than either payments cause.
- **evidence**: `medium` — one fully documented incident with a named root cause and a stated business impact, plus a large survey establishing that error-driven abandonment is a first-rank cause in general.
- **sources**: analytics-toolkit.com incident writeup, 2020 (via L09 corpus, accessed Aug 2026) ; https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate
- **how-to**: Put a hard alert on completed payments per unit time with a threshold that fires in hours, not days; at low volume, alert on "zero payments in 24 hours" and accept the coarser window. Treat every third-party script injected into the checkout as a suspect, including your own error tracker and analytics. After any deploy that touches the payment path, complete one real purchase with a real card end to end. Where a mandatory change can be split, run it as a non-inferiority check rather than a straight rollout (P-296 in `experimentation`).
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's charge-event flow follows from its billing mix rather than from its customer count: 30 of the 40 paying customers are billed monthly and 10 annually, so `30 + 10/12 = 30.83` — **roughly 31 charge events a month, about one a day**. That is far too sparse for an hourly alarm, because the expected count in any given hour is close to zero and a zero tells you nothing. The right threshold is "no completed payment in 48 hours": at about one a day, two consecutive empty days is roughly a two-in-a-thousand event if the rail is healthy, so it fires on a real outage and not on a quiet weekend. It would have caught the Analytics-Toolkit failure in two days rather than several. Pair it with a manual real-card purchase after every deploy that touches the checkout. That is one cron job and one habit. Note what the 40 is and is not: it is a *stock* of customers, and the alarm threshold has to be derived from the charge-event *flow* — the distinction P-295 in `experimentation` exists to protect, applied here to billing events rather than to a conversion-rate denominator.

### P-253 — Design the payment step for a screen half-covered by a keyboard

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: During form entry on a phone, the touch keyboard occupies roughly half the screen, so the effective viewport is about half of what the layout was designed against. Everything a buyer needs at the moment of typing — the field label, the running total, the reassurance line, the submit button — must fit in the remaining strip, or it is not there.
- **works-when**: any checkout with meaningful mobile traffic, which is nearly all of them.
- **fails-when**: nothing here fails, but the source is old enough to matter: the measurement is from 2013, before large phones and before modern keyboard behaviour, and the specific percentages should be treated as an order of magnitude rather than a spec.
- **observed-effect**: Baymard's mobile checkout work: the touch keyboard occupies approximately **50% of portrait screen space and 70–80% in landscape**, so the effective viewport during form entry is roughly half the layout's assumption (2013; per-finding sample not stated, drawn from a subset of Baymard's 700+ guideline corpus). Same source, same date: **60%** of mobile test subjects had serious trouble finding the guest-checkout option. Baymard's current benchmark still shows the average checkout at **11.3 visible fields** and **5.1 steps**, which is a lot of screen against half a viewport.
- **evidence**: `medium` — primary moderated research with a published corpus, but thirteen years old and with no per-finding sample published. It is included because nothing has replaced it: mobile checkout layout is one of the genuine voids in this literature.
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-checkout ; https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields
- **how-to**: Test the checkout on a real phone with the keyboard open, not in a desktop emulator with a narrow window. Keep the submit button and the running total in the visible strip while a field is focused — a sticky footer that carries both is the standard solution. Use the correct input modes so the numeric keypad appears for card, expiry and postcode. Never place the reassurance copy directly above the button where the keyboard will cover it.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyer photographs receipts with a phone, so the phone is the primary device and the desktop layout is the secondary one. Its six-field checkout is short enough to fit, but the "encrypted, we never store your card" microcopy currently sits directly above the pay button — exactly the position the keyboard occupies. Move it above the card field, where it will be visible while the card number is being typed, which is also where P-223 says the anxiety fires.

### P-254 — Budget the checkout page's weight like a conversion asset

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: Page speed operates on funnel *progression* at every step rather than on one conversion event, so its effect compounds multiplicatively across a multi-step purchase. Checkout and pricing pages are also the heaviest pages on most sites, because they accumulate comparison tables, chat widgets, A/B testing scripts, consent banners and analytics tags that the marketing pages do not carry.
- **works-when**: mobile traffic, and funnels with several progression steps.
- **fails-when**: as a causal promise. The study measured naturally occurring speed variation rather than a randomised intervention, and it was commissioned by a company that sells speed as a ranking and advertising narrative. Treat it as a prioritisation signal, not a forecast.
- **observed-effect**: Deloitte/55 for Google: **37 EU/US brand sites, 30M+ mobile sessions, 30 days, end-2019 data, with no UX redesigns during the study window and audited analytics.** A **0.1-second** improvement in mobile speed (First Meaningful Paint, Estimated Input Latency, observed load) was associated with: retail **+8.4% conversions and +9.2% average order value**; travel **+10.1% conversions, +10% booking completion, +2.2% checkout completion**; lead generation **+21.6% to form submission and +7% page views**; luxury **+40.1%** on product-detail→add-to-basket. The lead-generation figure is the closest analogue to a SaaS purchase step.
- **evidence**: `medium` — very large sample with genuine controls (no redesigns during the window), but observational rather than randomised, the document does not address correlation versus causation, and the commissioning party has a commercial interest.
- **sources**: https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions
- **how-to**: Measure the checkout and pricing pages' Core Web Vitals separately from the marketing site, because they are different pages with different payloads. Audit what is loading on the payment step specifically and delete anything that is not the form, the provider script and one analytics call — noting from P-252 that the error tracker is itself a suspect. Do not forecast a percentage from these numbers; use them to decide whether the work is worth doing at all.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's pricing page carries a chat widget nobody staffs, a testing script from an experiment that ended, and two analytics tags. None of them are on the payment step's critical path for a $9 purchase. Deleting them is an afternoon and removes three third-party failure modes as well as the weight, which is a better argument for the work than the +21.6% is.

### P-255 — Use the post-purchase screen for the one thing the buyer must do next

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: The confirmation screen is the highest-intent moment the buyer will ever have with the product and it is almost always spent on a receipt. The buyer has just paid, has not yet received value, and will decide within their first session whether this was a good decision. A screen that says "thank you, check your email" spends that moment on nothing.
- **works-when**: always, and especially for products where the value moment requires the buyer to do something (upload, connect, import, invite).
- **fails-when**: the screen is loaded with several asks — account setup, referral link, onboarding tour, app download, newsletter — which converts the highest-intent moment into a queue. Pick one action. It also fails if the confirmation itself is unclear: the receipt and the charged amount still have to be visible.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the post-purchase screen specifically; no source in this corpus measured it. The adjacent evidence is about what belongs there rather than what it earns: Baymard's guest-checkout guidance places **account creation on the confirmation page**, where name and email are already held and the ask collapses to setting a password. In Layer 11, the reactivation research points the same way from the other end — RevenueCat's diagnostic set for lapsed subscribers is built on the return path being **"one-tap reactivation without re-entering payment information"**, which only exists if the account was created properly at purchase.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — assembled from adjacent guidance, with no measurement of the screen itself. Rated honestly rather than borrowed up from Baymard's checkout work.
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-checkout ; https://revenuecat.github.io/codelabs/monetization-success-formula/reactivation/
- **how-to**: Decide the single next action that moves the buyer toward their first value moment and make it the only prominent element on the screen, with the receipt details present but subordinate. If you need a password, this is where you ask for it, as one field. Send the receipt by email as well, so the screen does not have to be the record. Instrument the transition from this screen to the value moment (`value_moment_reached`, instrumentation #12 in Layer 11) — it is the cheapest activation measurement you will ever build.
- **example-copy**: "You're on Solo — 100 receipts a month. Take a photo of the receipt nearest you and we'll have it categorised in about ten seconds. (Receipt for $9.00 sent to your email.)"
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's value moment is one receipt photo turned into a categorised row, and its confirmation screen currently shows a receipt and a "Go to dashboard" button. Replacing that with a live upload control and the sentence above puts the value moment inside the purchase session, which is also the strongest thing Ledgerly can do about the month-1 churn its price band predicts (P-264 in Layer 11).

### P-256 — Spend checkout effort in the order the abandonment data ranks, not in the order vendors sell it

- **layer**: `checkout`
- **mechanism**: The published ranking of abandonment causes and the published volume of vendor content about them do not agree, and the disagreement is at the top of the ranking rather than at the bottom. Cost transparency is the largest fixable category by a wide margin — 52 points of stated reasons — and almost nothing is sold into it, while payments, which has an entire industry selling into it, accounts for 19 and sits mid-table alongside trust. So a checkout roadmap assembled from what you read does not begin with the largest category, which is the expensive part. It does not follow that payments is the smallest thing on the list, and this pattern used to say so wrongly.
- **works-when**: always, at the point of deciding what to do about checkout at all — which is what makes this a sequencing pattern rather than a tactic.
- **fails-when**: your product's distribution genuinely differs from the survey's — for example, a business selling into a country whose dominant payment instrument you do not accept has a payments problem that dwarfs its cost-transparency problem. The ranking is a prior about a population, not a diagnosis of your funnel.
- **observed-effect**: Baymard's 2025 ranked causes (n=1,026 US adults, abandoners excluding "just browsing"): extra costs too high **40%**; delivery too slow 20%; didn't trust the site with card **19%**; site wanted account creation **18%**; checkout too long/complicated **17%**; website errors/crashes **17%**; unsatisfactory returns policy 13%; couldn't calculate total cost upfront **12%**; credit card declined **10%**; not enough payment methods **9%**; unknown 7%. Grouped: **cost transparency 40 + 12 = 52**; **friction 18 + 17 = 35**; **trust 19**; **payments 10 + 9 = 19**; **reliability 17**. The grouped ranking is therefore cost transparency, then friction, then trust and payments tied, then reliability — **payments is mid-table, not last, and the smallest group is reliability at 17.** (These are shares of respondents who could name more than one reason, so the group sums are a rough ordering device rather than an additive decomposition; the ordering is what this pattern uses and the ordering is robust to that.) Global cart abandonment sits at **70.22%**, an average across 50 separate studies (updated 2025-09-22). Baymard's modelled headline — a **35.26%** conversion gain available from better checkout design — is a **ceiling estimate for the average large-sized e-commerce site**, derived from a decade of testing on Walmart, Amazon, Wayfair, Crate & Barrel and ASOS, and is not a measured average lift (see Folklore).
- **evidence**: `medium` for the ranking — a large, demographically matched quantitative survey repeated annually inside a published methodology, but Baymard's alone, with no second organisation publishing a comparable ranked cause list against which the order could be checked. `medium` also, and separately, for the transfer to SaaS, since three of the top seven causes (delivery speed, returns policy, and part of "extra costs") have no SaaS analogue and must be dropped rather than reinterpreted.
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate ; https://baymard.com/research/checkout-usability
- **how-to**: Work the groups in rank order, dropping the causes that have no analogue in your product. For a SaaS self-serve purchase that leaves four groups: **cost transparency at 52** (P-238, P-239, P-248), then **friction at 35** — forced account creation at 18 (P-243) and length and form fields at 17 (P-241, P-242) — then **trust and payments, tied at 19** (P-223 in `trust-proof`; P-247 and P-249), and **reliability at 17** (P-252). Two things follow that the old, mis-grouped version of this pattern got wrong. Payments is not a category you may defer to last on the strength of this ranking: it ties trust. What separates them is cost rather than rank, because the security treatment around the card field is a copy-and-layout change while every additional payment method carries settlement, refund and reconciliation work a solo founder ends up doing by hand — so do the treatment first and treat method breadth as a scheduled decision rather than a skipped one. And reliability, the smallest group, holds the cheapest item on the entire list: the payment alarm in P-252 is one cron job, so run it out of rank order and early.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's instinct is to add payment methods, because that is what the payments blog posts are about. The ranking puts the top-up disclosure first, because it is the cost-transparency item and cost transparency is 52 (P-238). The deferred password follows it as friction at 18 (P-243). The security treatment around the embedded card field does **not** outrank payment-method breadth — trust and payments both sit at 19 — and the reason to do it first is that it costs an hour of layout while method breadth costs a reconciliation habit. Payment-method breadth is worth roughly 9% of abandoners on a base of maybe 60 abandonments a month, which is under six people and some of them would not have paid anyway; that is a reason to schedule it after the copy work, not a reason to call it the smallest problem Ledgerly has.

## Interactions

**P-241 (fewer fields) versus P-242 (more steps).** These are the layer's genuine contradiction and both are kept. Baymard's position, from a 344-site benchmark, is that **fields impact usability more than steps** and that splitting a long form across more screens is not equivalent to shortening it. GoodUI's pooled 14 tests point the other way, and its one published result is **+5.5% with a confidence interval of −0.89% to +12%**. The decider is order of operations: delete first, then split what survives. A four-step checkout containing eleven fields has taken the more expensive advice and skipped the cheaper one.

**P-238 (show the total) versus the commercial objection.** Disclosing costs early genuinely does suppress top-of-funnel entry, and nothing in this literature refutes that. The trade is lead volume against lead quality, and it is the same trade as the credit-card wall on a free trial. Decide it on paying customers per 1,000 visitors, not on conversion rate — because the two options have different denominators and the rate will always favour the gate.

**P-245 (remove the coupon field) versus running promotions at all.** These conflict directly, and the resolution is upstream of the checkout: if discounts are part of your acquisition mix, the field stays and the pattern does not apply. If they are not, the field is a permanent exit ramp advertising a price you are not offering. The pattern's real value is its size — the honest median is under one percent — which is a calibration for the whole layer.

**P-246 (hosted versus embedded) versus P-223 (`trust-proof`, security at the card field).** The choice determines who owes the security signalling. A hosted page inherits it; an embedded provider element in your own page does not, and the field itself is inside an iframe you cannot restyle. If you embed, the treatment goes *around* the field — a bordered panel, the accepted-card marks under the button, one line of microcopy above — and that work is not optional, it is the cost of the embed.

**P-250 (the dark-pattern boundary) versus P-237 (`trust-proof`, the fabrication boundary).** These are one boundary seen from two sides. Overclaiming proof and hiding a recurring charge are the same failure — a factual misstatement made to induce a purchase — at different points on the page, and they compound: a buyer who catches one goes looking for the other. Any audit that finds one should re-check the other before shipping.

**P-250 (disclose the recurring charge) versus `urgency` (layer 08) and the reverse-trial packaging in layer 02.** Every mechanism that defers the moment the buyer registers the real price — a nominal entry fee, a free period that converts silently, a countdown that compresses reading time — moves toward the JustAnswer structure. None of them are illegal in themselves and all of them are aggravating factors in the same complaint. Where a packaging choice requires the recurring price to be less prominent to work, the packaging choice is the thing to change.

**P-251 (easy cancellation) versus P-267 (`expansion-retention`, offer pause before discount).** The retention layer wants an interception in the cancel flow and this layer wants the flow short. The resolution is a hard cap: one screen, one offer, cancel visible on it and styled as a real option. Two interception screens, or a cancel button demoted to a text link, converts a retention tactic into the asymmetry this pattern exists to prevent.

**This whole layer versus `experimentation` (layer 12).** Almost nothing here is testable at low traffic — the published effects run from 0.8% to about 12% and a product converting roughly 5 buyers a month cannot detect any of them. Read P-283 before proposing a checkout test: the affordable instrument at this scale is completion rate per step, which is a funnel count, not an experiment. And note P-292 — a checkout win read at the checkout is not a win; read it at the revenue horizon, because some checkout changes shift plan mix.

## Where this layer is thin

**The whole evidence base is e-commerce, and it is being applied to SaaS without validation.** Baymard's corpus is cart and checkout research on retail sites. Shipping cost, delivery speed and returns policy — three of the top seven abandonment causes, 73 percentage points of stated reasons between them — have no SaaS analogue. What transfers cleanly is field count, guest checkout, cost transparency and payment-form security. Everything else in the ranking should be dropped rather than reinterpreted, and any deliverable that presents Baymard's full ranking as a SaaS diagnostic is overreaching.

**Nobody has published an in-market, large-N checkout experiment on a SaaS self-serve purchase.** The published checkout results in this corpus are single-company case studies without samples, durations or significance levels (PayU, Ubisoft, Zalora, Findsomeone), plus GoodUI's pooled patterns whose effect sizes are behind a paywall. The one fully public meta-analysis — the coupon field — resolves to under one percent at p = 0.1335. That is the honest calibration for the entire layer.

**GoodUI's checkout medians are paywalled.** Patterns #9 (Multiple Steps, 14 tests), #64 (Tunnel), #28 (Easiest Fields First), #63 (Trust Seals, 7 tests), #123 (Single Or Double Column Fields, 2 tests) and #129 (Right Or Left Aligned Forms, 3 tests) all render as the literal string `X.X%` without a subscription. What is visible is the test count, the sample sizes and the statistical-power line — not the direction or the size of the effect. Several of these rest on two or three tests, which is two or three anecdotes with a midpoint rather than a meta-analysis.

**The strongest payments evidence is vendor-run, and the vendor sells the conclusion.** Stripe's payment-method study is the best-designed experiment in this corpus — power analysis, A/A validation, hash-based randomisation, causal forest over 2M+ method-country pairs — and it concludes that you should enable more Stripe payment methods. Business count and duration are undisclosed. Stripe itself flags the self-selection confound in the Payment Element result. None of this is disqualifying and none of it is independent.

**Mobile checkout layout is a genuine void.** Baymard's mobile work is from 2013, before large phones and before modern keyboard and autofill behaviour. Nobody has published what replaced it. Given mobile's traffic share, the fact that the best available guidance on the constraint that governs the payment step is thirteen years old is the largest single gap in this layer.

**The dark-pattern boundary rests on one filing, reached through secondary carriers, and it is not adjudicated.** justanswer.com returned HTTP 403 on every path, so the verbatim copy in P-250 comes from three named carriers of the FTC complaint rather than from the company or the court. The complaint is an allegation. This corpus did not fetch the governing rule text for ROSCA or for any negative-option rule in force, so the legal characterisation should be verified before it is relied on in anything a client acts upon.

**Nothing here addresses invoiced, procurement-led or sales-assisted purchase.** The corpus is self-serve card checkout. A founder selling into companies with purchase orders, security reviews and net-30 terms will find that the binding constraint is a contract artefact rather than a form field, and this layer transfers weakly.

**A solo founder cannot learn any of this from their own data.** At 2,000 monthly visitors and roughly 5 new paying customers a month, every effect in this layer is smaller than the noise by a wide margin. These patterns are shipped on mechanism, on the abandonment ranking, and on the fact that most of them cost an afternoon — not because they were validated. The one measurement that *is* affordable is completion rate per checkout step, and it is a diagnostic, not a test.
