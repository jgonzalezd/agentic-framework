# Layer 11 — Expansion and retention

**Layer slug:** `expansion-retention` · **Loaded at:** Phase 7

This layer governs what happens after the card is charged: whether the price you set survives contact with churn, dunning, upgrades, top-ups, referrals and win-back — and whether the unit economics behind it are knowable at all at your size. The thing that goes wrong most often is not a retention tactic chosen badly. It is **a price being changed to fix a problem that is not a pricing problem**: under $10 a month, roughly a third of churn is a failed payment rather than a decision, and the two have opposite fixes. The second most common failure is arithmetic — quoting an LTV:CAC ratio computed on a few dozen customers, which is not a weak estimate but not an estimate at all.

## How to use this layer

Read **P-261 → P-263 first and in order**. They are the unit-economics gate: they tell you which numbers you are entitled to quote at your size, and everything downstream is worth less if that gate is skipped. Then **P-264 → P-266** are the involuntary-churn set, which is the highest-return work available to a low-priced product and the one most founders do last. **P-267, P-271 → P-274** are the cancel-flow and win-back set. **P-275 → P-277** are expansion mechanics (top-ups, overage pricing, rollover) and only apply to products with a real usage meter. **P-278 and P-279** are referral economics and are **not applicable below roughly 500 users** — the concentration effect they rely on has not formed yet, though the instrumentation is worth having early.

Two rules that govern the whole layer. **Consumer and B2B benchmarks are kept separate throughout and must never be mixed** — a 5%/year B2B logo churn and a 5%/month consumer churn are different universes, and Skok's 3:1 LTV:CAC and 5–7-month payback targets were derived from businesses with 1–2% *monthly* churn and expansion revenue. And **most of the evidence here comes from products with vastly more customers than a solo founder has**: RevenueCat's medians span 115,000 apps, Churnkey's involuntary-churn work covers 25 million subscriptions. Those numbers detect catastrophe and sanity-check a plan. They are not targets and they cannot tell you what will happen to you.

The `## The minimum instrumentation set` section below the patterns is the operative deliverable in a COLD regime. If the product has no analytics, that section is the work, and the patterns are what it makes answerable later.

## Patterns

### P-261 — Count churn events, not customers, before you believe an LTV

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: The precision of a churn estimate scales with the number of observed cancellations `k`, not with headcount and not with elapsed calendar time. For an observed proportion over `N` subscriber-periods the standard error is `sqrt(p(1−p)/N)`, and for small `p` the relative standard error collapses to approximately `1/√k`. Because `LTV ∝ 1/churn`, the relative error in your LTV is the relative error in your churn rate.
- **works-when**: you are deciding whether a CAC is affordable, and you can put a real cancellation count on the table.
- **fails-when**: you sell annual plans and no renewal has come due — then you have zero churn events by construction, and no amount of revenue makes the LTV knowable. It also fails as a reason to do nothing: the answer at low `k` is to switch metric (P-263), not to stop measuring.
- **observed-effect**: At a true monthly churn of 10%, the binomial arithmetic gives: **30 subscriber-months → ~3 churn events → 95% CI on churn of 0%–20.7% → implied lifetime 4.8 months to infinity → implied LTV unbounded above.** **100 subscriber-months → ~10 events → 4.1%–15.9% → LTV spread 3.8×.** **250 → ~25 events → 6.3%–13.7% → spread 2.2×.** **400 → ~40 events → 7.1%–12.9% → spread 1.8×.** **1,000 → ~100 events → 8.1%–11.9% → spread 1.5×.** **4,000 → ~400 events → 9.1%–10.9% → spread 1.2×.** (Author's binomial arithmetic on stated assumptions, not a cited benchmark.) **The thresholds: a ratio carries decision-grade information at roughly 100 observed churn events (±20%, a 1.5× spread); it carries directional information — "are we 3:1 or 0.5:1?" — at about 25 events (2.2× spread); below ~10 events it is decoration.**
- **evidence**: `strong` — this is a statistical identity, not an opinion. The 10%/month input is an assumption; the relationship is not.
- **sources**: http://www.brucehardie.com/papers/021/ ; https://www.evanmiller.org/how-not-to-run-an-ab-test.html
- **how-to**: Before quoting any LTV, write down `k` — the number of subscriptions that have actually ended. If `k < 10`, do not compute an LTV; quote realised revenue per payer to date instead (P-262). At `k ≈ 25` you may state a direction. At `k ≈ 100` you may state a ratio. Recompute `k` every month and let the confidence in your language track it rather than your enthusiasm.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly has ~40 paying customers. At a 10%/month churn rate, 25 churn events requires **250 subscriber-months**, which 40 customers accumulate in roughly **six and a quarter months**. 100 events requires **1,000 subscriber-months** — about **twenty-five months** at the current book, or sooner if the book grows. So Ledgerly reaches "we can state a direction" around month 6 and "we can state a ratio" some time in year three. Anything written before month 6 that contains a colon between CAC and LTV is a guess wearing a metric's clothes.

### P-262 — Report realised value, not projected LTV, until you have twelve months of history

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: Projected LTV requires extrapolating a churn curve you have not observed, which makes it unfalsifiable. Realised LTV — cumulative gross booking per payer at a fixed age — requires only data you already hold, and it can be wrong in a way you will notice.
- **works-when**: always, under twelve months of operating history.
- **fails-when**: you need to justify a CAC that only pays back in year two. Realised LTV will structurally understate you there, and the honest response is to say so explicitly rather than to switch metric mid-argument. It also fails silently if you compare your realised figure against someone else's projected one.
- **observed-effect**: RevenueCat's entire benchmark set is built on **RLTV (realised lifetime value) per payer** rather than projected LTV, precisely because projections cannot be checked. Published medians, **115,000+ apps, $16B+ revenue, 1B+ transactions, published 2026-03-19** — **consumer mobile subscription apps only**: global median **year-1 RLTV per payer $23**. By price band: high-priced **$35.89 month-1 / $62.19 year-1** (top quartile $109.64); mid **$15.78 / $28.75**; low **$6.67 / $10.69**. By geography: North America $17.02 / $26.07; Western Europe $17.89 / $26.64; India and South-East Asia $10.59 / $19.32. By category: Health & Fitness $24.23 / $35.64; Business $18.76 / $35.48; Productivity — / $24.95; Gaming $8.41 / $11.22; AI apps — / $30.16 (41% above non-AI at $21.37). **Consumer value is heavily front-loaded**: Health & Fitness realises $24.23 of its $35.64 year-1 value in month 1, which is 68%.
- **evidence**: `medium` for the medians as a description of that population — very large sample, published method, a metric chosen for falsifiability, but a single vendor's dataset with no second organisation publishing comparable realised-value medians, which is what a `strong` rating would require. **`anecdote` for any transfer to a web product**: every figure is mobile in-app purchase, net of nothing (these are gross bookings; Apple's Small Business Program takes 15% below $1M in prior-year proceeds, 30% above).
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps/ ; https://developer.apple.com/app-store/small-business-program/
- **how-to**: Define exactly two numbers and only two: `RLTV_m1` and `RLTV_y1` — cumulative net revenue per payer at 30 and 365 days after first purchase, by acquisition cohort. Publish those. Once you have twelve months of cohorts, and only then, fit a retention curve. When you compare against the RevenueCat medians, match on price band and category, and state that the cohort is mobile if your product is not.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly is web, Stripe, $9/mo Solo and $19/mo Books, with roughly 3% payment processing and near-zero COGS. Its `RLTV_m1` is close to its price — call it $8.70 net for a Solo customer — and its `RLTV_y1` is simply whatever the cohort has actually paid by day 365, which for the oldest cohort is a number Ledgerly can look up rather than model. The RevenueCat $23 median must **not** be used as Ledgerly's benchmark: it is mobile, it is net of nothing, and its population pays store commissions Ledgerly does not. Quote Ledgerly's own number and say it is an n of forty.

### P-263 — Use payback period as the small-N substitute for LTV:CAC, and never import the B2B target

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: `CAC payback (months) = CAC / (ARPU_monthly × gross margin %)` uses three quantities you measure exactly — CAC if you are spending money, ARPU exactly, gross margin exactly — and zero that you extrapolate. LTV:CAC requires the one quantity you cannot yet measure. That is the whole argument.
- **works-when**: under roughly 100 observed churn events, or any time the churn curve is still unstable — which is most of the first two years.
- **fails-when**: the product has genuine expansion revenue, where payback understates a cohort's value; and in businesses with long deliberate ramps, which a consumer subscription is not. It also fails if you import the benchmark along with the formula.
- **observed-effect**: **B2B SaaS, labelled as such:** Skok's targets are "many of the best SaaS businesses are able to recover their CAC in **5–7 months**", beyond **12 months** profitability becomes "anemic", the ceiling with abundant capital is **18 months**, and "the best SaaS businesses have a **LTV to CAC ratio that is higher than 3, sometimes as high as 7 or 8**." **These were derived from businesses with 1–2% monthly logo churn and expansion revenue.** The consumer translation is arithmetic, not a benchmark: a consumer app at $10/month with a 15% store commission and 10%/month churn has an expected lifetime of ten months, so a six-month payback would consume roughly **60% of lifetime gross profit** on acquisition. Skok & Reiss also name three assumptions in the naive LTV formula that "typically don't hold true in the real world" — constant churn ("churn curves have quite different shapes from one company to another, and frequently improve over time"), linear expansion ("this is highly unlikely to be the case"), and constant gross margin — plus the terminal failure: with very long lifetimes and negative churn, "LTV can become infinite."
- **evidence**: `medium` — the B2B benchmarks are long-standing and widely corroborated within B2B SaaS; the consumer translation is arithmetic on cited inputs rather than an independently measured consumer benchmark.
- **sources**: https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/saas-metrics-2/ ; https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/ltv/ ; https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps/
- **how-to**: Compute `CAC / (monthly price × gross margin)`. On mobile that margin is 0.85 inside Apple's Small Business Program and 0.70 at standard rate; on web it is roughly 0.97 after card processing. For a consumer subscription, target payback inside **one to three billing cycles**, not five to seven months. Publish the payback number, not the LTV, in any document someone will act on — and if a B2B benchmark appears in that document, label the line "B2B" next to it.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly Solo is $9/month on Stripe, so gross profit per month is about **$8.70**. A one-to-three-cycle payback target puts Ledgerly's affordable CAC at roughly **$9 to $26**. That number is knowable today and it is the number to put in front of any channel decision. The LTV:CAC ratio Ledgerly would like to quote instead is not available until roughly month 6 for a direction and year three for a figure (P-261).

### P-264 — Split churn into voluntary and involuntary before you touch the price

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: A cancelled subscription and an expired card produce the same row in your revenue report and require opposite interventions. At low price points they are close to the same size, so a blended churn number is roughly half signal and half payments bug — and the founder reading it concludes the price is wrong.
- **works-when**: always, and most urgently below $10 a month.
- **fails-when**: never, in principle. On mobile you must take the store's classification rather than your own, because you cannot observe the retries — which is a limit on the measurement, not a reason to blend the number.
- **observed-effect**: **Involuntary share of all churn by price band** (Churnkey's analysis of Stripe's 2024 report — $1.4T processed across 200M subscriptions — combined with Churnkey's own 5.4M failed payments across 25M subscriptions over one year): **under $10 — 35%**; $10–$30 — 23%; $30–$100 — 26%; $100–$1,000 — 19%; $1,000–$10,000 — 15%; over $10,000 — 24%. **By segment: B2C overall 24%, B2B overall 16%** — kept separate deliberately. By industry: digital goods 29%, business services 26%, personal services 25%, education 24%, merchandise 23%, SaaS 22%, travel 18%, leisure 17%, insurance 9%. **Platform split** (RevenueCat SOSA 2026 vs 2025): share of cancellations that are billing errors — **Google Play 28.2% → 31%**; **App Store 15.1% → 14%**. Recurly's network, data stated as July 2026, monthly rates: all industries total **3.60%** (voluntary 2.34%, involuntary 1.25%); the **$10–$25 ARPC band runs 4.29% total with 1.30% involuntary**.
- **evidence**: `strong` — two very large independent payment networks agreeing, with the price-band cut published rather than inferred.
- **sources**: https://churnkey.co/blog/involuntary-churn-benchmarks/ ; https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026 ; https://recurly.com/research/churn-rate-benchmarks/
- **how-to**: Put a `churn_type` property on your subscription-expired event on day one (instrumentation #7 below). Report voluntary and involuntary churn as two separate lines in every review, never a blended figure. **Only voluntary churn is evidence about your price.** If you are on Google Play below $10, assume roughly a third of apparent churn is a payments problem until you have measured otherwise.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's Solo tier at $9 sits in the "under $10" band, where **35%** of churn is involuntary. If Ledgerly loses four customers in a month, the prior says roughly one and a half of them never chose to leave. Ledgerly is on Stripe rather than a store, so it **controls the retry schedule** — which makes this the single most recoverable line in its P&L and puts P-265 ahead of any price change on the roadmap.

### P-265 — Fix the payment rail before you touch the price

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: Recovering involuntary churn is close to free incremental revenue, because the customer already decided to pay. Changing the price is a bet with an unknown distribution; recovering a failed payment is a collection with a known one.
- **works-when**: you have any measurable involuntary churn — that is, always.
- **fails-when**: on iOS and Android, where the store owns the retry schedule. Apple runs a **60-day retry window** and only marks a subscription involuntarily churned when it cannot recover within it. Your levers there are in-app grace-period messaging and a short payment-update path, not retry logic.
- **observed-effect**: **Silent retries + a failed-payment wall + dunning together recovered 70% of all detected involuntary churn** (Churnkey, 2024 data, 5.4M failed payments across 25M subscriptions). **Stripe Smart Retries alone recover 51%** of failed payments in an average of **5.5 days**; Churnkey's Precision Retries plus Stripe reach **55% in 4.8 days** (+4pp, 0.7 days faster). **Stripe Adaptive Acceptance recovered $6B of falsely-declined transactions in 2024**, with a **+60% year-over-year retry success rate**, 70% greater precision and 35% fewer retry attempts. **Structured dunning overall makes 60–85% of involuntary churn recoverable** in SaaS (Baremetrics, 148 Recover customers as of Dec 2024, updated 2026-06-02). Platform-specific, **Google Play only**: enabling grace periods gives **+57% recovery of renewal declines**; account holds give **+35% decline recovery and −8% involuntary churn**; the In-App Messaging API gives **2× user recovery**; combined, grace + hold + in-app messaging recovers **~30–35% of failed payments against a ~10–15% platform-default baseline** — roughly a tripling. One named case: **Truecaller recovered 40% of at-risk subscribers, up from a 15% baseline**.
- **evidence**: `strong` for the existence and rough size of the effect — multiple very large payment networks in agreement. **`medium` for any specific number**, since all of them are published by vendors selling recovery products, and the Google Play figures are platform features that **do not exist on Stripe and must never be quoted for a web product**.
- **sources**: https://churnkey.co/blog/involuntary-churn-benchmarks/ ; https://stripe.com/blog/ai-enhancements-to-adaptive-acceptance ; https://baremetrics.com/blog/involuntary-churn ; https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/google-play-billing-error-churn-how-to-fix
- **how-to**: On web, turn on smart or adaptive retries and the card account updater before writing a single dunning email — both are switches, not products, and both are free. On mobile, add an in-app banner during the store's grace and retry window that deep-links to the payment-update screen. Measure `billing_issue_recovered` by `recovery_source` (instrumentation #11) so you can see which lever is carrying the weight rather than assuming.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly is on Stripe, so both switches are available at no cost. Against a book of 40 customers at 10%/month blended churn, roughly four cancellations a month of which about **1.4 are involuntary** (P-264), a 51% silent-retry recovery returns about **0.7 customers a month** — $6.30 of MRR, permanently, for one afternoon. That is a small number and it is a larger number than anything else Ledgerly can do to its retention this month.

### P-266 — Retry silently first; write the emails much later

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: Most declines are soft — insufficient funds at that moment, a transient network failure, a card the network has already updated. Retrying at the right moment resolves them with no customer awareness at all. Emails only reach the residual hard-decline population, which is small and largely inert, and every email spends attention you will want later.
- **works-when**: you control billing, which means web and Stripe.
- **fails-when**: the card is genuinely dead, or the customer intends to leave. No retry schedule fixes intent, and a retry cadence aggressive enough to trip issuer fraud heuristics makes the next attempt worse.
- **observed-effect**: **Stripe Smart Retries alone recover 51% of failed payments** in ~5.5 days. **The three dunning emails add 2.8% + 1.9% + 1.7% = 6.4 percentage points combined** (Churnkey, 2024 data). The highest-performing message in the whole sequence is the one sent **before** anything breaks: a **pre-dunning email 30 days before stored-card expiry gets a 73% open rate and 11% click-through** (Baremetrics, updated 2026-06-02). **This is the opposite of how most founders allocate effort** — they write the emails and never look at the retry schedule.
- **evidence**: `strong` for the shape of the allocation — the 51%-versus-6.4pp contrast comes from one large dataset but is a difference of an order of magnitude, not a marginal call. `medium` for the individual percentages, all vendor-published.
- **sources**: https://churnkey.co/blog/involuntary-churn-benchmarks/ ; https://baremetrics.com/blog/involuntary-churn
- **how-to**: Build in this order and stop when you run out of time: **(1)** enable smart retries; **(2)** enable the card account updater and network tokens; **(3)** send a pre-dunning email 30 days before stored-card expiry; **(4)** only then write the failed-payment sequence, capped at three emails. Do not lead with the email sequence — it is the lowest-yield item and the most visible to customers, which is exactly the wrong combination.
- **example-copy**: "Heads up — the card on your Ledgerly account expires next month. Updating it takes about fifteen seconds and stops your receipts from stopping. [Update card]"
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's founder instinct is to write a three-email dunning sequence, because that is the visible, writable version of the problem. The evidence says those three emails are worth about **6.4 percentage points** of a base that is already ~1.4 customers a month — roughly one customer a year. The two switches above are worth eight times that and take fifteen minutes each.

### P-267 — Offer pause before you offer a discount

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: A meaningful share of cancellations are seasonal or situational rather than verdicts on your price. A pause preserves the subscription record, the payment method and the entitlement history, all of which make returning frictionless. A discount permanently lowers ARPU for a customer who may not have needed one, and it teaches the customer that cancelling produces a better price.
- **works-when**: products with episodic use — fitness, travel, seasonal work, project-based tools, anything with a tax year or a busy season.
- **fails-when**: the customer's problem is that the product does not work. Pause then defers the churn and pollutes your retention numbers with dormant subscriptions that look like customers. It also fails if the pause screen is not obviously escapable — see P-251 in `checkout`.
- **observed-effect**: Recurly (data stated as July 2026): **"38% of consumers prefer pausing over canceling"**, and where pause was offered, **pause usage increased 337%**. Loop, citing Chargebee's 2024 Retention Benchmarks across **1,000+ DTC Shopify subscription brands**: **67%** prefer pausing when both options are shown, with cancellation flows overall saving **20–35%** of would-be churners. ⚠️ **The 38% and 67% figures conflict.** The defensible claim is "a substantial minority to a majority"; the exact number is unsettled, and the two sources measure different populations (Recurly's mixed network versus DTC physical subscriptions).
- **evidence**: `medium` — two independent sources agreeing on direction and disagreeing by nearly a factor of two on magnitude, neither publishing a sample for the preference figure.
- **sources**: https://recurly.com/research/churn-rate-benchmarks/ ; https://www.loopwork.co/blog/why-subscribers-cancel-save-offers-that-work
- **how-to**: Put pause first in the cancel flow, before any discount offer, on a single screen with the cancel button still visible and still styled as a real option. Cap the pause at one to three months with an automatic resume. **Track paused subscriptions as a separate state, never as active** — otherwise you will hide churn from yourself, which is the specific way this pattern turns into a reporting problem.
- **example-copy**: "Slow month? Pause for up to 3 months — your receipts and history stay exactly as they are, and nothing is charged until you come back. [Pause] [Cancel anyway]"
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyer is a freelancer whose bookkeeping load is seasonal, with a hard peak around tax filing and a trough after it. That is the exact profile the pattern is for: a cancellation in May is often "I have nothing to file until next year", not "this is too expensive". A three-month pause with auto-resume converts a permanent loss into a gap. At 40 customers, expect this to move single customers, and report paused separately so those single customers do not quietly inflate the active count.

### P-268 — Sell the annual upgrade at month three or four, not at checkout

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: At checkout the customer has no evidence the product works, so an annual price is a large unbacked bet and reads as a trap. After three successful monthly renewals they have proof, and the annual price reads as a reward for a decision they have already made three times.
- **works-when**: a monthly plan exists, the product has repeated-use value, and you can detect engagement.
- **fails-when**: pre-product-market-fit. Converting a monthly subscriber to annual before you know whether the product retains converts your fastest churn signal into a twelve-month blind spot — you trade the thing you most need to learn for cash you may have to refund. It also fails if the offer fires during a cancel flow or a payment-recovery sequence, where it reads as a company that has noticed you are leaving.
- **observed-effect**: One operator across "dozens of creator apps" reports **8–14% conversion** on an in-app offer at month four, **4–8%** on a day-90 renewal email, and a **10–20% catch rate** on a salvage offer to cancelling annual trialists, at an effective discount of **30–45% versus monthly** — **that last figure is one unnamed operator's report of what they charge, not a sizing recommendation, and it sits well outside the sourced 16–25% web band in P-093; size from P-093, never from this line.** Churnkey reports **+189 days average lifetime and +$146 per subscriber** for engagement-triggered monthly→annual conversion across **50,000+ matched subscriber pairs** — a within-population matched design, which is the best available evidence in this corpus that the move has a real effect rather than re-labelling subscribers who would have stayed anyway. Vendor-reported, not independently replicated.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` for the 8–14% (one operator, no sample, no denominator) to `medium` for the Churnkey result (large matched-pair sample with a stated method, vendor-reported).
- **sources**: https://www.builtbyfoundry.io/blog/annual-vs-monthly-subscriptions-creator-apps ; https://churnkey.co/growth/library/monthly-to-annual-conversion ; https://developer.paddle.com/build/retain/configure-term-optimization-automatic-upgrades/
- **how-to**: Trigger on tenure **and** engagement rather than calendar alone — Churnkey's approach scores tenure, engagement depth and usage consistency, and Paddle's Term Optimization does the same across in-app, email and payment-recovery flows. Show the offer in-app at the next active session with a single email fallback and a cooldown after dismissal. **Suppress it entirely while a cancel flow or a payment-recovery sequence is running.** Log it as `plan_changed` with `change_type: term_change` (instrumentation #15) — a term change is not an upgrade and must not be counted as one.
- **example-copy**: "you've been with us 90 days, here's an annual offer" — the framing one operator reports working in renewal emails. `[unsourced — no large-sample source publishes verbatim high-performing annual-upgrade copy, and no A/B'd variants with results were located. Treat this as a starting hypothesis, not a proven asset.]`
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly should not run this yet. With ~40 customers and roughly six months to its first directional churn reading (P-261), the monthly plan **is** Ledgerly's measurement instrument — every month each customer either renews or does not, and that is the only verdict Ledgerly gets. Converting its best customers to annual now would replace forty monthly verdicts a month with silence until next year. Revisit at the point where the monthly retention curve has flattened (P-280).

### P-269 — Make the annual discount a rate you keep, not a one-off coupon

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: A one-time discount creates a renewal cliff twelve months out, where the price jumps back to list and the customer re-evaluates from scratch. A permanent percentage attached to the annual term makes the term itself the thing of value and removes the cliff entirely.
- **works-when**: you can represent a durable discounted price in your billing system — straightforward on Stripe; on mobile, use a separately-priced annual SKU rather than an introductory offer.
- **fails-when**: you need the ARPU back later. A forever-percent discount is genuinely forever, and you will carry a cohort permanently below list that you cannot reprice without a migration.
- **observed-effect**: Churnkey reports the forever-percent discount contributes an additional **11.2 percentage points of churn reduction** on top of the term change itself (50,000+ matched subscriber pairs, vendor-reported, no independent verification). **This pattern publishes no sizing figure of its own, because the sizing is owned by P-093 in `04-price-points.md`, which is the authoritative source for it**: a web-SaaS median annual discount of **20%** inside a core band of **16–25%**, on n=560 plans across 207 products (CompareEdge, 2026), with the working discount computed as a break-even against churn rather than chosen from a band. **Mobile consumer contrast, and nothing more than a contrast:** RevenueCat SOSA 2026 gives a **$10 monthly / $34.80 annual** median pair, implying roughly 71% off the monthly run rate, and Adapty's 2026 data gives **$12.99 / $38.42**, implying about 75%. Those are app-store consumer medians shaped by store price tiers and store-mediated cancellation, they run three to four times the web band, and **they are never a target for a web product**. `[unsourced practitioner aside, recorded and never prescribed from: an earlier draft of this pattern reported operators "describing" a 30–45% band. No operator was named, no sample was given, and nothing in this corpus supports it. It contradicts the sourced web band above and must not be quoted, sized from, or averaged with anything.]`
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — a single vendor's churn figure with no independent verification. The sizing question is not evidenced here at all and is answered in P-093.
- **sources**: https://churnkey.co/growth/library/monthly-to-annual-conversion ; https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps/ `[mobile IAP]` ; https://adapty.io/state-of-in-app-subscriptions/ `[mobile IAP]` ; P-093 in `04-price-points.md` for the sizing
- **how-to**: Do not size the discount from this pattern. Compute the break-even against your own churn by the method in P-093, and expect the answer to land in the **16–25%** web band around a **20%** median — and if your break-even comes out above 25%, read that as P-093 instructs: the problem is churn, and a discount would be paying to hide it rather than to fix it. What this pattern governs is the *shape* of whatever number P-093 gives you. Represent it as a permanent price attached to the annual term, not as a coupon with an expiry, so the renewal lands at the same number the customer already agreed to. Then apply two guardrails: no stacking with other discounts, and never present the annual offer during an active cancel flow.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly Solo at $9/month has an annual run rate of $108 and publishes annual at **$90** — ten months for twelve, a **16.67%** discount, which is the figure P-093 derives for this product and the only annual discount Ledgerly carries anywhere in this library. The pattern's contribution is that $90 is printed as the annual price rather than as "$108, 16.67% off for the first year": the second version guarantees a re-evaluation in twelve months at exactly the moment the customer has the least recent memory of the value. Note what the mobile medians would have done here — 71% off $108 is about $31 a year, roughly $2.60 a month for a product with a real per-receipt cost — which is why they are a contrast and not a target.

### P-270 — Do not read annual retention as proof the product is loved

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: An annual subscriber *cannot* churn for 365 days. Their retention through months 2–11 is a billing artefact, not a behaviour. The churn is deferred rather than avoided, and it lands as a cliff — which means the number that looks like loyalty is partly a contract term and partly a selection effect that existed before you did anything.
- **works-when**: whenever you are using retention as a product-quality signal, or presenting it as one to someone else.
- **fails-when**: **never as a caution, but note that annual is often still the right commercial choice** — cash upfront, lower payment-failure exposure, fewer renewal decisions — even when it is the worse learning choice. The pattern says take the cash and refuse the reassurance, not avoid annual plans.
- **observed-effect**: **The correlation is large and consistent.** RevenueCat, 10,000+ apps: 12-month retention **28% annual versus 11% monthly** (2.5×); median first-renewal **56% monthly versus 27% annual**, which the source itself warns are **not comparable** because a monthly first renewal is a 30-day survival test and an annual one is a 365-day test. Adapty, 16,000 apps, **trial-originated** subscribers only: Day-380 **19.9% annual versus 14.2% monthly** (1.4×). **Two credible vendors with large samples disagree by roughly a factor of two on the size of the annual advantage, which is itself the finding.** **Three things are entangled in the gap and at least two are not the plan:** (1) **mechanical** — the measurement window forbids the event; (2) **selection** — people who pay a year upfront were already the most confident, and the plan revealed that confidence rather than creating it; (3) **commitment**, which is the only part a conversion campaign can capture. **The evidence that it is not mostly causal:** if annual plans caused loyalty, cancellations should cluster at renewal. They do not — **35% of all annual cancellations occur in month 1** (SOSA 2026), and SOSA 2025 reports cancellations clustering in "the first and last month". RevenueCat states directly that annual subscriptions **"can mask a broken product"** and that the apparent retention is **"delayed churn"**, and warns that "anything you are testing around months 2-11 to improve retention will be harder to measure the impact of unless you have a massive dataset."
- **evidence**: `medium` — the correlation is very well documented across two independent large datasets; the decomposition into mechanical, selection and causal components is a mechanism argument supported by the month-1 cancellation cluster rather than by a randomised test. **Nobody has randomised plan duration at scale.**
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/monthly-subscriptions-when-to-offer ; https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/annual-vs-monthly-renewal-rates ; https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-trends-benchmarks-2026 ; https://adapty.io/state-of-in-app-subscriptions/
- **how-to**: Track **engagement** retention for annual subscribers alongside billing retention and treat the engagement curve as the truth. Pre-PMF, keep a monthly plan available specifically because it gives you a monthly verdict. And police the language: "our annual subscribers retain better" is a fact about your book; "moving people to annual will improve retention" is a hypothesis, and the Churnkey matched-pair result in P-268 is the only decent evidence for it.
- **worked-example**: If Ledgerly ships the $90 annual plan and sees 12-month retention of 45% against 15% on monthly, the correct sentence is "annual subscribers retained at 45%, and we cannot separate the plan from the kind of customer who chose it." The temptation is to conclude that annual plans fixed retention and to push everyone onto them, which would delete the only monthly signal Ledgerly has — and at 40 customers, that signal is the entire measurement programme.

### P-271 — Time win-back to the recurrence of the problem, and always hold out a control

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: Consumers resubscribe when the underlying need returns — tax season, a new project, a trip, an injury healed. A day-30 win-back email fires on your schedule, not theirs. And because a large fraction of churned subscribers return unprompted, any campaign without a holdout will claim credit for people who were coming back anyway.
- **works-when**: products with episodic, recurring need.
- **fails-when**: continuous-use products where a lapse genuinely means the value was rejected; and for annual churners, where the return rate is structurally tiny and the campaign is close to a waste of the sending reputation.
- **observed-effect**: **Unprompted reactivation within one year, no win-back campaign** (RevenueCat SOSA 2026, 115,000+ apps, **consumer mobile**): **monthly subscribers 20.1%** (up from 13.7% in SOSA 2025); weekly 9.0%; **annual 5.2%** (range quoted 4–6%). By price: **high-priced monthly 28.9%** versus **low-priced monthly 15.4%**; **high-priced annual 4.4%**. By category: **Productivity monthly 36.1%** (doubled from 17.1%); Health & Fitness monthly ~12%. By geography: Asia-Pacific monthly 24%; North America monthly ~18%, the lowest region. Annual reactivation is **4.9–5.9% across every geography** and **4.4–5.6% across every price tier** — i.e. it barely varies. **Post-reactivation tenure averages four months** before churning again. The headline that follows: **~95% of cancelled annual subscribers never come back**, and monthly subscribers return at roughly **4×** the annual rate. RevenueCat's framing: "Users reactivate when the problem comes back, not when your win-back email lands." **The win-back-campaign rates circulating (8–15%, 15–25%) come from vendor blogs and SEO content with no disclosed sample** — `[unsourced — would need a named operator or platform to publish reactivation rate for campaign-exposed versus holdout churned cohorts, with N and window]`.
- **evidence**: `medium` for the unprompted baselines — the largest consumer subscription dataset available, cut many ways and consistent across cuts, but all of it RevenueCat's, and internal consistency inside one vendor's dataset is not the independent corroboration `strong` requires. `anecdote` for the timing tactic itself, which no source tested.
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/20-of-your-churned-users-will-come-back-but-are-you-ready ; https://revenuecat.github.io/codelabs/monetization-success-formula/reactivation/
- **how-to**: Identify the seasonal or event trigger for your category and fire the win-back on it rather than on a day count. **Always hold out 10–20% of churned users and measure lift against them** — with a ~20% organic return rate for monthly consumer subscribers, an un-held-out campaign will report a 20%+ "success rate" while doing nothing at all. Log `holdout_group` on `offer_presented` and `subscription_reactivated` (instrumentation #16–17). If your monthly reactivation is materially below 20%, suspect friction in the return path (P-272) before suspecting the offer.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's trigger is the tax calendar, not day 30. A freelancer who cancelled in June is not persuadable in July and is very persuadable in January. At 40 customers Ledgerly will accumulate maybe twenty churned accounts in a year, which is far too few to power a holdout comparison — so the honest position is that Ledgerly should **build the holdout field now and not claim a lift later**. The RevenueCat baselines are mobile and cannot be Ledgerly's benchmark; they are a reason to expect that some fraction returns without being asked, which is what makes the holdout necessary.

### P-272 — Make returning one tap, and never delete the account

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: The reactivation decision is made in a moment. Re-entering payment details, redoing onboarding, or discovering that their data is gone converts a returning customer into a lost one — and the returning customer is the cheapest customer you will ever get, because the acquisition was paid for once already.
- **works-when**: always.
- **fails-when**: data-retention obligations force deletion, in which case say so explicitly at cancellation and offer an export. It also fails if "never delete" is implemented as "never let them delete", which is a different and worse product.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the one-tap path specifically. RevenueCat's recommended tactic set is explicitly (1) pause instead of cancel, (2) win-back offers for lapsed subscribers, (3) **"one-tap reactivation without re-entering payment information"**, (4) trigger on need, not schedule. Their published diagnostic: **monthly reactivation materially below the ~20% baseline indicates friction or poor timing in the return path** rather than a bad offer. Supporting arithmetic from P-271: post-reactivation tenure averages four months, so the return is worth roughly four billing cycles.
- **evidence**: `medium` — a vendor recommendation set backed by their own 115,000-app reactivation data, with the diagnostic (below-baseline reactivation implies friction) being the part that is actually evidenced.
- **sources**: https://revenuecat.github.io/codelabs/monetization-success-formula/reactivation/ ; https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/20-of-your-churned-users-will-come-back-but-are-you-ready
- **how-to**: Keep the entitlement history, the payment method and the user's content after cancellation. Make the first screen a returning user sees a one-tap resubscribe **at the price they had**. On mobile, use the store's win-back offer types so the purchase completes without leaving your app. Pair this with P-251 in `checkout`: the cancellation confirmation is the right place to say the data is still there and that returning is one tap.
- **example-copy**: "Welcome back. Your 1,240 receipts are exactly where you left them. [Restart Solo — $9/month]"
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's asset is the receipt archive, which is the thing a freelancer cannot reconstruct and the reason returning is easy to make attractive. Deleting a cancelled account's receipts after 30 days would destroy the single strongest reactivation lever the product has. The correct policy is to retain, to say so at cancellation, and to offer an export — which also satisfies the honest-deletion requirement without volunteering to burn the archive.

### P-273 — Make the churn survey one required question with a stable code list

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: A cancelling customer has already decided and wants the process over. Every additional field trades response rate for detail. One required question with a fixed enum yields analysable, comparable-over-time data; a free-text box yields a pile of prose nobody will ever code.
- **works-when**: you control the cancel flow (web) or can show an in-app prompt before deep-linking to the store (mobile).
- **fails-when**: store-mediated cancellation on iOS, where you often never see the cancellation until it is done. Your only window there is between `auto_renew_disabled` and expiry, which is why that event exists separately in the instrumentation set.
- **observed-effect**: Baremetrics recommends a **two-stage design**: one required reason question at cancellation, then a follow-up by email for depth, "to keep initial friction low for disengaged customers"; survey completion drops sharply after 7–8 minutes. The value of a stable enum is a time series, which is destroyed the moment the code list changes.
- **evidence**: `medium` — practitioner guidance from a company that has run these for many SaaS products, with no published response-rate comparison between designs.
- **sources**: https://baremetrics.com/blog/cancellation-survey ; https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-churn-reasons-how-to-fix
- **how-to**: Ship exactly one required radio question with five to seven stable codes plus optional free text. Use a code list you will not change. Suggested list, aligned to the reason distributions observed in the wild: `not_using_enough` / `too_expensive` / `missing_feature` / `switched_to_alternative` / `technical_problems` / `temporary_break` / `other`. Then email the follow-up.
- **example-copy**: Follow-up questions Baremetrics recommends, verbatim: *"What made you question if [product] was a good fit for you?"*; *"When did you decide to cancel, what prompted the decision?"*; *"What are you using instead now?"*; *"What could have been done to prevent cancellation?"*; *"Would you re-subscribe if X was no longer an issue?"* Questions they recommend **avoiding**: *"Do you think you will use X again?"* and a context-free NPS question, because the answers are not actionable. RevenueCat adds two designed to separate mis-selling from unmet expectations: *"What initially prompted your signup?"* and *"Did our service meet the needs you expected when joining?"*
- **worked-example**: At 40 customers Ledgerly will collect maybe four responses a month, which is not a distribution — it is four sentences. That is fine and it is the correct scale of ambition: the enum exists so that in two years the time series is analysable, and the emailed follow-up exists because at n=4 the free text is worth more than the code. Do not change the code list when the answers are surprising.

### P-274 — Treat "too expensive" as a value verdict until proven otherwise

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: "Too expensive" is the socially easiest answer on any cancellation form. It is a comparison between price and *perceived delivered value*, and the denominator is almost always the broken half. Cutting price in response fixes the numerator of a problem in the denominator, and buys you a cheaper version of the same churn.
- **works-when**: interpreting any cancel-reason distribution.
- **fails-when**: you are genuinely mispriced against a named alternative the customer can point to — which is exactly why the follow-up question in P-273 matters, and why the cross-tabulation below is the actual test.
- **observed-effect**: **Google Play's own cancellation survey across 99.5M expiration entries** (via RevenueCat, SOSA 2025): **insufficient usage 37.02%**, **cost 34.64%**, other 11.68%, found a better app 9.54%, technical issues 7.12%. **The largest single reason is not using it enough**, and the two together are **72%** of all stated reasons. RevenueCat's read on the cost line: "that may not be a price reason but a value issue." **A different category with a different shape**, kept separate: DTC physical subscriptions (Loop, citing Chargebee's 2024 Retention Benchmarks across 1,000+ Shopify brands) — price 31%, overstock 16%, variety 15%, no longer needed 15%.
- **evidence**: `medium` for the distribution — 99.5 million expiration entries is by far the largest cancel-reason dataset in this corpus, but it is Google Play's own survey instrument relayed by RevenueCat, so the sample is enormous and the number of independent organisations behind it is one, and its answer options were written by the platform rather than by a researcher. `medium` also for the interpretation, which is a mechanism argument rather than a measured decomposition.
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/subscription-app-churn-reasons-how-to-fix ; https://www.loopwork.co/blog/why-subscribers-cancel-save-offers-that-work
- **how-to**: Cross-tabulate `cancel_reason_submitted` against actual usage in the 30 days before cancellation. If the "too expensive" cohort had near-zero usage, it is a value or activation problem and a discount will buy you a cheaper churn. If the "too expensive" cohort were heavy users, that is the rare case where the price genuinely is the issue — and the right response is usually **a lower tier, not a lower price**, because it preserves the ARPU of everyone who was not complaining.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's cross-tab is cheap to build: for each cancellation, count receipts processed in the prior 30 days. A Solo customer who cancelled at $9 having processed 6 receipts against a 100 allowance did not have a price problem; they had an activation problem, and the fix is the post-purchase value moment in P-255. A Solo customer who cancelled having processed 98 receipts is the rare genuine price signal, and the response is a tier between Solo and Books, not a cut to Solo.

### P-275 — Trigger the top-up at the limit, not on a schedule

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: A top-up or add-on offered at the moment the customer hits a ceiling is a solution to a problem they are currently having. The same offer sent as a campaign is an interruption. The first reads as service; the second reads as extraction, and the difference is entirely in the timing.
- **works-when**: the product has a natural, honest usage limit tied to the value metric — credits, storage, exports, minutes, seats.
- **fails-when**: the limit is artificial and the customer can tell, at which point the top-up reads as a toll booth you installed and damages the subscription itself, not just the add-on. It also fails when the top-up is priced below the next tier's marginal rate, which cannibalises upgrades (P-276).
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. RevenueCat argues (2026-02) that hybrid monetisation — subscription plus usage-based or consumable pricing — is becoming the default for consumer apps specifically because AI introduces genuine variable cost, but **publishes no adoption or revenue-share figures**: `[unsourced — would need RevenueCat or Adapty to publish the share of subscription apps also selling consumables and the resulting revenue split, which neither does as of their 2026 reports]`. The shape is supported indirectly: SOSA 2026 shows AI apps earning **41% more revenue per payer** ($30.16 versus $21.37 year-1 RLTV) while **churning roughly 30% faster** — high value, unstable subscription, which is precisely the profile where consumption-based top-ups fit better than a flat subscription.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — a vendor's forward-looking argument with no adoption data behind it, plus an indirect inference from AI-app economics.
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/ai-hybrid-monetization ; https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps/
- **how-to**: Instrument `limit_reached` (instrumentation #13) **before** you build any top-up, so you know how often the moment occurs and at what usage level. Then offer the top-up in-context at the limit, with the smallest useful unit and a visible price. Never email a top-up campaign to people who have not hit a limit. Watch for cannibalisation: if top-up purchases rise while plan upgrades fall, the top-up is priced too cheaply relative to the next tier.
- **example-copy**: "You've used all 100 receipts this month. Add 50 more for $5, or move to Books ($19/month, 400 receipts) — at your current pace Books is cheaper from next month."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly already sells a top-up pack and does not instrument the limit. Building `limit_reached` first would tell it how many Solo customers hit 100 in a month and what their usage distribution looks like above it — which is also the answer to whether the Solo allowance is set correctly, a more valuable question than the top-up itself.

### P-276 — Peg one flat top-up price at or just above your entry tier's per-unit rate

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: If the top-up price per unit sits below any tier's in-plan per-unit price, a customer on that tier can buy units more cheaply as top-ups than by upgrading, and the ladder inverts. Pegging a single flat overage rate at or just above the **entry** tier's rate — the most expensive in-plan rate — makes inversion structurally impossible at every tier, and makes the penalty widen automatically as customers climb.
- **works-when**: the product meters a countable unit and has more than one tier.
- **fails-when**: your unit differs in kind across tiers — where the higher tier's unit is genuinely better, per-unit comparison across tiers is meaningless and the peg logic does not apply. It also fails where the marginal cost of the unit is high enough that the entry-tier rate does not cover it.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` — this is a structural argument, evidenced by observed implementations rather than by a measured result. **ElevenLabs Reception AI** (observed 2026-08-12) publishes a single flat overage: **"Additional usage is billed at $0.40 per credit"**, applied uniformly across Basic ($22/mo, 60 phone minutes), Plus ($55/mo, 200) and Premium ($99/mo, 500). `derived`, assuming one credit equals one phone minute — **an assumption the page does not confirm, flagged rather than asserted** — the in-plan rates are $0.3667, $0.2750 and $0.1980, so the top-up premium runs **+9% / +45% / +102%**. The ladder can never invert because $0.40 sits above the most expensive in-plan rate; the upgrade pressure escalates with tier size automatically; and it is one number to publish. The contrasting implementations: **Bannerbear** refuses top-ups entirely — *"It is not possible to go over your monthly image / video API credits limit"*, forcing a tier upgrade — and **Descript** and **Runway** keep top-up rates off the pricing page entirely, behind the dashboard, so the public ladder stays clean. Runway's minimum top-up is **1,000 credits against a Standard plan allowance of 625** — the smallest available top-up is 1.6× the entire monthly plan, which is an upgrade nudge disguised as a minimum.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — four observed implementations on one date, no measured outcome for any of them, and the ElevenLabs arithmetic rests on a stated unit-mapping assumption.
- **sources**: L15 teardown corpus — elevenlabs.io/docs/reception-ai/billing/plans-and-pricing.md, bannerbear.com/pricing, descript.com/pricing, runwayml.com/pricing, all observed 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Compute the per-unit price of every tier. Set one flat top-up rate at or slightly above the **highest** of them, which will be your entry tier. Publish it as a single number with no per-tier table. If the resulting rate looks punitive at the top tier, that is the pattern working — it is the pressure that moves large customers to the next tier or to a conversation.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's rates: Solo $9/100 = **$0.090 per receipt**; Books $19/400 = **$0.0475**; top-up **$5/50 = $0.100**. The top-up sits just above Solo's in-plan rate, which is the highest rate on the ladder, so the peg holds and no tier can be undercut by stacking packs. Walk the crossover: at 150 receipts a Solo customer pays $9 + $5 = **$14** against Books at $19, so the pack wins; at 200 they pay $9 + 2×$5 = **$19**, exactly level with Books; at 250 they pay $9 + 3×$5 = **$24** against $19. So **Books wins for any month above 200 receipts** — packs are sold whole, so the 201st receipt forces a third pack and jumps the bill to $24 — and the gap widens with every pack after that. The rejected alternative shows what the peg is for. At $4 a pack the top-up prices receipts at $0.080, below Solo's own $0.090, and the ladder inverts: the same 200-receipt customer pays $9 + 2×$4 = **$17** against $19, so stacking beats upgrading, and Books does not become the cheaper option until the third pack is forced at $9 + 3×$4 = **$21**, which is 250 receipts and not the 300 this example previously claimed. The customer spends that whole range buying units at a discount to the plan they are already on. One number changed, and the inversion window closes.

### P-277 — Sell rollover as a feature, and make purchased units permanent

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: Unused allowance is the most common reason a customer talks themselves out of renewing — "I didn't use it, I'm cancelling." Rollover converts that from a loss into a balance, which is a retention asset. But rollover given away everywhere destroys the use-it-or-lose-it pressure that drives usage, so the working design gives plan credits an expiry, sells rollover as a top-tier feature, and makes **purchased** units permanent — which justifies a top-up price above the plan rate without ever saying "we're charging you more."
- **works-when**: metered products where allowance genuinely goes unused in some months.
- **fails-when**: units carry real marginal cost that you have already incurred, or where an uncapped balance could accumulate into a hoard that suppresses several months of future revenue. Both observed implementations cap it for exactly this reason.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` — observed mechanics only, all 2026-08-12. **ElevenLabs**: *"Unused credits roll over for up to two months"* with a maximum balance of **"3× your monthly quota"**, and **"Downgrading or cancelling forfeits unused credits."** Generous enough to reduce "I didn't use it" churn, capped so it cannot become a hoard, and forfeited on cancellation so the balance is a reason to stay rather than a reason to leave with something. **Runway**: Standard and Pro — *"monthly credits don't roll over — they reset within 24 hours of your billing date"*; Max — *"up to one month of unused credits rolls over to the following month"*; and, separately, *"Any additional credits you purchase never expire."* So rollover is **sold as a premium-tier feature**, plan credits expire on the cheap tiers, and **purchased credits are permanent while plan credits are not**.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — two observed implementations on one date, no measured retention effect for either, no source in this corpus tested rollover against no rollover.
- **sources**: L15 teardown corpus — elevenlabs.io/pricing, runwayml.com/pricing, both observed 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Give plan allowance an expiry and say so plainly. Cap any rollover at one to three months and at a multiple of the monthly quota. Make **purchased** units permanent and price them accordingly — permanence is a non-price sweetener that justifies a top-up above your plan rate (P-276) and the customer experiences it as a trade rather than a markup. Decide the forfeit-on-cancellation rule deliberately: forfeiting makes the balance a retention asset, and not forfeiting makes it a goodwill asset. Say which in the terms.
- **example-copy**: "Unused receipts roll over for two months, up to 200 in the bank. Receipt packs you buy never expire."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyer has a seasonal load — light months and a filing crunch. Rollover capped at two months and 200 receipts converts the light months from a cancellation prompt into a stockpile the customer is saving for March, which is exactly the churn the pause pattern (P-267) otherwise has to catch. Making the repriced $5 packs permanent gives Ledgerly a defensible reason for pricing them above the Solo rate, which is the argument P-276 needs in order to be sayable out loud.

### P-278 — Denominate the referral reward in product, not cash

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: For a software product, a month of service has near-zero marginal cost to you and full sticker value to the customer. A cash reward costs you cash at face value. Product rewards also self-select for people who actually want the product, which improves the quality of what the referral brings back.
- **works-when**: marginal cost of service is low and the reward is genuinely wanted — which requires the product to have real value, so the pattern quietly assumes something worth referring.
- **fails-when**: your product has real per-user variable cost (AI inference, physical goods, licensed content), where free months are genuinely expensive; and where consumers cannot perceive the reward's value. **The counter-evidence is real**: consumers state a preference for cash, and in ecommerce outcome data cash beat coupons.
- **observed-effect**: **Every figure in this pattern reaches us through referral-software vendor blogs and none was read at primary level** `[secondary — referralrock.com for Dropbox and Morning Brew, referralcandy.com for Evernote and the 500-programme study, impact.com for the consumer-survey figures. Dropbox, Evernote and Morning Brew published none of this themselves in anything this corpus fetched, and every carrier sells referral software, so each is an interested party retelling a case study it did not run. Treat the numbers as the genre's received account rather than as observations.]` **Dropbox**: give-get, symmetric, paid in product — **500MB to the referrer and 500MB to the referee**, capped at 16GB on Basic and 32GB on Plus — against a paid-search CAC of **$233–$338** `[secondary, referralrock.com; no date, no method, no Dropbox source]`, producing **35% of daily signups** in the first 15 months and **2.8M direct referrals in February 2010** `[secondary, same carrier]`. **Two caveats almost every retelling omits and this source carries: one-third of signups already came from word of mouth before the programme launched, and the long-run effect was a "permanent 60% bump" — the famous 3,900% is total growth over the window, not referral-attributed growth.** **Evernote** paid in **free Premium time** — a sample of the paid product, deliberately contrasted with Dropbox's storage and PayPal's cash; exact point values, participation and attributed growth are **not published**. **Morning Brew**, the most legible economics in the set: milestone-based, **give-only**, paid in physical swag (stickers/tote/notebook at low tiers, mystery box at 7 referrals, apparel higher, a work-from-home setup at 1,000). Grew from 100,000 to 4.5M subscribers; referrals drove **~80% of growth before paid acquisition**, settling at **~30% of ongoing growth**, at **~$0.25 per referred subscriber versus $4–5 on Facebook/Instagram** — a 16–20× CAC advantage. Participation in 2019: **225,000 subscribers had made at least 1 referral; 80,000 had made 3+**. **Counter-evidence**: consumers state a preference for **cash (58%)**, and in ecommerce outcome data **cash/commission rewards were 2.2×–4.5× more likely to produce meaningful referral revenue than coupon-only programmes** (ReferralCandy, 500 established Shopify referral programmes, window 2025-07-01 → 2026-06-30).
- **evidence**: `medium` — three well-documented named programmes pointing one way, one 500-programme ecommerce study pointing the other, with the resolution being that the ecommerce comparison was cash versus **coupons**, not cash versus product access.
- **sources**: https://referralrock.com/blog/dropbox-referral-program/ ; https://www.referralcandy.com/blog/evernote-referral-program ; https://referralrock.com/blog/morning-brew-referral-program/ ; https://impact.com/referral/top-10-referral-marketing-statistics/ ; https://www.referralcandy.com/blog/referral-program-benchmarks-whats-a-good-conversion-rate-in-2025/
- **how-to**: Give free time or expanded limits on both sides. Size the reward against your own gross profit per payer rather than against a published floor: the **$21 or 11%-discount** figure consumers are said to expect, and the **$10 store credit** said to be the most common reward, are `[secondary — impact.com's 2025 summary of a *State of Referral Marketing 2024* survey it did not run, with no sample, no population and no primary document located; the underfunding conclusion drawn from the pair is that summary's, not a measurement]` and are recorded here as a rough sense of scale, never as a number to set a reward from. **Pay only on the referee's converted and retained event** — after their first successful renewal — never on the share and never on the signup. The arithmetic that governs the ceiling: a $5 reward paid on conversion at a 10% link conversion rate costs $5 per acquired payer; the same $5 paid on *signup* costs $50 per acquired payer.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly should size the combined give-get at well under half of its own year-1 gross profit per payer — computed from its own book, not from RevenueCat's mobile $23 median, which does not apply to a web product with no store commission. One free month on each side of a $9 plan is $18 of perceived value across the pair against a true cost of roughly $0.54 in processing and near-zero serving. Note that $18 does **not** clear the $21 figure quoted above, which is one more reason not to treat that figure as a floor: it is secondary, its population is unknown, and a solo product cannot buy its way past it without giving away more than a referred customer is worth. Size on the cost, and pay it when the referee's second month renews.

### P-279 — Design the referral ladder for the top 1%, and the discovery for everyone

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: Referral output is extremely concentrated — the median advocate refers once and stops, while a tiny group refers repeatedly and produces most of the volume — so the reward structure should escalate. At the same time, the binding constraint on total participation is not reward attractiveness but whether people ever see the link.
- **works-when**: you have enough users that a top 1% exists, roughly above 500.
- **fails-when**: under about 100 customers, where the concentration has not formed and a tiered ladder is complexity with nothing behind it.
- **observed-effect**: **83% of advocates refer exactly once**; the top **1.1%** (11+ referrals) generate **30.2%** of all referred customers; only **20%** of programmes use tiers at all, three tiers being most common; **62%** of consumers say leaderboards would increase participation. **60% of non-participants say they simply never received a referral link or code** — the largest single cause of non-participation. **Realistic participation**: top-quartile share-action rate **4.64%**, top-decile **13.38%** (share-button clicks per eligible prompt); median programmes sit well below. **Time to first measurable referred sale: 14 days typical, 68% of programmes within the first month.** Referred customers are **10.7×** more likely than other channels to become advocates themselves. **>78%** of programmes reward both sides. All from ReferralCandy's 500-programme study (**consumer ecommerce, not subscription**, window 2025-07-01 → 2026-06-30) and impact.com's 2025 summary of the *State of Referral Marketing 2024*.
- **evidence**: `medium` — a 500-programme study with a stated window and a consumer-survey companion, but the population is ecommerce rather than subscription, and the two sources are both published by referral-software vendors.
- **sources**: https://www.referralcandy.com/blog/referral-program-benchmarks-whats-a-good-conversion-rate-in-2025/ ; https://impact.com/referral/top-10-referral-marketing-statistics/ ; https://referralrock.com/blog/morning-brew-referral-program/
- **how-to**: Two jobs, kept separate. **Distribution**: put the link somewhere every user passes repeatedly — after the value moment, in the settings root, in every transactional email footer. **Ladder**: three milestone tiers with escalating rewards, where tier 1 is reachable at one referral so the median advocate gets something, and the top tier is aspirational. Track `referral_link_shared` separately from `referral_converted` (instrumentation #19) so you know which of the two jobs is failing.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly at ~40 customers is far below the threshold. At a top-quartile 4.64% share-action rate, forty customers produce roughly **two shares**. A tiered ladder here is pure overhead. What is worth doing now is the instrumentation and one link in the settings root: at n=40, the finding available is qualitative — *do any of these forty people ever share anything?* — and that is a real finding. The programme economics are not available and should not be modelled.

### P-280 — Read the cohort curve for flattening, not for level

- **layer**: `expansion-retention`
- **mechanism**: A retention curve that flattens has a stable core of retained customers, and its asymptote — not its month-1 value — determines the business. A curve that keeps declining at a constant rate has no core, and its LTV converges to a much smaller number regardless of how good month 1 looked. There is a second reason the level misleads: because high-churn-propensity customers leave first, the surviving cohort is progressively enriched with low-churn customers, so cohort-level retention rates **typically increase over time** and extrapolating an early slope forward is biased, not merely noisy.
- **works-when**: you have at least three monthly cohorts with three or more months of history each.
- **fails-when**: annual plans, where the curve is a step function and tells you nothing until month 12 (P-270); and at very small N, where apparent "flattening" is often just the cohort running out of people to lose.
- **observed-effect**: **Consumer subscription** practitioner consensus (Lenny Rachitsky, 2020-06-09, survey of 20 growth leaders including Andrew Chen, Brian Balfour, Casey Winters and Elena Verna): **6-month user retention — consumer SaaS subscription "good" ~40%, "great" ~70%; consumer transactional "good" ~30%, "great" ~50%.** Public comparators from the same piece: **Netflix 66% at 12 months; Spotify 72% at 6 months; Hulu 53% at 12 months.** **B2B, labelled separately and not transferable**: SMB/mid-market SaaS 6-month user retention "good" ~60% / "great" ~80%; consumer SaaS 12-month **net revenue** retention "good" ~55% / "great" ~80%. Against those reference brands, **RevenueCat's median mobile subscription app retains 11% of monthly-plan subscribers at 12 months** (10,000+ apps; lower quartile 5%, upper quartile 19%) — a nearly 4× spread around the median, which is how far the median app sits from the names everyone benchmarks against. Fader & Hardie's structural result: ignoring customer heterogeneity "causes a downward-biased estimate of the residual value of the customer base."
- **evidence**: `medium` — the benchmark bands are a practitioner survey with no published methodology; the RevenueCat quartiles are large-sample and well documented; the heterogeneity result is peer-reviewed and mathematical.
- **sources**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-good-retention-issue-29 ; https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/one-year-retention-rates-insights ; http://www.brucehardie.com/papers/022/
- **how-to**: Plot cumulative retention by cohort month on one chart, one line per acquisition cohort, and look at three things in order: **(1)** does any cohort go flat, and at what level; **(2)** is month-1 retention improving across successive cohorts; **(3)** is the *slope* between month 3 and month 6 improving. Month 1 is a promise-and-onboarding metric. Month 3 is the habit metric. **Month 12 is the business metric, and only it determines LTV.** Do not extrapolate the month-1 slope forward — heterogeneity guarantees the survivors' churn rate will keep improving, so a straight-line projection understates the base.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly has enough customers for three cohorts of roughly a dozen each — small enough that a single cancellation moves a line by eight percentage points. The chart is still worth building, because the question it answers is qualitative: does *any* cohort stop declining? If Ledgerly's month-3-to-month-6 slope is flat while month 1 is bad, the problem is activation (P-255 in `checkout`) and the price is fine. If the slope never flattens, no pricing change will fix it. Do not compare the levels to Netflix's 66%: that is a different product, a different category, and a company with a marketing budget larger than Ledgerly's total addressable market.

## Interactions

**P-264 (split the churn) versus every price change anyone will propose.** This is the layer's ordering rule and it is close to absolute below $10 a month. A blended churn number at that price point is roughly two-thirds a product signal and one-third a payments bug, and the two have opposite fixes. Any price recommendation made before `churn_type` exists is being made on a number that is 35% noise of a known kind.

**P-268 (convert to annual) versus P-270 (annual retention is not proof) versus P-261 (count churn events).** These pull hard against each other pre-PMF and the resolution is the measurement, not the money. Converting monthly subscribers to annual removes eleven churn events per customer per year — which is the numerator of the only estimate you have. A product that has not yet reached 25 observed churn events is buying cash with its measurement programme. After the curve has flattened (P-280), the trade reverses and the cash is the better deal.

**P-269 (permanent annual discount) versus P-262 (realised value).** A forever-percent discount permanently lowers the realised value of every cohort that takes it, and realised value is the metric you are reporting. Size the discount knowing that you will be quoting the discounted number as your RLTV for years, and that you cannot reprice that cohort without a migration.

**P-275/P-276 (top-ups) versus `tier-architecture` (layer 03).** A top-up priced below any tier's in-plan rate is a hole in the ladder, and it will be found — the customers who find it are precisely your heaviest users, the ones the ladder was built to move upward. Whenever the tier layer changes a price or an allowance, recompute every tier's per-unit rate and re-check the peg. This is a maintenance obligation, not a one-time design decision.

**P-277 (rollover) versus P-267 (pause).** Both address the same churn trigger — "I didn't use it this month" — and a product with genuine seasonality should not need both. Rollover is the better fit where the value metric is countable and the customer's usage is lumpy; pause is the better fit where the customer's absence is total and dated. Shipping both produces a customer who has banked three months of allowance *and* paused, which is a state your revenue reporting will not handle.

**P-271 (win-back with a holdout) versus every retention vendor's dashboard.** With a ~20% unprompted return rate among churned monthly consumer subscribers, a campaign measured without a holdout will report roughly a 20% success rate while doing nothing whatsoever. This is not a subtle bias; it is the entire effect. The `holdout_group` property is the cheapest fraud-prevention measure in the instrumentation set, and the fraud it prevents is self-directed.

**P-274 ("too expensive" is a value verdict) versus `price-points` (layer 04).** The cancel-reason distribution is the most tempting input to a price decision and the least reliable one, because the largest true reason (insufficient usage, 37%) and the most-stated reason (cost, 35%) are nearly the same size and the second frequently means the first. Never move a price on a cancel-reason distribution alone; move it on the cross-tabulation with usage.

**This whole layer versus `experimentation` (layer 12).** P-284 governs everything here that involves a comparison: fix the sample size in advance, because checking a retention test repeatedly and stopping when it goes significant pushes a nominal 5% false-positive rate to **26.1%** in Evan Miller's worked case. And the selection-versus-causation discipline in P-270 is the same discipline as P-293 — when customers choose their own plan, the difference in what happens next is not the plan's effect. Under a few hundred paywall views per variant per month you cannot randomise at all, and the honest answer to "does price affect my retention?" is **"I cannot know yet"**.

## Where this layer is thin

**Almost every figure here comes from a product with vastly more customers than a solo founder has, and several come from a different platform.** RevenueCat's medians span 115,000 apps; Churnkey's involuntary-churn work covers 25 million subscriptions; ReferralCandy's study covers 500 established programmes. These are useful for detecting catastrophe and for sanity-checking a plan. They are not targets. RevenueCat's monthly-plan year-1 retention runs **5% at the lower quartile and 19% at the upper — a nearly 4× spread around an 11% median** — so knowing the median tells you almost nothing about where you will land.

**The consumer benchmarks are overwhelmingly mobile in-app purchase, and this layer is frequently applied to web products.** Store commissions, store-owned retry windows, store-mediated cancellation and store win-back offers all shape those numbers, and none of them exist on Stripe. Where a figure is mobile it is labelled mobile in the pattern that carries it; nothing in this file should be transferred to a web product without checking that label.

**LTV:CAC is genuinely unavailable below ~100 observed churn events, and most advice pretends otherwise.** By the arithmetic in P-261, you need roughly 100 events for a decision-grade ratio and about 25 for a directional one. At 40 customers and 10%/month churn you reach 25 events at around month 6. Everything written before that point is a guess dressed as a metric.

**The involuntary-churn tooling is priced for companies with revenue.** Churnkey, Baremetrics Recover, Paddle Retain and their peers charge in a range that is absurd against a book of sixty customers at $10 a month. The free substitutes are the whole of P-265 and P-266: enable smart retries and the card account updater (both switches, not products), send one pre-dunning email 30 days before stored-card expiry, and on mobile accept that the store owns the retry and put a grace-period banner in-app.

**Referral programmes are the wrong tool at this size, and the referral data is ecommerce rather than subscription.** With 60 customers and a top-quartile 4.64% share-action rate you will generate roughly three shares; the concentration finding (top 1.1% of advocates producing 30.2% of referrals) means the mechanism only switches on at scale. The 500-programme study behind most of these numbers is Shopify ecommerce, not subscription software, and both referral sources are published by referral-software vendors.

**There is no good public data on expansion revenue for small consumer products.** This is the genuine void. Expansion benchmarks are overwhelmingly B2B — net revenue retention, seat expansion — and the consumer hybrid-monetisation literature is currently predictive rather than empirical. `[unsourced — would need RevenueCat or Adapty to publish the share of subscription apps also selling consumables and the resulting revenue split, which neither does as of their 2026 reports]` The top-up and rollover patterns here (P-276, P-277) rest entirely on **four observed implementations on one date with no measured outcomes**, and one of them carries a stated unit-mapping assumption the source does not confirm.

**No large-sample source publishes verbatim high-performing copy for annual upgrades, win-backs or save offers.** Every copy example in this layer is either one operator's paraphrase or a survey-design recommendation.
