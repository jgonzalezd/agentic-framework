# Layer 01 — Value metric

**Layer slug:** `value-metric` · **Loaded at:** Phase 3

This layer governs the single decision that everything downstream inherits: **what the price scales on.** Not how much — what *more of* costs more. The tier ladder is built on top of the metric, the page copy explains the metric, and the expansion path is the metric growing. Get the metric right and mediocre price points still work; get it wrong and no amount of price testing repairs it, because you are optimising the height of a ladder leaning against the wrong wall. The failure that costs the most is also the quietest one: choosing a **quantity** metric when the product is really a capability purchase, or a **capability** metric when the product is really consumed. Those are two different businesses with two different funnels, and the mistake is invisible from the inside — it shows up months later as a conversion rate nobody can explain and a cohort that converts once and never expands.

## How to use this layer

Read **P-001 → P-004 first, in order.** They are the sequencing patterns and they decide whether you even have a metric to argue about. **P-002 is the load-bearing pattern in this layer** and the one most often skipped: it separates the quantity question from the capability question before either gets priced. **P-005 → P-011** are the selection filters — apply all of them to every candidate metric and expect most candidates to die on one of them. **P-012 → P-014** are the legibility tests, which is where a theoretically correct metric usually fails in practice; run them before you commit, because a metric the buyer cannot compute is a metric you will have to withdraw. **P-015 → P-018** are situational: the dual-metric escape hatch, the outcome ceiling, the honest retreat to a flat fee, and the disclosure obligation that comes with a variable unit.

In a **COLD** regime you cannot test a metric against conversion, so lean on the mechanism patterns (P-002, P-005, P-006, P-009) and the legibility tests (P-012, P-013), which are checkable against five real prospects and a whiteboard. In **HOT**, the one thing worth instrumenting before anything else is the *distribution* of your candidate metric across existing users — P-004's third screen and P-022 in Layer 02 both need it, and it is the cheapest data in pricing.

## Patterns

### P-001 — Choose the metric before you argue about the number

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: A price point captures one spot on a demand curve. A value metric generates an unbounded set of them from a single decision, and it converts the customer's own growth into revenue growth without a renegotiation. That is why the metric outranks the number by two full priority levels — the number is a scalar, the metric is a function.
- **works-when**: You are pre-launch, repricing, or auditing a page whose conversion is bad for reasons nobody can name. Also whenever usage genuinely varies across customers and the variance correlates with value received.
- **fails-when**: Every customer consumes roughly the same amount — then a flat fee is simpler, converts better, and the metric conversation is over-engineering (see P-017). Also fails when the metric is expensive or confusing to meter, in which case the theoretically superior metric loses to the one you can actually bill.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The strongest sourced statement is directional and is Patrick Campbell's: *"if you get everything else wrong in pricing, but you get your value metric right, you'll do ok"* (Lenny's Newsletter, 2020-10-27, no sample disclosed). Ramanujam states the same rule as *"how you charge is often more important than how much you charge"* (Marketing Journal interview, 2016-07-03). **The circulating "value-metric companies grow 38% / 2× faster" figures trace only to AI-generated SEO sites and are discarded** — see Folklore. Campbell's published priority stack: **P1** segments + value metric; **P2** order-of-magnitude price, positioning, packaging; **P3** add-ons, specific price points, localisation, discounting; **P4** freemium, market expansion, multi-product.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(unanimous across Campbell, Poyar, Verna and Ramanujam; no clean public counterfactual, and every voice in the consensus sold pricing advisory)*
- **sources**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/saas-pricing-strategy · https://www.paddle.com/blog/value-metrics-pricing · https://www.marketingjournal.org/monetizinginnovation/ · https://review.firstround.com/its-price-before-product-period/
- **how-to**: Before touching a number, write the metric decision down as a standalone artefact: the unit, why it tracks value, and what it would take to meter it. Then set the price order-of-magnitude only ($10 vs $100 vs $1,000) and stop. Return to digits at Phase 5, not before.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's metric is receipts processed, and it is defensible: a freelancer who processes 400 receipts is running a bigger book than one who processes 40, and dreads it proportionally more. The current ladder ($9/100, $19/400) is a metric decision that was made correctly and then priced without checking the arithmetic — see P-022 in Layer 02.

### P-002 — Decide whether you are fencing on quantity or on capability, and say which

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: This is the centrepiece of the layer. **A quantity fence requires the buyer to want the product *again*. A capability fence requires them to want a *better version*.** Those are different psychological events with different triggers, different timing, and different funnels. A quantity fence converts on *recurrence* — the buyer hits the wall through success, and the upgrade is a re-order. It works only if consumption is repeated and roughly forecastable, and its whole conversion engine is the limit. A capability fence converts on *aspiration or obligation* — the buyer wants something they cannot currently do, and the upgrade is a different product. It works only if the withheld capability is nameable in one sentence and genuinely wanted by a minority. Pick the wrong one and you get a page where nobody upgrades for a reason nobody can articulate: quantity fences on a one-shot product produce customers who convert once and never expand, and capability fences on a consumed product produce customers who feel the cheap tier is deliberately crippled.
- **works-when**: **Quantity** — consumption repeats on a predictable cadence, the unit is countable, and heavier users genuinely get more value. **Capability** — segments differ in *what they do* rather than how much, and the gated capability sits after the activation moment rather than on the path to it.
- **fails-when**: Quantity fails when the buyer's need is finite (a one-time migration, a single tax year), because there is no second purchase to convert; when volume is unforecastable, because the buyer cannot self-select a tier; and when the cap arrives before the aha moment, converting the fence into a bounce. Capability fails when the gated feature is what the product is fundamentally *for* — Stripe's framing is that gating core-value features early in adoption "creates resentment rather than upgrades" — and when the capability is easily substituted, so the fence leaks. **The mixed failure is the common one:** a product that fences on quantity in the free tier and capability in the paid tiers, so the free user's upgrade trigger points at a rung whose differentiator they were never shown.
- **observed-effect**: The two fence families are formalised in the L16 fence taxonomy (compiled 2026-08-12) as the **usage/quantity fence** and the **feature fence**, each with distinct fit and break conditions, drawn from live pricing pages fetched 2026-08-12 (Zapier: 100 free tasks → 750/1,500/2,000 … 2,000,000; Vercel Pro: 10M edge requests then $2 per 1M; Notion: SAML SSO at Business, audit logs and SCIM at Enterprise; Vercel: audit logs and project RBAC Enterprise-only). The cleanest published evidence that the *quantity* limit — not the feature set — carries conversion in a quantity-fenced product: **Athenic raised its free-tier task limit from 100 to 500 and free→paid conversions fell 60%** (via Artisan Growth, `[aggregator — underlying source not independently reachable]`). Against that, on the capability side, **no controlled test isolating a feature fence from a quantity fence on the same product exists in the public record.** In AI products specifically the market has converged hard on quantity: allowance-defined tiers with identical feature sets across consumer rungs are the dominant published shape (Airtable 500 → 25,000 AI credits Free→Business; Midjourney 3.3 / 15 / 30 / 60 fast GPU hours at $10/$30/$60/$120; Claude Pro/Max 5×/Max 20× at $20/$100/$200 differing by headroom — all observed 2026).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the taxonomy is well-attested across primary pricing pages; the conversion asymmetry between the two fence types rests on one aggregator-sourced experiment)*
- **sources**: L16 fence taxonomy 2026-08-12 · https://zapier.com/pricing · https://vercel.com/pricing · https://www.notion.com/pricing · https://stripe.com/resources/more/saas-pricing-and-packaging-strategy · https://www.artisangrowthstrategies.com/blog/saas-pricing-experiment-results-real-tests-what-they-changed · https://www.eesel.ai/blog/midjourney-pricing
- **how-to**: Answer one question in writing before you design a tier: *when a customer outgrows their plan, what happened to them?* If the answer is "they did more of the same thing", you are on a quantity fence and the entire ladder should differ on the number, with the feature list identical across consumer rungs. If the answer is "they started doing a different thing", you are on a capability fence and the ladder should differ on named capabilities, with volume secondary. Write the sentence a buyer would say at the moment of upgrade — "I ran out" versus "I need X" — and check that your pricing page contains the words in that sentence. If you cannot decide, you have two segments, not one metric; run P-015.
- **example-copy**: Quantity fence, at the wall: "You've used all 100 receipts this month. Books gives you 400." · Capability fence, at the wall: "Multi-client tagging is on Books. Solo keeps one client book."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly is a **quantity** product and should commit to it. A freelancer's bookkeeping dread is recurring and forecastable — they know roughly how many receipts a month they get — so the buyer can self-select a rung before signing up, which is exactly the condition a quantity fence needs. The upgrade sentence is "I ran out," not "I need multi-client tagging." That has a concrete consequence for the ladder: the accountant seat and multi-client tagging currently sitting on Books are *capability* fences bolted onto a quantity ladder, and they muddy the one variable the buyer is supposed to compare. Either move them to add-ons (P-015 in Layer 02's demand-share test) or accept that Ledgerly has two segments — solo freelancer and freelancer-with-an-accountant — and price them as two ladders.

### P-003 — Write the value sentence, and let the blank name the metric

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: Metric selection collapses into a sentence-completion exercise. Forcing the sentence exposes whether you actually know what the customer gets more of, and it separates the value quantity from the cost quantity, which are usually different nouns and are almost always confused.
- **works-when**: There is a customer-side quantity that tracks the value they receive, that you can measure reliably, and that they can predict well enough not to fear the bill.
- **fails-when**: The sentence completes with something you cannot instrument, or with a quantity the customer would rather have *less* of. A metric that penalises the behaviour you want — charging per project when you want more projects, per collaborator when collaboration is the retention mechanism — is a tax on your own growth loop and shows up as suppressed usage rather than as churn.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the technique. The canonical illustrations: **Michelin** moved from selling tires to charging fleets per kilometre driven, so a tire lasting 20% longer earns roughly 20% *more* revenue instead of cannibalising sales; **Optimizely** rejected per-seat pricing in favour of monthly unique visitors; **HubSpot Marketing** switched from events to contacts because contacts are *"easier to understand and much more predictable"* (First Round Review ~2016; Strategyzer, undated; Paddle, undated). No controlled comparison of any of these switches has been published.
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://review.firstround.com/its-price-before-product-period/ · https://www.strategyzer.com/library/madhavan-ramanujam-shares-3-common-pricing-mistakes-companies-make-with-products-services · https://www.paddle.com/blog/value-metrics-pricing
- **how-to**: Write "our customer gets more value when ___ goes up" and fill the blank five different ways. Then write the mirror sentence — "our cost goes up when ___ goes up" — and notice where the two nouns diverge. Where they diverge you must choose the value noun and manage the cost gap in packaging (Layer 02); where they coincide you have the rare clean case (P-007).
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's two sentences: *value* rises with **receipts processed**; *cost* rises with **model calls per receipt**, which is a function of receipt legibility, not of receipt count. The nouns diverge, and the divergence is the whole reason the ladder needs the COGS stress test in Layer 02 rather than a per-unit price set by intuition.

### P-004 — Run the four-step metric screen and expect most candidates to die

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: Candidate metrics fail for four distinct reasons — customers reject them as unfair, they do not scale with customer size, they are gameable, or they cannot be metered. Screening in that order kills the cheapest failures first and stops you spending a month instrumenting a unit the customer will refuse.
- **works-when**: You can reach 5–20 real target customers to run step 2 against. The screen is cheap enough to run pre-launch with no traffic at all.
- **fails-when**: You run it on people who will never buy, or you stop at step 2 because the customers liked the metric. Step 3 is the one that decides whether the metric produces net revenue retention, and step 4 is the one that decides whether you can ship it this quarter.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Campbell's published four-step screen (Lenny's Newsletter, 2020-10-27, no sample disclosed): (1) brainstorm **5–10** proxy metrics that plausibly correlate with delivered value; (2) take them to customers and prospects, narrow to **1–2** the customers themselves agree are fair; (3) verify that **larger customers consume more** of the metric; (4) verify the metric **encourages retention rather than workarounds** — a metric customers game by sharing logins or batching exports is the wrong metric. Cited live examples: Wistia = videos/channels; Zapier = zaps/connections; HubSpot Marketing = contacts. The independent six-criteria list for a price metric adds **predictability** — the metric must let customers forecast costs (Product Tranquility, undated, fetched 2026-08-12).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(one credentialed operator's published procedure, corroborated in structure by an independent metrics source; no outcome data)*
- **sources**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/saas-pricing-strategy · https://www.producttranquility.com/pricing-metrics-your-secret-weapon-in-the-quest-for-value-based-pricing/ · https://www.paddle.com/blog/value-metrics-pricing
- **how-to**: List 5–10 candidates. Put each in front of five real prospects with the question "would it be fair if we charged on this?" and keep the one or two that survive. Then pull your own usage data and check the correlation between customer size and metric consumption — if it is flat, the metric will not expand revenue and you have a flat-fee product wearing a meter. Finally, spend ten minutes trying to cheat your own metric; if you succeed in ten minutes, your customers will succeed in five.
- **example-copy**: "Would it be fair if we charged you by the number of receipts you upload?"
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's five candidates: receipts processed · clients tracked · exports generated · months of history retained · seats. Receipts survives steps 1–3 (bigger freelancers upload more) and passes step 4 weakly — a determined user could batch a month of receipts into one composite photo, which is a real gaming vector worth capping at the product level rather than the pricing level. Seats dies at step 3: a solo freelancer has one seat forever, so the metric never grows.

### P-005 — Align the metric with delivered value, not with incurred cost

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: Cost-aligned metrics make your revenue a function of your inefficiency. Every improvement in cost-to-serve then reduces the amount you can charge, so engineering quality and revenue fight each other. Value-aligned metrics invert that: getting better at delivering the unit increases margin without touching price, and getting cheaper at producing it is pure gain.
- **works-when**: There is a customer-side quantity distinct from your resource consumption, and you can hold the mapping between them as an internal parameter you tune as costs move.
- **fails-when**: The value quantity and the cost quantity diverge by more than roughly 5×, so a single price on the value unit either loses money on the tail or overcharges the median. That is the condition that forces either a second metric (P-015) or a hybrid package (Layer 02). It also fails where the buyer is a developer who already thinks in your cost unit and reads the abstraction as obfuscation.
- **observed-effect**: Michelin's per-kilometre model is the cleanest published statement of the mechanism: selling tires means a longer-lasting tire cannibalises revenue; charging per kilometre means a tire that lasts 20% longer earns roughly 20% more revenue (First Round Review, ~2016 — no revenue figures published for the switch). The AI category has moved the same way and said so: *"vendors are increasingly pricing on UX-level units like actions, assists, conversations or generations… this abstracts tokens entirely"* (Flexera, 2026). The counterweight is real and dated: **inference cost has been falling fast enough that a price set at a 2× markup drifts toward 4× or 6× within a year** — a16z's 2024 "LLMflation" analysis estimated roughly **10× annual decline** in inference cost per unit of capability — which is the single largest quiet margin lever in the category, and it only exists if your price is denominated in the value unit rather than the cost unit.
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://review.firstround.com/its-price-before-product-period/ · https://www.flexera.com/blog/ai/ai-consumption-tokens-credits-saas-pricing/ · https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/ai-unit-economics-pricing-margins-services-2026-framework
- **how-to**: Keep the cost meter internally for COGS and margin monitoring, and expose only the value unit to the customer. Hold the value-unit-to-cost-unit ratio as a configuration value you revise quarterly rather than as a published rate. When costs fall, do not automatically pass the whole reduction through — decide deliberately how much of it is a price cut and how much is margin recovery.
- **example-copy**: "100 receipts a month" — never "100,000 processing units a month".
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly prices on receipts and meters tokens. A receipt from a faded thermal printer costs several times a clean PDF, but the customer never sees that; internally the ratio is tracked at p50 and p95 and it is the p95 figure that sets the package (see P-021 and P-022 in Layer 02).

### P-006 — Sell the countable noun the buyer already uses; never credits, never multipliers

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: A unit named after the buyer's output is self-explaining, so the pricing page needs no conversion table and the buyer can price their own workload *before* purchase. A unit named after an internal resource — credits, compute units, tokens — imports an exchange rate, and every exchange rate is a thing the buyer must study, a thing you can silently devalue, and a thing a sophisticated buyer will use to reverse-engineer your markup.
- **works-when**: Your product produces one dominant, countable output, and cost variance per output stays within roughly 3–5×.
- **fails-when**: You genuinely ship many heterogeneous features with incompatible natural units — then an abstraction layer is the lesser evil and you owe the buyer a published burn table (P-026 in Layer 02). It also fails as a *retention and expansion* unit at the very top of a ladder: a multiplier is useless to a first-time buyer and nearly perfect for an upgrading one, because someone who has hit the ceiling knows exactly what their consumption feels like and "5× that" is precisely the information they need. **Do not read this pattern as "multipliers are always wrong" — read it as "a multiplier is an expansion unit, not an acquisition unit."**
- **observed-effect**: Teardown of **7 AI pricing pages, fetched 2026-08-12** (OpenAI/ChatGPT, Claude, Cursor, Midjourney, ElevenLabs, Gamma, Perplexity) found exactly five unit strategies, which sort on one axis — **the more accurately a unit tracks the seller's real COGS, the less the buyer understands it**: the physical peg (ElevenLabs: 1 credit = 1 character), the compute-time unit (Midjourney: fast GPU hours, ≈1 GPU minute per image), the disclosed-variable credit (Gamma: ~50 credits per 10-slide deck, 2–40 per image, 5–10 per refinement), the multiplier (Claude "5× / 20× more usage than Pro"; Cursor; OpenAI Pro), and the adverb ladder (OpenAI's main tiers: "Limited" → "More" → "Expanded" → "Maximum"). **Multiplier tiering is the category default — five of the seven use it somewhere, and the convergence on 5× and 20× specifically is striking.** Two corroborating prescriptions from parties with opposite commercial interests: Software Pricing Partners (2026-04-16, sells consulting that competes with credits) says meter *"a primary operation the customer already names — simulations run, documents processed"* and summarises as *"find the value metric that credits were invented to avoid choosing"*; Metronome (2025-09-22, sells the usage-billing infrastructure credits require) calls cost-plus credits *"born of necessity rather than preference,"* a **bridge, not a destination**, and quotes its own customer: *"Our finance team likes it. Our customers don't know what a credit does."* Vendors that abandoned credits moved toward named units — Fireflies.ai and Synthesia toward transcription and video minutes.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(a 7-page census plus two independent prescriptions from opposed commercial interests; no conversion test of named-unit versus credit pricing exists)*
- **sources**: L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12 · https://softwarepricing.com/blog/credit-based-pricing-ai/ · https://metronome.com/blog/the-rise-of-ai-credits-why-cost-plus-credit-models-work-until-they-dont · https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gamma-pricing · https://flexprice.io/blog/elevenlabs-pricing-breakdown
- **how-to**: Write down the sentence your customer says when they describe getting value — *"I ran an analysis on my competitor"*, *"I turned a shoebox of receipts into a spreadsheet"*. The noun in that sentence is your meter. Print the number on the card. Only introduce an abstraction layer once you genuinely have three or more features with incompatible natural units, and if you do, publish the burn table on the pricing page rather than in a docs page.
- **example-copy**: "25 analyses per month" — not "2,500 credits". · "100 receipts a month" — not "3× the Free allowance".
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly already holds the position six of those seven companies would pay for — a unit that is simultaneously the buyer's unit of value and a countable thing. A freelancer knows how many receipts they get a month before they ever see the product. The temptation will arrive the first time a batch of faded receipts costs 10× a clean one, and the instinct will be to introduce credits so price can track cost. **That trade is refused.** Cursor ran precisely that experiment in public, on a large audience, with a good-faith rationale, and had to apologise and issue refunds (P-009).

### P-007 — The one clean case: a peg that is simultaneously the buyer's count and the cost driver

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: The comprehension-versus-flexibility trade in P-006 has exactly one documented escape. Where the physical quantity the buyer counts *is* the quantity your cost scales with, you can publish a peg that is both perfectly legible and perfectly cost-aligned. You buy that with a permanent constraint: a published peg cannot be changed without an announced repricing.
- **works-when**: Your COGS is close to linear in a quantity the buyer can count in advance — text length, minutes of audio, pages, images. This is a property of your problem domain, not a strategy you can adopt by deciding to.
- **fails-when**: Your workload is not linear in any buyer-countable quantity, which is the normal case. Attempting a peg you cannot honour produces the worst of both worlds: buyers price their workload against a published rate, then discover the rate does not hold, and you must either eat the margin or redenominate (P-038 in Layer 02).
- **observed-effect**: **ElevenLabs is the only clean case in the 7-page census (2026-08-12)**: 1 credit = 1 character on Multilingual v2, **0.5 credits per character** on Flash/Turbo, so a 12,000-character script costs 12,000 credits and the Creator allowance of 121k covers roughly ten of them a month. It works because text-to-speech COGS is unusually close to linear in characters. Overage is published in the same unit: **$0.30 per 1,000 characters on Creator, falling to $0.12 per 1,000 on Business.** The census's own conclusion: *"ElevenLabs escapes the trade because characters happen to be both what the buyer counts and what the cost scales with. That coincidence is a gift of their problem domain, not a strategy anyone else can copy."* Note also that a **time-denominated** unit (Midjourney's fast GPU hours) auto-adjusts to model economics in a way a count cannot — a 3× more expensive model simply consumes 3× the hours with no announcement — which is the opposite trade: high flexibility, low pre-purchase comprehension.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` *(one clean case in a 7-product census; the mechanism generalises, the availability does not)*
- **sources**: L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12 · https://flexprice.io/blog/elevenlabs-pricing-breakdown · https://www.eesel.ai/blog/midjourney-pricing
- **how-to**: Test for the clean case by plotting your measured cost per unit against the buyer-countable quantity across a few hundred real jobs. If the relationship is tight — say within a 2× band across the p10-to-p90 range — publish the peg and accept the repricing constraint. If it is loose, do not publish a peg; publish a named count with a disclosed variance instead (P-018).
- **example-copy**: "1 credit = 1 character" — ElevenLabs, 2026-08-12
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly does **not** have the clean case: cost per receipt varies with image quality and line-item count, not with receipt count. So Ledgerly sells the count ("100 receipts") without pegging it to anything, keeps the token meter internal, and discloses the variance in the FAQ rather than in the unit.

### P-008 — Never denominate a prosumer product in raw tokens

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: Tokens measure your compute, not their value, and no non-developer can forecast them. Token pricing also publishes your markup, because model rates are public — a sophisticated buyer reverse-engineers your margin in an afternoon and negotiates you toward infrastructure cost.
- **works-when**: Never, for a prosumer or SMB buyer. Reserve token metering for an API product whose users already think in tokens and are pricing against other APIs.
- **fails-when**: Immediately. Buyers cannot budget, and the ones who can compute your markup will.
- **observed-effect**: The category has moved the other way, explicitly: *"vendors are increasingly pricing on UX-level units like actions, assists, conversations or generations… this abstracts tokens entirely"* (Flexera, 2026). Software Pricing Partners names cost-structure exposure as the first of six fatal flaws of credit pricing and cites **a $600,000 bill shock that ended an enterprise relationship outright** (2026-04-16 — single unnamed case, and SPP sells the consulting that competes with credits). Raw usage/token metering survives in the census only among API products: OpenAI API, Amazon Bedrock, Anthropic API.
- **evidence**: `strong` *(near-universal practice in the consumer/prosumer segment, with an explicit stated direction of travel from an independent industry source)*
- **sources**: https://www.flexera.com/blog/ai/ai-consumption-tokens-credits-saas-pricing/ · https://softwarepricing.com/blog/credit-based-pricing-ai/
- **how-to**: If you already show tokens, keep the token meter internally for COGS and replace the customer-facing number with a named action. Where you must expose a rate, expose it per action, and hold the token-to-action mapping as an internal parameter you tune as models get cheaper.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly never shows a token count anywhere in the product or on the page. The internal dashboard shows tokens per receipt at p50 and p95; the customer sees "receipts".

### P-009 — Reject metrics that break when the buyer automates

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: A metric is a bet on how the work gets done. Seats bet that humans consume the product one login at a time; a flat plan bets that a human is typing. Automation breaks both bets in the same direction: an agent has no seat, and a successful agent *reduces* the headcount that was your meter — so you get paid less for working better. The exposure is unbounded and arrives suddenly rather than gradually.
- **works-when**: Never for agentic or automatable value. Seats remain legitimate for collaborative human software where AI is incidental and each additional person genuinely gets their own value (CRM, helpdesk, design tools with per-user state).
- **fails-when**: Immediately and structurally, the first time someone points a loop at a flat plan.
- **observed-effect**: Seat-based pricing fell **21% → 15%** of B2B companies in twelve months (Growth Unhinged, survey fielded Apr–May 2025, n=240+), and remains at **29% among companies above $150M ARR** (same author, survey Apr–May 2026, n=230) — a lagging cohort, not a counter-example. The concrete break: from **2026-04-04** Anthropic barred Claude Pro and Max flat plans from powering third-party agent frameworks, with the reported rationale that a single agentic session could consume **$1,000–$5,000 of API cost** (PYMNTS, 2026). Cursor and Replit both revisited pricing in 2025 for the same reason. **The widely repeated companion claim that "seat-based AI pricing produces 40% lower gross margins and 2.3× higher churn" has no traceable primary source or methodology and must not be cited** — see Folklore.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the adoption shift is documented in two consecutive surveys by the same author; the margin and churn deltas are folklore, and the ToS fence is a single vendor's defensive reaction rather than a measured effect)*
- **sources**: https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-b2b-monetization · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026 · https://www.pymnts.com/artificial-intelligence-2/2026/third-party-agents-lose-access-as-anthropic-tightens-claude-usage-rules/ · https://www.productgrowth.blog/p/ai-pricing-credits-vs-seats-vs-outcomes
- **how-to**: Ask what your metric does if the buyer wraps your product in a script. If the answer is "revenue stays flat while cost multiplies", the metric is broken and you need either a consumption dimension or a terms fence. If you already sell seats, keep the seat as the *access* fee and add a consumption dimension for the machine work rather than replacing the seat price outright — that retrofit is what Figma, HubSpot, Adobe and Cursor all did in 2025, adding credit models without killing seats, and it is materially more survivable than a wholesale switch.
- **example-copy**: "Your plan covers receipts you upload yourself. For bulk or programmatic import, use the API plan."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's metric survives automation by construction — a receipt is a receipt whether a human or a script uploads it, and revenue scales with volume either way. The exposure Ledgerly *does* carry is the free tier: 5 receipts a month is cheap to abuse at scale via disposable accounts, which is a fraud problem rather than a metric problem and belongs in the terms (P-037 in Layer 02).

### P-010 — Do not charge per seat unless the second seat creates value

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: Per-seat pricing is the default because it is easy to bill, not because it tracks value. Where a shared login does not degrade the experience, seat pricing taxes collaboration — usually the thing that makes the product sticky — and caps expansion at the customer's headcount growth rather than at their success.
- **works-when**: Each additional user genuinely gets their own value and holds their own state.
- **fails-when**: One account can serve a whole team; the growth loop depends on inviting more people; or cost scales with compute rather than with humans. **And it fails as a question entirely for a single-user consumer product** — a freelancer has one seat forever, so both the seat default and the canon's argument against it are irrelevant.
- **observed-effect**: Seat-based fell **21% → 15%** in twelve months (n=240+, Apr–May 2025). Lovable removed per-seat pricing entirely and moved to credits with "unlimited users at every level" (Stripe customer story, 2025–26 — n=1, and Stripe markets the billing product). **The widely-quoted "about 8 out of 10 companies using per-user pricing should be using a different value metric" is Paddle/ProfitWell marketing with no disclosed method**, and ProfitWell sold the pricing consulting that claim generates — see Folklore.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the adoption shift is well sampled; the prescriptive "80% are wrong" figure is not evidence)*
- **sources**: https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-b2b-monetization · https://www.paddle.com/blog/value-metrics-pricing · https://stripe.com/customers/lovable-elena-verna
- **how-to**: Ask whether a second person on the account changes what the first person can accomplish. If not, seats are a billing convenience masquerading as a value metric. **Cohort warning: this entire pattern is B2B reasoning.** Every large sample behind it surveys B2B software companies, typically $1M–$50M ARR; the ChartMogul study's typical respondent is $1–10M ARR with **$50–$249 monthly ARPU** (n=200, Jan 2026). A sub-$30/month consumer product sits outside every one of those samples, and neither the seat disease nor its cure applies.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly explicitly rejects seats as a metric, and the rejection is stated in the deliverable so it does not read as an oversight. The one place a seat could appear is the accountant seat on Books — which is a *capability* fence for a two-person workflow, not a metric, and should be priced as an add-on rather than as a per-seat rate.

### P-011 — Kill any metric your customer can trivially game

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: Every meter creates an incentive to reduce the metered quantity. If reducing it is cheap and does not reduce the value the customer receives, they will do it, and your revenue falls while your cost does not. The gaming is usually invisible in aggregate — it shows as suppressed usage, which reads like weak engagement rather than like a pricing defect.
- **works-when**: You can run the exercise honestly against your own product before launch.
- **fails-when**: You confuse gaming with legitimate efficiency. A customer who genuinely needs fewer units because your product got better is not gaming you; a customer who batches ten jobs into one to dodge the meter is. The test is whether the workaround costs them anything.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. This is step 4 of Campbell's published screen — verify the metric *"encourages retention rather than workarounds"*, with shared logins and batched exports named as the canonical evasions (Lenny's Newsletter, 2020-10-27). The arbitrage version at the ladder level is documented and checkable: where per-unit price rises as you climb (a **quantity surcharge**), a buyer needing the larger allowance is better off buying two small accounts — the failure Umbrex calls a breach of "arbitrage-resistant" fence design (fetched 2026-08-12). The volume-discount data shows the defensive response: products selling a genuinely identical unit at every tier cluster at **17–33% total entry-to-top discount** (ElevenLabs core **17.5%** across a 200× volume range; Descript AI credits **27.8%**), because a steep curve on an identical unit is an invitation to account-splitting; products that can fence on quality run **58–95%** (PhotoAI **94.8%**, Bannerbear **69.5%**, Runway **58.3%**) — all derived from prices observed 2026-08-12.
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/saas-pricing-strategy · https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/price-fences-framework/ · L15 volume-discount analysis 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Spend ten minutes trying to cheat your own meter. Then check the ladder arithmetic for the account-splitting arbitrage: for every adjacent pair of rungs, confirm per-unit price does not rise as you climb, and confirm that *n* copies of the cheap rung do not beat one copy of the expensive rung. If your unit is identical across tiers, hold your total entry-to-top discount inside the 20–40% band unless you have built a quality fence that makes the cheap tier's unit genuinely worth less.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's gaming vector is compositing several receipts into one image. The fix is a product-side line-item cap rather than a pricing rule, because a pricing rule against it would be unenforceable and would read as suspicious. On the arbitrage side, Ledgerly's entry-to-top discount is $0.09 → $0.0475 per receipt = **47%**, which is above the 20–40% identical-unit band — defensible only if Books' receipts genuinely differ (multi-client tagging, longer history). If they do not, two Solo accounts at $18 beat one Books at $19 for a 200-receipt user, which is close enough to matter.

### P-012 — Test legibility with the mental-arithmetic test, not with your own comprehension

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: You cannot judge your own metric's legibility because you already know the answer. The operational substitute is a behavioural test: can a buyer estimate their own monthly bill in their head, without opening a second page? If not, the metric is a credit system wearing a costume, whatever it is called.
- **works-when**: Any self-serve product where the buyer decides alone in under a minute.
- **fails-when**: Genuinely configured enterprise purchases where a calculator and a conversation are expected — though even there, published scenarios beat a calculator (see P-136 in Layer 05).
- **observed-effect**: Elena Verna's published rule is a **five-second test**: the value metric must be understandable within five seconds of landing on the pricing page (undated, prescriptive, no A/B data behind any item in her eleven-point anatomy). The independent version from the AI lane is the mental-arithmetic form: *"test it by asking whether a buyer can estimate their monthly bill in their head without opening a second page"* (L10 synthesis, 2026-08-12). The census result that shows what failure looks like: Perplexity's "4,000 bonus credits" against an undisclosed baseline was rated **the weakest unit communication of the seven pages** — imprecise *and* confusing, by borrowing the vocabulary of precision without the frame (fetched 2026-08-12). The demand-side counterpart: **78% of IT leaders reported surprise AI-related charges in the past year** (Zylo 2026 SaaS Management Index, n=218 IT leaders — Zylo sells SaaS management software).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(two independent prescriptive sources plus one census observation; no controlled test of metric legibility against conversion exists)*
- **sources**: https://www.elenaverna.com/p/the-dna-of-a-great-pricing-page · L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12 · https://blog.hubspot.com/website/ai-credits-buyers-guide
- **how-to**: Show your pricing card to five people who resemble your buyer and ask one question: "what would this cost you next month?" Time them and record the answer. If more than one of the five cannot answer inside ten seconds, or if their answers disagree by more than 2×, the metric is not legible and the tier ladder built on it will underperform for reasons your analytics will never surface.
- **example-copy**: "What would Ledgerly cost you next month?"
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly passes: a freelancer who says "about 60 receipts a month" reads Solo's 100 and answers "$9" immediately. The failing counterfactual is instructive — had Ledgerly launched with "900 processing credits, 8–15 credits per receipt", the same five people would have needed the burn table, and the top-up would have needed a second calculation.

### P-013 — Translate the unit into the buyer's own frame, on the card

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: A number answers "what does this cost". A translation answers "is this enough for me", which is the question that actually gates the purchase. The translation costs one sentence and converts a quantity into a lived period of work.
- **works-when**: You can name a recognisable usage cadence for your modal buyer.
- **fails-when**: Your usage distribution is so wide that any single translation misleads a large minority — then give a range and label it as one, or give two translations for two named buyer types.
- **observed-effect**: The best line found in the 7-page census is **OpenAI's "128K / ~250 pages of text"**, which converts the seller's unit into the buyer's frame at no cost. Gamma pairs its credit burn table with the whole-output translation — **1,000 credits ≈ 15–20 decks** — which is what makes an otherwise opaque variable credit usable. Microsoft Copilot Studio publishes the raw exchange rate without the translation (**2 credits per generative answer, 10 for tenant graph grounding**) and is correspondingly harder to price against. All observed 2026. **No conversion test of the translation sentence exists.**
- **evidence**: `medium` *(consistent practice among the pages the census rated highest; no measured effect)*
- **sources**: L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12 · https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gamma-pricing · https://blog.hubspot.com/website/ai-credits-buyers-guide
- **how-to**: Write the allowance twice on the card — once as the number, once as the period of work it buys. Derive the translation from your own median customer's actual cadence, not from a round figure that reads well. Put it on the plan card, not in a docs page.
- **example-copy**: "100 receipts a month — about a full month of expenses for a one-person shop."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's Solo card carries "100 receipts a month" and, beneath it, "about a full month of expenses for a one-person shop". Books carries "400 receipts a month — enough for several client books". The translations are bracketed until the median receipt count per paying customer is measured: `[translation unverified — needs the p50 monthly receipt count across the 40 paying customers before it can be stated as fact]`.

### P-014 — Prefer predictability for consumer, value-tracking for B2B

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: Two good metrics often survive the screen and they optimise different things. A value-tracking metric maximises revenue capture as the customer grows; a predictable metric maximises conversion by removing bill anxiety. Which one wins depends on whose money it is. A B2B buyer spends someone else's budget and can absorb variance; a consumer spends post-tax money against an already-crowded mental subscription budget and is structurally more loss-averse.
- **works-when**: You have two surviving candidates and must break the tie.
- **fails-when**: You use it to justify a flat fee on a product with genuinely 50× usage variance — then predictability is bought with a margin inversion you have not priced (see P-022 in Layer 02).
- **observed-effect**: The relevant cohort split: **70% of AI spend comes from customers' existing tech/software budget** in B2B (Growth Unhinged, survey Apr–May 2026, n=230) — the buyer is not spending their own money. Against that, **flat-fee pricing is still 37% among companies under $5M ARR** (same survey), and the ChartMogul cohort note applies: the typical surveyed respondent is $1–10M ARR at **$50–$249 monthly ARPU** (n=200, Jan 2026), which is far above a consumer price point. The only direct consumer cut available in the whole canon: **B2C/hybrid free-to-paid conversion, good 5–7% / great 8–12%**, against B2B-focused at 6–10% / 15–20% (n=200, Jan 2026) — one row inside a B2B study with an undisclosed number of B2C respondents.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the adoption figures are well sampled; the consumer/B2B tie-break rule is an inference from the budget-source data, not a measured effect)*
- **sources**: https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026 · https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/ · https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/saas-pricing-strategy
- **how-to**: When two metrics survive P-004, ask whose budget pays. Consumer and prosumer: take the metric the buyer can forecast, even if it captures less. B2B: take the metric that tracks value, and buy back the predictability with a base fee and a published overage rate rather than by degrading the metric.
- **example-copy**: n/a (structural)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyer pays with their own post-tax money, so predictability wins the tie. Receipts is both the value-tracking and the predictable candidate, which is why the metric was never in doubt — the difficulty for Ledgerly is entirely in the packaging (Layer 02), not in the metric.

### P-015 — Add a second, depth metric rather than abstracting the first

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: One count cannot carry both volume and cost when the cost of a single unit varies widely. The reflex is to abstract the unit into credits so the price can track cost. The better move is to keep the countable noun and add a *second, bounded* dimension — depth, size, or quality — which does three jobs at once: it gives the free tier a real fence that is not just a small number, it creates an upgrade reason for the low-volume/high-need buyer a pure count cannot monetise, and it bounds your worst-case cost per unit, which is the thing that actually hurts you.
- **works-when**: Unit cost varies mainly along one nameable dimension (input size, sources consulted, output length, model quality) that the buyer also recognises as a capability.
- **fails-when**: You add a third and fourth dimension. Two nouns are learnable; a matrix of exchange rates is a credit system with extra steps. Also fails when the second dimension is invisible to the buyer, in which case it reads as an arbitrary restriction rather than as a tier feature.
- **observed-effect**: **Gamma's dual unit is the most transferable structure in the census** (2026-08-12): variable credits meter cost while "Up to 10 slides per prompt" versus "Up to 100 slides per prompt" communicates capability. **Photoroom scales its cost meter and its value meter at different rates** — AI credits **4.7×** across tiers, exports **10×** (observed 2026-08-12) — i.e. the value-side meter is deliberately made the faster-growing one. Midjourney's structure is the same idea in time units: metered fast GPU hours plus an unmetered slow lane. The volume-discount data supports the mechanism from the other side: only products that can fence on *quality* sustain a steep entry-to-top curve (PhotoAI **94.8%**), while identical-unit products are stuck in the **17–33%** band.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(three independent live products running the structure; no controlled comparison against a single-metric ladder)*
- **sources**: L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12 · L15 teardowns and volume-discount analysis 2026-08-12 · https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gamma-pricing
- **how-to**: Name the depth dimension in the buyer's language and put a number on it per tier. Scale the *value* meter faster than the *cost* meter across the ladder — the count modestly, the capability aggressively — so the upgrade argument rests on the dimension that costs you least. Then check that the free tier's depth limit sits below the point where your worst-case unit cost becomes painful.
- **example-copy**: "Free: 5 receipts a month, up to 10 line items each. Solo: 100 receipts a month, up to 100 line items each."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's depth dimension is line items per receipt, which is both the cost driver (more line items, more output tokens, more validation) and a real capability difference (a restaurant bill versus a supermarket shop). Free at 10 line items caps the worst case that abuse would otherwise exploit; Solo at 100 covers essentially every real receipt. The 10× jump on depth is a more persuasive upgrade argument than the 20× jump on count, and it is the jump that protects margin.

### P-016 — Charge on an outcome only above roughly 80% success, and define it verifiably

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: An outcome metric puts the failure cost on you. Below a high success rate you eat the cost of every failed attempt while booking nothing, and revenue becomes a function of model quality you do not fully control. Above it, an outcome is the tightest possible alignment between what you charge and what the buyer values, and it commands the highest prices in the category — which is why it is the ceiling every activity metric is eventually measured against.
- **works-when**: The outcome is discrete, attributable to your system, machine-verifiable, and you can absorb cost variance on failures.
- **fails-when**: The outcome is fuzzy or contested. **The entire fight in this category is over what counts as a success**, and definitions tighten over time, which mechanically reduces billable volume on existing contracts.
- **observed-effect**: Live outcome prices, 2026: **Intercom Fin at $0.99 per resolution** (and "Fin for platforms" at **$49/mo including 50 resolutions**, then $0.99 each); **Zendesk at ~$1.50 per automated resolution committed, ~$2 pay-as-you-go**, narrowed in a **May 2026** restructure to *Verified* Resolutions confirmed by LLM evaluation; Sierra, Decagon and Ada on custom enterprise contracts of $50k–$600k+/yr. Salesforce Agentforce originally charged **$2 per conversation** — including failures — and pivoted to flexible credits after customer backlash. Adoption: **5% in mid-2025, with 25% expecting it by 2028** (Growth Unhinged, survey Apr–May 2025, n=240+ — an intentions survey, and intention surveys in pricing are historically optimistic). The ≥80% success threshold is Product Growth's stated criterion (2026-03-23, updated 2026-08-09), not a measured breakpoint. **Cohort and interest warning: the richest comparison source, fin.ai, is Intercom's, and Intercom sells the $0.99/resolution model it benchmarks.**
- **evidence**: `medium` *(several live implementations with published rates; the success-rate threshold is one analyst's heuristic and the adoption forecast is an intentions survey)*
- **sources**: https://fin.ai/learn/ai-customer-service-agent-pricing-comparison · https://www.productgrowth.blog/p/ai-pricing-credits-vs-seats-vs-outcomes · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-b2b-monetization
- **how-to**: Write the outcome definition as if you were drafting an SLA a customer will dispute, then build the verification *before* you build the billing. Zendesk's move to *Verified* Resolutions is the tell: the second-generation version of every outcome price adds a verification layer, so build it first. If you cannot verify it today, ship an activity metric and instrument the outcome so you can convert later — that instrumentation is the difference between cost-plus economics and outcome economics.
- **example-copy**: "You're charged when Ledgerly files a categorised entry your accountant accepts — not when it tries."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's outcome is a *correctly categorised* entry, and it is not yet chargeable: correctness is judged by the freelancer, not by the system, so it is contestable and unverifiable today. The right move is to log the downstream signal now — did the user edit the category after processing? — so that in twelve months there is a verifiable definition and a measured accuracy rate to price against. Until then Ledgerly charges on receipts, and the deliverable states explicitly that this caps its ceiling at cost-plus economics.

### P-017 — If you cannot explain the metric in one line, use a flat fee

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: Metric maximalism has a cost the canon rarely concedes: comprehension. At a low price point the buyer's decision budget is seconds, and a comprehensible flat price can out-convert a theoretically superior usage metric by more than the metric's extra capture is worth. The flat fee is not a failure state; it is a legitimate answer at small scale, and it becomes wrong only when your usage distribution is skewed enough to invert the margin.
- **works-when**: Usage variance across customers is low; the price point is low enough that the buyer will not deliberate; or you are pre-scale and cannot yet meter reliably.
- **fails-when**: Usage is genuinely skewed and each unit has real marginal cost. Then a flat fee does not fail gradually — it inverts, and the inversion arithmetic is in P-022 in Layer 02. Never choose a flat fee on comprehension grounds without running that stress test first.
- **observed-effect**: **Flat-fee pricing is 37% among companies under $5M ARR**, against 22% overall in the prior year's wave (Growth Unhinged, survey Apr–May 2026, n=230; and Apr–May 2025, n=240+). The canon's own data cuts against its metric maximalism here, and its authors mostly advise companies past that stage. At the indie end the same conclusion appears from practice rather than survey: TinyWow charges **$20/mo** and fences its free tier on ads, CAPTCHAs and queue position while giving away every tool; Bannerbear, at 2.5× that price point, **refuses to sell overage at all** — verbatim, *"It is not possible to go over your monthly image / video API credits limit"* — because simplicity is worth more than the marginal revenue (both observed 2026-08-12).
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-state-of-b2b-monetization-in-2026 · L15 teardowns 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Try to write the metric as a single line on a plan card with no asterisk. If you cannot, and your usage variance is inside roughly 3×, ship the flat fee and revisit at scale. If you cannot *and* your variance is wide, you do not have a flat-fee product — you have a metric problem you are avoiding, and it will surface as a margin problem.
- **example-copy**: "$9 a month. Everything included."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly can write its metric in one line, so the flat fee is not needed — but it is worth recording as the rejected alternative. A flat $12/mo for unlimited receipts would be simpler and would very likely convert better at 2,000 monthly visitors; it is refused because the margin arithmetic in P-022 shows a heavy user at 400 receipts inverting it, and Ledgerly has no way to identify heavy users before they sign up.

### P-018 — Disclose the variance in the unit rather than hiding it

- **layer**: `value-metric`
- **mechanism**: A named count with variable cost will eventually deliver fewer units than the buyer expected. Disclosing the variance in advance costs a little purchase-time clarity and buys immunity from the grievance; discovering it costs the relationship. The asymmetry is stark because the discovered version arrives at exactly the moment the buyer feels cheated.
- **works-when**: Your unit varies in cost or in consumption and you can characterise the variance honestly in one sentence.
- **fails-when**: The disclosure is so hedged that it functions as a blanket disclaimer, or it is buried in terms rather than sitting near the number it qualifies. A disclosure nobody reads before purchase provides no protection and no honesty.
- **observed-effect**: Gamma publishes both the burn table (**~50 credits per 10-slide deck; 2–40 credits per AI image by quality; 5–10 per refinement**) and the whole-output translation (**1,000 credits ≈ 15–20 decks**), and states the variance rather than hiding it (2026). The census's opposite case: **Cursor's June 2025 change removed the request counter from the UI while redefining "unlimited"; users reported all-day capacity shrinking to about an hour; Cursor issued a public apology on 2025-07-04 and refunded unexpected charges from mid-June to early July.** The category-wide observation from the same census: **nobody explains their COGS on a pricing page** — the closest anyone comes is post-hoc damage control, and the category has apparently decided that explaining cost structure to consumers reads as excuse-making. The FAQ is where disclosure actually lives: *"the card sells, the FAQ discloses."*
- **evidence**: `medium` *(one clean positive case and one well-documented public failure; no controlled test)*
- **sources**: L12 AI-tool teardown census 2026-08-12 · https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gamma-pricing · https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/cursor-ai-pricing/ · https://www.vantage.sh/blog/cursor-pricing-explained
- **how-to**: Write one sentence in the buyer's register naming what makes a unit consume more than expected, and put it in the FAQ directly under the allowance question. Keep the card clean. Never let the first signal of variance be a customer noticing they ran out early.
- **example-copy**: "A receipt is a receipt — but a 40-line supermarket till roll counts as two."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's disclosure sentence sits in the FAQ under "What happens if I go over 100 receipts?": long multi-page receipts count as more than one, with the threshold stated as a line-item number rather than as a vague warning. That sentence is uncomfortable to write and correct to publish.

## Interactions

**P-002 (quantity vs capability) versus Layer 03 (tier architecture) — this is the layer's main handoff.** The fence choice made here determines the shape of the entire ladder, and the two layers fail together if the choice is left implicit. A quantity fence produces a ladder whose feature lists should be *identical* across consumer rungs so the upgrade decision has exactly one variable; a capability fence produces a ladder whose volume allowances should be roughly equal so the decision is about what you can do, not how much. A ladder that varies both at once is not a compromise — it is two ladders overlaid, and the buyer resolves the ambiguity by leaving. If you cannot decide, the honest reading is that you have two segments (P-015) and should say so in the deliverable.

**P-002 versus P-015 (second metric) versus P-017 (flat fee).** These three are the escape hatches for the same problem — one metric cannot carry the load — and they are ordered by cost. P-017 is cheapest and gives up capture. P-015 costs one extra number on the card and preserves capture. Abstracting into credits (rejected by P-006, packaged in P-025 in Layer 02) is the most expensive because it costs comprehension permanently. Try them in that order, not in the order your COGS spreadsheet suggests.

**P-005 (value alignment) versus P-021/P-022 (COGS floor and inversion, Layer 02).** Deliberately in tension. This layer says charge on the value unit and manage the cost gap elsewhere; the packaging layer says the cost gap can invert your margin and must be priced. The resolution is sequencing, not compromise: choose the metric on value grounds here, then let the packaging layer decide the *shape* — allowance, overage, degraded lane — that makes the value metric survivable. **Never let the COGS spreadsheet choose the metric.** That is how products end up selling tokens.

**P-006 (named unit) versus P-007 (the peg) versus P-013 (translation).** These form a ladder of decreasing purity. If your cost is linear in a buyer-countable quantity, publish the peg and accept the repricing constraint. If it is not, sell the count and translate it. If you cannot even do that honestly, you are in credit territory and owe a published burn table. The mistake is jumping from the top of that ladder to the bottom the first time COGS misbehaves.

**P-009 (automation-proof metrics) versus P-010 (seats) versus Layer 02's terms fence (P-037).** P-009 and P-010 are the same finding read at two different time scales — seats are a metric that was already broken for collaboration and became structurally broken with agents. Neither is fully fixable inside the metric, because the exposure is behavioural rather than dimensional: the terms fence in Layer 02 is the instrument that actually contains it, and it must be written before launch rather than after the abuse.

**P-012/P-013 (legibility) versus P-005 (value alignment).** The census finding is that these trade off almost perfectly: the more accurately a unit tracks your COGS, the less the buyer understands it. There is exactly one documented exception (P-007). Everyone else must choose, and the choice sorts by whether the buyer can estimate their own volume *in advance* — voice work and image generation can, chat and coding agents cannot. Decide which kind of buyer you have before deciding which side of the trade to take.

**P-016 (outcomes) versus P-001 (metric first).** Outcome pricing is the ceiling of this layer and the reason not to be complacent about a good activity metric. An activity metric caps you at roughly cost-plus economics; the path up is to find the downstream result your activity produces and eventually charge for that. That path requires instrumentation started *now*, which is a Phase 8 deliverable driven by a Phase 3 decision.

## Where this layer is thin

- **There is no controlled test anywhere in the public record comparing a quantity fence against a capability fence on the same product.** P-002 — the centrepiece of this layer — rests on a taxonomy drawn from live pricing pages plus one aggregator-sourced experiment (Athenic, 100 → 500 free tasks, −60% conversion). The mechanism is strong and the evidence is thin, and that gap should be stated in any deliverable that leans on it.
- **No published study measures the effect of changing a value metric.** Every canonical example — Michelin, Optimizely, HubSpot's events-to-contacts switch — is a narrated success with no counterfactual, no before/after conversion data, and obvious survivorship exposure. What we have is a mechanism with a good causal story, which is what `medium` means.
- **The whole canon is B2B, and mostly $1M–$50M ARR.** Every large sample cited in this layer (n=200, n=230, n=240) surveys B2B software companies with a sales or sales-assist motion available, and so does the one larger survey the library carries elsewhere (the n=800+ benchmarks survey tabulated in `benchmarks.md`, which no pattern here cites). A sub-$30/month consumer product sits at the extreme edge of, or entirely outside, all of them. The single direct consumer figure in the literature is one row: **B2C/hybrid free-to-paid, good 5–7% / great 8–12%** (n=200, Jan 2026).
- **No source publishes a defensible sample size for willingness-to-pay research.** The "minimum 100 respondents, 100–150 per segment" guidance that dominates search results traces only to AI-generated sites. Ramanujam's public answer is deliberately anti-methodological ("talk to at least one person, because most companies are not doing even that"). `[unsourced — would need a market-research methods text or Simon-Kucher's published methodology to state a defensible n]`
- **Metric legibility has never been measured against conversion.** The five-second test and the mental-arithmetic test are prescriptive heuristics from credentialed operators with no A/B data behind them. It is entirely plausible that legibility matters far more at consumer price points than at the B2B prices where the advice was written, and nobody has checked.
- **The AI-pricing evidence base is one year old and moving.** Every figure in the AI-adjacent patterns here is dated 2025–2026 and several are already contradicted by their own publishers a year later. The gross-margin band, the credit adoption rate, and the outcome-pricing forecast are all moving targets; treat any single-year level as directional and re-derive quarterly.
- **A solo founder at ~2,000 monthly visitors cannot test a metric at all.** Metric changes are not A/B-testable at that scale — they change the whole page and the whole product experience. The honest posture is to choose the metric on mechanism, run P-012 against five real prospects, instrument the metric's distribution across existing users from day one, and defer the question until the distribution itself tells you something.
