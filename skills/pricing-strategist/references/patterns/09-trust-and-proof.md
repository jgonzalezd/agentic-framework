# Layer 09 — Trust and Proof

**Layer slug:** `trust-proof` · **Loaded at:** Phase 6

This layer governs everything on the page whose job is to answer *why should I believe you* — social proof and where it sits, credibility numbers, testimonials, ratings, badges, press and awards, security signals at the payment moment, privacy positioning for products that ask for intimate disclosure, and credential substitutes for advice products with no clinicians on staff. The thing that goes wrong most often is not weak proof; it is **proof that does not exist yet being invented anyway**. A product with no customers cannot show a customer count, a rating or a testimonial, and a page that shows one is lying in a way that is checkable, embarrassing and — where it misdescribes the product's data handling or its commercial terms — regulator-visible. The second most common failure is proof that is real but placed where the objection is not, so it never reaches the moment it was built for.

## How to use this layer

Read **P-236 first if the product has no customers** — it is the only pattern in the library written for that case, and it changes which of the others are even available to you. Otherwise the load-bearing three are P-221 (proof adjacent to the objection), P-223 (visual security at the card field) and P-228 (audit the numbers before you print them); everything else is situational. **P-229 through P-231 apply only to products that ask the buyer to disclose something they would not say out loud** — journals, therapy-adjacent tools, decision help, health, finance — where privacy is not a compliance footnote but the gate on whether the input box gets filled at all. **P-232 through P-235 are the credential-substitute set**, for advice and insight products where the obvious proof (a licensed human) does not exist. In a COLD regime, treat every `observed-effect` here as a prior only: none of the placement findings were measured at low traffic, and a product with 2,000 monthly visitors cannot power a proof-placement test.

## Patterns

### P-221 — Put the proof where the objection fires

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: Objections are triggered by specific page elements — the price triggers "is this worth it", the card field triggers "can I trust you with this", the commitment triggers "can I get out". Proof delivered anywhere else arrives before the objection exists or after the buyer has left. Adjacency, not volume, is what makes proof work.
- **works-when**: the page has an identifiable moment of maximum doubt — a price block, a plan card, a payment step — and you own real proof you can put beside it.
- **fails-when**: the proof is thin. Three testimonials with stock photos placed next to the price actively signal that nobody uses the product; proximity amplifies whatever the proof already says, including its weakness. Also fails when the same proof is repeated at every anchor until it reads as decoration.
- **observed-effect**: Covenanteyes.com — home page with six reassurances added, 4 variations tested: **+17% paid accounts** (GoodUI Datastory #20, Dec 2015, consumer subscription; sample size, duration and significance are paywalled). GoodUI's published free result for adding social counts: **+3.1% conversions** (GoodUI blog, 2016-09-22, no sample published). Paltalk — plans split across folds with no social proof → all plans above the fold in a comparison table **plus testimonials**: **+6.74% successful purchases**, revenue uplift "more than USD 2,000", 53 days, 50/50 split of all incoming traffic, significance not stated (VWO case study, B2C virtual-goods subscription, accessed Aug 2026) — **the layout change and the testimonials were bundled and are not separable**.
- **evidence**: `medium`
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/datastories/ ; https://goodui.org/blog/adding-social-counts-increases-conversions/ ; https://vwo.com/success-stories/paltalk/
- **how-to**: Write down the three objections a buyer has at the instant they see the price, and answer each one inside the price block rather than on a trust page or a testimonial band 2,000px down. Move your single strongest piece of proof from the top of the landing page to sit beside the plan cards. Calibrate to the **+3.1%** GoodUI actually published, not to the datastory headlines — GoodUI's own +191% signups figure came from replacing a modal popup, a different intervention entirely.
- **example-copy**: "Refunded in full within 30 days, no questions asked — email is enough."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's guarantee currently lives in the FAQ at the bottom of the pricing page. Move it under the Solo and Books CTAs as one line each. With ~2,000 monthly visitors and ~40 paying customers, Ledgerly cannot test this: detecting the +3.1% GoodUI published on a base of roughly 40 conversions/month would take years. Ship it on the mechanism and instrument the CTA click as a diagnostic only.

### P-222 — Keep the proof visually subordinate to the CTA

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: Proof and the call to action compete for the same fixation. A logo wall in full brand colour beside a form is a set of bright, high-contrast rectangles that pull the eye away from the one element you need clicked. Desaturating the proof preserves its credibility function while returning attention to the button.
- **works-when**: the proof is visually loud — colour logos, photographs, star graphics, badges — and sits within the same visual block as the primary CTA.
- **fails-when**: the proof is already quiet (plain text counts, small type), where further suppression just makes it unreadable. Also fails as a general licence to hide proof; the tested change was a *colour* change, not a removal.
- **observed-effect**: WorkZone — customer logos converted from colour to **black and white** beside the demo request form: **+34% form submissions, 22 days, 99% significance**, sample size not published (VWO, B2B SaaS, accessed Aug 2026). This is the best-documented result in the whole proof cluster and it is about *toning proof down*, not adding more.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (one company, one test, no published sample — but with an unusually complete significance and duration record for this literature)
- **sources**: https://vwo.com/blog/ab-testing-examples/
- **how-to**: Render logo walls, press marks and rating graphics in a single muted colour or greyscale, at a size that reads as supporting evidence rather than as a second headline. The CTA should remain the highest-contrast object in its block. If a piece of proof cannot survive being desaturated, it was decoration.
- **worked-example**: If Ledgerly ever earns accounting-press mentions, render them as grey wordmarks under the tier cards, not as full-colour logos above them. At Ledgerly's scale the WorkZone result is unrunnable as a test — it is a design rule, not a hypothesis.

### P-223 — Reinforce security visually at the card field, not in the footer

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: Users do not assess actual security. They form a gut judgement from how secure the page *looks*, and they form it at the moment of maximum exposure — the card-number field. A footer badge is not present at that moment; a visual treatment on the field itself is.
- **works-when**: self-serve checkout for a brand the buyer does not already know — which is every early-stage product taking payment on its own domain.
- **fails-when**: established brands with existing trust, where security signalling adds little and can read as protesting too much. Also fails when badges are scattered site-wide rather than placed at the point of anxiety, and fails when the visual treatment is applied to a field the payment processor renders inside an iframe you cannot restyle.
- **observed-effect**: **19%** of US shoppers abandoned in the past three months because they "didn't trust the site with their credit card information", and **18%** explicitly look for security indicators before entering card details (Baymard, n=1,026 US adults demographically matched to the US adult internet population, 2025). Site adoption of visual reinforcement at the card fields: **89% of major US e-commerce sites failing (2012) → 66% failing (2016)** — still a majority. Baymard's finding across seven years of moderated testing is that the determining factor is the **visual robustness of the payment section**, not the presence of a badge somewhere on the page.
- **evidence**: `medium` (a repeated large-N quantitative survey plus 25 rounds of moderated testing over 4,400+ participant-site sessions and 54 benchmark rounds across 344 sites, with published methodology — but every one of those studies is Baymard's, so this is one organisation's corpus and cannot carry `strong` under this library's rule. It remains the best-evidenced item in this layer, and it is self-report and e-commerce.)
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/blog/perceived-security-of-payment-form ; https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate ; https://baymard.com/research/checkout-usability
- **how-to**: Give the card-number field a visually distinct container — a shaded panel, a border, an inline lock glyph — so the security cue is adjacent to the anxiety rather than in the footer. Add one line of microcopy at the field itself. Note the cohort limit before transferring this: Baymard's corpus is e-commerce checkout, not SaaS self-serve purchase, though the card field is the element that transfers most cleanly.
- **example-copy**: "Encrypted in your browser. We never see or store your card number."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly takes payment through a hosted provider component. The card field itself cannot be restyled, so the treatment goes around it: a bordered panel containing the field, the payment-brand marks under the button, and the microcopy line above. If the provider's hosted page is used instead, the security signalling is inherited — which is one of the arguments in P-246.

### P-224 — Pick a seal by consumer brand recognition, and read the 49% as the real finding

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: Seal trust is transferred consumer-brand familiarity, not an assessment of a certificate authority. Users trust the anti-virus brands they already know, which is why recognisability beats cryptographic merit.
- **works-when**: small, unknown merchants with a genuine trust deficit to close, taking card details on their own domain.
- **fails-when**: established brands — the same research finds minimal impact where customers already trust the site. Fails harder as a substitute for the page's own security design (P-223), which is doing most of the work.
- **observed-effect**: Baymard seal survey (2,510 responses, 1,286 valid seal selections, US adults, Google Consumer Surveys, run 2013-01-09→11, with re-runs through 2023): Norton **~36%**, McAfee **~23%**, TRUSTe **~13%**, BBB Accredited **~13%**, Thawte ~5%, Trustwave ~3%, GeoTrust ~3%, Comodo ~3% — and **49% chose "don't know / no preference"**. In later rounds **a homemade/fake seal outperformed most established SSL vendor seals**. GoodUI pattern **#63 "Trust Seals", 7 tests** (Swimsuitsforall 66,919 visitors 2018; Kenhub.com 3,516; Menufy.com 14,269; test #600 7,986 visitors 2025-07-18 adding four accepted-card icons) — **all medians paywalled, rendered as the literal string `X.X%`**.
- **evidence**: `medium` (large survey, but stated-preference, US-only, originally 2013; the 49% "no preference" majority caps how much the ranking can mean, and the GoodUI effect sizes are unobtainable)
- **sources**: https://baymard.com/blog/site-seal-trust ; https://goodui.org/patterns/63/
- **how-to**: If you display a seal, choose the consumer-recognisable brand over the technically superior certificate. Then read the 49% as the primary result: for half of users the seal is noise, so spend the effort on P-223 instead. **Do not fabricate a seal.** It tests well precisely because users cannot distinguish it, which is a finding about user vulnerability, not a licence — and see P-237.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyer is a freelancer entering a card on an unknown site, so a recognisable mark has a plausible role. But at ~40 customers, the accepted-card marks under the pay button cost nothing and carry more of the signal than a purchased seal would. Skip the paid seal; spend the money on nothing.

### P-225 — Prefer a specific, precisely-odd count to an adjective

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: A round number reads as marketing rounding; an odd, precise number reads as a live query against a real database. "5,600 licensed providers" carries a claim about the existence of a system that counted them, which an adjective ("thousands of trusted providers") does not.
- **works-when**: you have a real count large enough not to embarrass you, and a way to keep it current.
- **fails-when**: the count is small — a precise "47 customers" is honest and unpersuasive, and precision then draws attention to the number you were hoping to gloss. Also fails when the precise number is hard-coded and drifts out of date (P-228).
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Observed practice: Talkspace publishes "**4,999,000** therapy sessions", "**1,199,000** members helped", "**5,600** licensed providers", "1 Million+ Users", "More than 60,000 5-star reviews"; 16Personalities publishes "**1.56B+** Total tests taken", "**197M+** Tests taken in United States", "**91.2%** Accuracy rating"; MentorCruise publishes "97% satisfaction rate", "42,000+ Matches made", "an average rating of 4.9 out of 5" (all observed 2026-08-12). On pricing pages specifically: Grammarly's subhead "Trusted by over 40 million people and 50,000 organizations", Todoist's `[unverified — transcribed as "337K+ ★★★★★ reviews", but the identical token "337K+" was transcribed the same day for an unrelated 16Personalities metric (see P-226), so one of the two is almost certainly a copy error and neither can be re-fetched. Do not print or benchmark against this figure until Todoist's page is fetched again and the count re-read.]`, Linear's "Trusted by more than 40,000 companies" — three of 33 pages transcribed on 2026-08-12 spend the subhead purely on proof, which means those companies believe credibility rather than comprehension is the blocker at that moment.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (prevalence observation across two independent teardown corpora; no controlled test of precision versus rounding was located)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus, observed 2026-08-12 ; L17 33-page pricing-page transcription, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Query the number rather than typing it, and print it at the precision the query returns. Note the tell in 16Personalities' "91.2% Accuracy rating" — a self-reported, precisely-decimalised claim that functions as a science signal without being one; use precision on facts you can defend, not to dress up a self-assessment. If the honest number is small, do not print it — go to P-236 instead.
- **example-copy**: "1,482 freelancers processed 214,000 receipts through Ledgerly last month." *(illustrative structure only — Ledgerly's real figures are 40 customers; do not print this)*
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly has ~40 paying customers and ~2,000 monthly visitors. "Trusted by 40 freelancers" is true and actively harmful. The only count Ledgerly can honestly print at this scale is a *usage* count if it is large — receipts processed rather than customers. If 40 customers at the Solo allowance of 100 receipts/month were even half-utilised, that is roughly 2,000 receipts a month, and "Over 20,000 receipts processed" becomes available after ten months of that run rate. Until the number exists, print nothing.

### P-226 — Put a live-activity counter beside the cumulative total

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: A cumulative total proves the product existed; a today-counter proves it is alive *right now*. The two answer different doubts, and the second is the one a buyer has about a product they have never heard of.
- **works-when**: genuine daily volume large enough that the number is never embarrassing and never zero.
- **fails-when**: low or spiky volume — a counter that reads "3 today" is worse than no counter, and one that reads the same number every day is transparently fake. Live-activity proof requires real volume, and thin versions of it read as manufactured.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. 16Personalities pairs "**1.56B+** Total tests taken" with a today-counter `[unverified — transcribed as "337K+ Tests taken today", but the identical token "337K+" was transcribed the same day for Todoist's review count (see P-225), two unrelated metrics at two unrelated companies, so one of the two is almost certainly a copy error and neither can be re-fetched. The pairing of a cumulative total with a today-counter is the observation that stands; the magnitude of the today-figure is not reportable until the page is fetched again.]`. The today-figure is the one a cumulative total cannot substitute for, and that is the whole of what this pattern rests on.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (single observed implementation, no test)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus, 16personalities.com, observed 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Only ship this if the daily number is genuinely large and genuinely computed. Never fake motion — a rotating "someone in Denver just subscribed" widget with no underlying event stream is fabricated proof under P-237.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly at ~2,000 monthly visitors and ~40 customers has no daily number worth showing. This pattern is unavailable until volume roughly two orders of magnitude higher exists; note it as a thing to add later, not a gap to fill now.

### P-227 — Substantiate the badge, or downgrade the claim

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: "Most popular" is a falsifiable factual claim about your customer base. "Recommended" and "Best value" are editorial opinions and cannot be falsified. Labelling a tier "most popular" when it is not your modal plan is a lie a competitor, a journalist or a customer can check — and the honest version is strictly stronger, because a stated percentage is proof while a badge is an assertion.
- **works-when**: three or more tiers where the buyer has no strong prior about which fits.
- **fails-when**: two tiers (nothing to disambiguate), or when two tiers carry badges — which destroys the signal and happens in production. Also fails with sophisticated buyers when the badged plan is transparently the one that benefits you and the copy gives no reason.
- **observed-effect**: Badges appear in **38.2% (115 of 301)** detected pricing experiments across 24 companies (Lazyweb Research, July 2026; the author flags this as "a lower bound, counting mentions not audited pages" and states these are "detected UI differences with inferred reasoning, not measured conversion lift"). **Cohort label: that 301-experiment pool is dominated by mobile paywall screens — the same study reports a sub-pool of 255 mobile "All plans" screens (see P-135 in `05-page-anatomy.md`) — so 38.2% is mobile-paywall prevalence, not pricing-page prevalence.** In a 33-page transcription (2026-08-12) the market split roughly evenly between factual badges (9 instances) and opinion badges (9), and **Grammarly badges two tiers "Most popular" while Dropbox badges two "Best Value"**. The substantiated alternative: Intercom "suggests that a particular plan was chosen by **53% of businesses**" (via Smashing Magazine, 2022-07, https://bfy.intercom.com/). **No badge lift figure is defensible** — see Folklore.
- **evidence**: `medium` (prevalence is well documented across two corpora; the effect is not measured anywhere located)
- **sources**: https://www.lazyweb.com/research/most-popular-badge-prevalence-pricing-pages ; L17 33-page transcription 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Query your own subscription table. If the tier you want to promote is genuinely the modal plan, say "Most popular". If it is not, say "Recommended" or "Best value". If you can compute the actual share, print the share instead of the badge — it is proof rather than a label. Badge exactly one tier per visible grid.
- **example-copy**: "Chosen by 6 in 10 freelancers who process more than 100 receipts a month."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly has 40 customers across Solo ($9/100) and Books ($19/400). A share statement computed on 40 accounts is technically true and statistically meaningless, and printing "63% choose Solo" invites a buyer to ask 63% of what. At this scale use "Recommended for most freelancers" on Solo, and revisit the share claim when the denominator is large enough to survive being asked about.

### P-228 — Audit every proof number for staleness and internal contradiction before it ships

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: Proof numbers are typed once and then live forever in a template. They drift from the truth in both directions — understating (which wastes the asset) and overstating (which is a false claim) — and different pages of the same site drift differently, producing self-contradiction that a buyer comparing two pages can see.
- **works-when**: always, before printing any count, rating or percentage.
- **fails-when**: never — though the fix is to generate the number, not to check it more often.
- **observed-effect**: Documented drift in the teardown corpus, all observed 2026-08-12: **Finch's homepage says "500k+ ratings" against an actual App Store count of 738,701, and rounds a 4.947 rating up to 5.0**. **Day One says "Over 150,000 5-star reviews" on its homepage but "more than 200,000" on its privacy pledge page.** **Rosebud's own page is internally inconsistent** — separate extraction passes over the same page surfaced "150K+ users", "35,311 reviews" and "735,311 ratings", conflicting with the "Trusted by 100K+ users" and 4.9-stars-across-5K-reviews figures by two orders of magnitude; the lane's verdict was that only "4.9 stars" and "Trusted by 100K+ users" are reportable, and both are single-source.
- **evidence**: `medium` (three independent products in one corpus, all first-party pages, directly observed on one date)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus — finchcare.com, dayoneapp.com, rosebud.app, plus Apple App Store listings, all observed 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Keep one canonical query per proof number and render every instance from it. Grep the whole site for each figure before a launch and reconcile the hits. When a store rating is the source, print the store's number to the store's precision — rounding 4.947 to 5.0 converts a real fact into a false one for no gain. Never round *up* into a claim you cannot support.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's landing page, pricing page and app-store-style listing would each carry a receipts-processed figure. Compute it in one place from the receipts table and interpolate it; do not paste it. At 40 customers this is a habit worth forming before the numbers get big enough to matter.

### P-229 — For disclosure products, put the privacy claim beside the input box, in a verb the user can feel

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: For a product that asks someone to write out something they would not say out loud, privacy determines whether the box gets filled at all. The hesitation happens at the cursor, not on the policy page, so the claim has to be adjacent to the cursor. And a destruction verb the user can picture outperforms an abstract one: "shred" is visceral in a way "delete" is not.
- **works-when**: the product requires intimate disclosure before it can deliver value — journals, therapy-adjacent tools, decision help, health, personal finance — and you have a privacy property that is actually true.
- **fails-when**: the claim is not backed by the architecture. The hedge is legible: a product that must read the user's text to run a model over it cannot claim it is unable to read it, and the copy will show the seam (P-230). Also fails if the claim is repeated so often it starts raising the doubt it was meant to close.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for placement. Documented remediation-as-feature: after its **$7.8 million FTC settlement in 2023 for sharing user data with Facebook and Snapchat** `[secondary — simplypsychology, 2026; healthline, 2026]`, BetterHelp shipped a **"shred"** button letting users *"permanently remove all conversations."* Contrast Talkspace, where deletion requires contacting support and *"filling out various forms,"* which helpguide judged *"could've been made simpler"* `[secondary, helpguide, updated 2026-04-22]`.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (copy and product observation across one product neighbourhood; no conversion measurement of privacy placement exists in this corpus)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus, observed 2026-08-12 ; secondary carriers: simplypsychology.com, healthline.com, helpguide.org
- **how-to**: Put one plain sentence immediately adjacent to the field where the user types the sensitive thing — not in the footer, not in the FAQ. Name the mechanism, name what leaves the device, and give the user a destruction affordance with a verb they can feel. Then make the affordance real and instant; a shred button that files a support ticket is worse than no button.
- **example-copy**: "The photo is read in your browser and thrown away. We keep the four numbers we pulled off it, and you can wipe those with one click."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly asks the buyer to upload photographs of receipts, which are a ledger of where someone was and what they bought — more intimate than the product's framing suggests. The privacy line belongs on the upload control on the free tier, not on the pricing page, because the hesitation happens at the first upload. Whether the browser-side claim above is true for Ledgerly is `[architecture unknown — do not print this string until an engineer confirms the image never reaches a server]`.

### P-230 — Claim a mechanism, not an intention

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: A user can verify a claim about capability; they can only trust a claim about intent. "We will not read your data" asks for trust. "We could not read your data if we wanted to" describes a property of the system, which is checkable in principle and therefore worth more. The corollary is diagnostic: a company that can only offer an intention will write an intention, and buyers who have been burned read the difference.
- **works-when**: the architecture genuinely supports a capability claim — end-to-end encryption, on-device processing, no accounts, nothing stored.
- **fails-when**: it does not. AI-over-your-text and end-to-end encryption are structurally incompatible, and the copy shows it. Claiming a capability you do not have is the most severely punished misstatement in this layer, because it is falsifiable by a single security researcher.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. The observed spectrum (all 2026-08-12): **Day One** — "Privacy is the literal foundation upon which Day One is built", "End-to-end encryption is built into Day One and turned on by default", and the strongest sentence located anywhere in the corpus: **"Even if we wanted to read what's in your journal (we don't), we couldn't."** **Stoic** claims exclusivity without claiming encryption: "The journals are securely stored on your devices, and safely backed to ensure they're never lost. Absolutely nobody can access them but you." **How We Feel** offers opt-in only: "All of your data is kept on your device unless you opt-in to send an anonymized version of your check-ins to be used in research to help more people." **Rosebud**, which must read the text to run AI over it, manages only "Your data is encrypted in transit and at rest to protect your privacy" plus a hedged **"HIPAA aligned"** badge — "in transit and at rest" is explicitly not end-to-end, and "aligned" is not "compliant". **Talkspace** promises it *"will not share or disclose your personal information to anyone"* and then carves out that data *"can be used for research purposes, quality assurance activities, and product development,"* with "direct identifiers" removed.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (verbatim copy observation across six products in one lane, single observation date; no measured conversion effect)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus — dayoneapp.com/privacy-pledge/, getstoic.com, howwefeel.org, rosebud.app, talkspace.com, all observed 2026-08-12. **Method flag:** the fetch tool imposed a ~125-character quote cap, so short strings are high-fidelity and longer quotes may be clipped rather than byte-exact.
- **how-to**: Write down what your system is *structurally incapable* of doing with the user's data, and claim only that. If the honest answer is "we can read it but we don't", say exactly that instead of dressing it up — the hedged version of an absolute claim is more damaging than a plain limited one, because the hedge is the part people notice. Note that specificity and humour can carry the promise: Rocket Money's *"We don't accept cash, crypto, tacos, or private jets in exchange for your personal information. Some states (hi, California) have their own rules, so check out our Privacy Policy for the full scoop."* works because "tacos" and "private jets" make an abstract commitment concrete.
- **example-copy**: "We can read the receipts you upload — we have to, to categorise them. We don't sell them, we don't train on them, and you can delete your whole history from Settings in one click."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly cannot claim Day One's line, because a model must read the receipt to categorise it. The correct move is the limited plain claim above, and the mistake would be to reach for "bank-level encryption" or "HIPAA aligned"-style formulations, which signal to exactly the buyer who cares that the strong claim was unavailable.

### P-231 — Treat privacy as a loss-averse asset: claim it once, plainly, and make it true

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: Baseline trust in this category is high, so privacy claims buy little on the upside; a breach or a regulator finding costs the category's most valuable asset. The payoff is asymmetric, which means the correct spend on privacy *copy* is small and the correct spend on privacy *truth* is large.
- **works-when**: any product handling personal data, especially where the buyer has not been given a reason to doubt.
- **fails-when**: as an argument for silence — a product whose architecture is genuinely better than its competitors' should say so once, clearly, because it is a real differentiator (Day One's positioning is built on it).
- **observed-effect**: helpguide's BetterHelp user survey found **"83 percent said they were confident or very confident"** their information would be protected — **even after** the 2023 FTC settlement `[secondary, helpguide, 2026]`. Against that: the settlement itself was **$7.8 million** `[secondary, simplypsychology, 2026]`, and *"the Federal Trade Commission later found some of BetterHelp's practices to be deceptive"* `[secondary, healthline, 2026]`.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (a single secondary-sourced survey with no published sample or method, plus one regulatory outcome; the asymmetry argument is a mechanism, not a measurement)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus, secondary carriers helpguide.org, healthline.com, simplypsychology.com, all fetched 2026-08-12 (betterhelp.com returned 403 on every path)
- **how-to**: Make the claim once, in plain language, at the point of hesitation, and never again. Spend the rest of the effort making it true and making the deletion path work. Do not run privacy as a recurring marketing theme — repetition raises the salience of a doubt most buyers do not have.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly should carry one privacy sentence at the upload control and one line in the FAQ, and nothing else. The engineering work behind a one-click history wipe is a better investment than a privacy landing page.

### P-232 — Substitute a named model and a specified deliverable for a credential you do not have

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: When no human expert is involved, a buyer has nothing to attach the price to. Two substitutes work: naming the intellectual lineage the output is derived from, which borrows authority from a body of work; and specifying the artefact physically — its format, its length, its contents — which converts an unverifiable promise into a checkable description.
- **works-when**: advice, insight or analysis products delivered without a human, where the buyer cannot assess quality before purchase.
- **fails-when**: the named model is decorative — attaching a scientific-sounding lineage to output that does not use it is a credential claim, and a false one. Also fails when the specification is padding ("comprehensive", "in-depth") rather than a physical description.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Observed practice (2026-08-12): UnderstandMyself sells a **"$9.95"** one-off report positioned as *"based on the Big Five Aspects Scale, the scientific model that describes your personality through the (Big Five) factors and each of their two aspects."* The Enneagram Institute sells **"$20 per test"** and specifies the artefact rather than the benefit: *"one, single-use test code"*, delivered as a PDF containing *"your scores for all nine types in a full-spectrum profile, as well as the Expanded Type Descriptions (of over 2,500 words each) for your top three scores."* Talkspace, which does have clinicians, still leads with **"Backed by 30+ peer reviewed studies"** and **"80% say it's as or more effective as in-person therapy."** The lane's ranked list of credential substitutes actually in use put peer-review citation first, then comparative efficacy, precise live counters, named scientific model, credential-by-profession, quantified satisfaction, unconditional refund, deliverable specification, explicit disclaimers, certification assertion — and concluded that for a product with no clinicians, the available levers are **precise counters, the named model, the unconditional refund, deliverable specification and explicit disclaimers**.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (verbatim copy across four independent products, one observation date, no conversion measurement)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus — understandmyself.com, enneagraminstitute.com/rheti, tests.enneagraminstitute.com/orders/create, talkspace.com, all observed 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Name the body of work your output is derived from, in the same sentence as what it produces. Then describe the deliverable the way a shipping manifest would: format, length, sections, how it arrives, how long it takes. Specifying the artefact is how a no-human product justifies a price, and it is available on day one with zero customers.
- **example-copy**: "Categories follow the IRS Schedule C expense lines, so the export drops straight into your return. You get a CSV and a PDF: one row per receipt with vendor, date, amount, tax and category, plus a monthly summary by line."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's value metric is receipts processed, and its buyer is a freelancer who dreads bookkeeping once a month. The lineage claim available to Ledgerly is the tax-form category schema rather than a scientific model, and it does the same job: it tells the buyer the output is derived from something authoritative rather than invented. Combined with the physical specification of the export, this is proof Ledgerly can print before it has a single customer.

### P-233 — State plainly what the product is not

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: A disclaimer reads as legal hedging when it is buried and as candour when it is prominent. Naming the limits of what the product does makes the remaining claims more credible, because a seller willing to disqualify itself is a seller who is not selling everything.
- **works-when**: the product sits adjacent to a regulated or high-stakes category — advice, health, legal, tax, financial — where the buyer's unspoken question is "is this pretending to be the real thing".
- **fails-when**: the disclaimer is the only honest thing on a page that otherwise overclaims, or when it is so extensive it reads as a liability shield rather than a description.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Abby (AI talk-it-through product, abby.gg, observed 2026-08-12) states plainly: *"Abby is not a licensed therapist, psychologist, or psychiatrist"*, *"Abby cannot diagnose mental health conditions"*, *"not a replacement for professional therapy or clinical care"*. Rosebud carries the same move in one clause — *"not a substitute for a human connection"* — in its own voice.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (two observed implementations in one lane, no test)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus — abby.gg, rosebud.app, observed 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Write one short line naming the professional service you are not, placed near the value proposition rather than in a footer. Keep it to a sentence. Pair it with what you *are* so it reads as scoping rather than apology.
- **example-copy**: "Ledgerly is not an accountant and does not file anything. It turns receipts into clean, categorised rows — what you do with them is between you and your accountant."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyer does their own bookkeeping, so the fear is "will this get my taxes wrong". The disclaimer above scopes the product honestly and simultaneously sharpens the promise: clean rows, not filings.

### P-234 — Let the third party assert what you cannot say yourself

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: There are claims a seller cannot make in its own voice — either because they are regulated, or because self-praise is discounted on arrival. A user's own words carry both the claim and its credibility, because the reader knows the company did not choose the phrasing. The structure is standard for regulated-adjacent categories: **first-party hedges, third-party asserts**.
- **works-when**: you have real customer language, and the claim you need is comparative or superlative.
- **fails-when**: you have no customers — this pattern is unavailable pre-launch, and manufacturing the quotes is the exact failure P-237 covers. It also fails when the testimonial is doing work the company would be sanctioned for doing itself: curating third-party claims you could not legally make is a thin defence, not a shield.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Rosebud positions itself as "a companion rather than replacement" in its own voice while user quotes carry the substitution argument: *"ONLY 12.99 a month, way cheaper than a therapist"*, *"Much cheaper than private talk therapy"*, *"better than the therapists that I have paid for"*, *"I prefer this over seeing my psychiatrist/therapist"* (observed 2026-08-12). 16Personalities uses the same mechanism for quality rather than comparison, putting the user's word in quotation marks inside its own subheadline: *"Only 10 minutes to get a 'freakishly accurate' description of who you are and why you do things the way you do."* Intro.co frames testimonials as **counterfactual savings** rather than satisfaction — quotes citing "$14,000" saved on interior design and "$600+" on fashion advice, with named locations (Los Angeles CA, Chicago IL, Houston TX, New York City, Newport Beach CA).
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (three observed implementations, one lane, one date)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus — rosebud.app, 16personalities.com, intro.co, observed 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Mine your existing customer language for the sentence you wish you could write, and quote it verbatim with attribution. Prefer quotes that state a counterfactual with a number ("I saved $X", "it replaced $Y a month") over quotes that state satisfaction — the session pays for itself inside the testimonial. Keep the borrowed word in quotation marks when you lift it into your own copy, because the quotation marks are what make it believable.
- **example-copy**: "'Forty minutes of receipt-typing a month, gone.' — Marta, freelance illustrator" *(structure only — Ledgerly must quote a real customer or print nothing)*
- **worked-example**: With ~40 customers Ledgerly has enough to find two or three real quotes, and the highest-value one is a counterfactual: how many minutes or dollars of bookkeeping the buyer stopped spending. That quote makes the $9/mo Solo price argument better than any first-party sentence could. Before those 40 customers existed, this pattern was simply unavailable.

### P-235 — Use press, awards and logo walls only where the relationship is what the wall implies

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: A logo wall makes an implicit claim about a relationship, and the claim varies enormously by wording. "Trusted by employees at Google" is not "trusted by Google", and a reader who parses the difference has been told something about your willingness to imply more than you have.
- **works-when**: the coverage, award or customer relationship exists and is describable in a sentence you would be comfortable having quoted back at you.
- **fails-when**: the wall implies enterprise adoption that consists of individuals with a work email address; when awards are old enough to be misleading; or when the wall is the only proof on the page, in which case it is doing the job customer evidence should be doing.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the wall itself. Observed practice, all 2026-08-12: Day One carries genuine platform awards — "Apple App of the Year", "Apple Editors' Choice", "Apple Design Award", App Store 4.8 across 118,000 ratings — alongside a press wall (Time, The Verge, Washington Post, Lifehacker, 9to5Mac, Wired, New York Times). Noom runs an "AS SEEN IN" wall (Fortune, WebMD, Healthline, Forbes, Fast Company, The New York Times, Bloomberg). **Rosebud's institutional bar reads "Trusted by employees at Google, Microsoft, Meta, Y Combinator, UC Berkeley, MIT, NYU"** — a careful formulation that is not a customer-logo claim. GoodUI pattern **#117 "Company Logos", 5 tests** — medians paywalled; the tests include GoodUI's own pricing page adding customers and contributors (2020-03-04, 6,021 visitors, measuring sales) and Backstage.com at 221,012 and 23,626 visitors.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` (prevalence observation plus a paywalled 5-test GoodUI pattern with no obtainable effect size)
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus, observed 2026-08-12 ; https://goodui.org/patterns/117/
- **how-to**: For every logo, write the one-sentence relationship it stands for. If the sentence is weaker than the wall implies, either write the weaker sentence as the label ("Used by people at…") or drop the logo. Date your awards. Render the wall per P-222 so it does not outshout the CTA.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly has no press and no enterprise customers. If a freelancing newsletter mentions it, that is one line of honest text — "Written up in [newsletter], [date]" — not a logo wall of one. A wall of one logo is worse than no wall, because it advertises the size of the set.

### P-236 — The pre-launch proof set: what a product with no customers can honestly put on the page

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: Every pattern above this one requires customers. A zero-customer product cannot show counts, ratings, testimonials, live activity, plan shares or logo walls, and the temptation is to manufacture them. The way out is that customer evidence is only one of several things proof can be made of: **the artefact itself, the lineage it derives from, the risk you absorb, the cost the buyer will bear, the limits you name, and the property of the system you can demonstrate** are all verifiable without a single customer, and several of them are stronger than a thin testimonial would have been.
- **works-when**: pre-launch, at launch, or at any point where the honest customer number would embarrass you (roughly: below what a buyer would consider a crowd in your category).
- **fails-when**: the product is a commodity where the only real differentiator *is* adoption, or where the category's buyers are known to require reference customers (most enterprise procurement). Then the honest answer is that the page cannot close the sale yet and the first customers must come from direct outreach, not from the page.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` — **no source in this corpus measured any of the following on a zero-customer product.** The set is assembled from what customer-bearing products were observed doing that does not depend on having customers. Each component below is a directly observed 2026-08-12 implementation:
  1. **Specify the artefact physically.** Enneagram Institute: *"one, single-use test code"*, PDF format, *"Expanded Type Descriptions (of over 2,500 words each)"* for your top three scores. **This is how a no-human product justifies a price when nobody has bought it yet.**
  2. **Name the intellectual lineage.** UnderstandMyself: *"based on the Big Five Aspects Scale, the scientific model that describes your personality through the (Big Five) factors and each of their two aspects."*
  3. **Price the time cost before the money cost.** 16Personalities: *"Only 10 minutes to get a 'freakishly accurate' description of who you are and why you do things the way you do."* The first cost the buyer accepts is minutes, not dollars, and it is a claim you can make on day one.
  4. **Make the refund unconditional and say so in the buyer's words.** 16Personalities: *"If you're unhappy with the product, just email us at support@16personalities.com within 30 days, and we'll refund your money – no questions asked."* At near-zero marginal cost the refund rate is a rounding error; the claim transfers your risk to yourself, which is the one thing a stranger can verify you have done.
  5. **Give away a complete instance of the output.** 16Personalities delivers the full free result and sells depth afterwards, monetising demonstrated satisfaction rather than sunk cost; MentorCruise's risk reversal is *"Try your first call for free with every mentor you're meeting"* — a sample of the thing itself rather than a trial of a subscription. A zero-customer product's strongest available proof is a published worked example of exactly what the buyer will receive.
  6. **Claim a system property.** Day One: *"Even if we wanted to read what's in your journal (we don't), we couldn't."* A capability claim is verifiable and needs no customers (P-230).
  7. **State what you are not.** Abby: *"Abby is not a licensed therapist, psychologist, or psychiatrist."* Candour is available at zero scale (P-233).
  8. **Say the purchase shape plainly when it is unusually favourable.** 16Personalities states the $29 Premium Career Suite is *"One-time purchase (not subscription-based)"* as a feature.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` — and weaker than that: **this is an assembled inference, not a finding.** Every component is a real observed implementation, but all of them were observed on products that already had customers, and no source measured whether the set converts without them.
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus — 16personalities.com, 16personalities.com/premium-profiles, understandmyself.com, enneagraminstitute.com/rheti, mentorcruise.com, dayoneapp.com/privacy-pledge/, abby.gg, all observed 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Build the page from the six things you can verify today: the deliverable's physical specification, the lineage the output derives from, the time it takes, the refund, a complete published example of the output, and the limits you are willing to name. Add the founder's own name and reason for building it if it is genuinely relevant experience — it is a credential, and it is one you have. Then leave the customer-proof slots **empty** rather than filled: no placeholder testimonials, no "join thousands", no rating stars, no logo wall of one. An empty slot reads as a new product; a fake one reads as a dishonest product, and the buyer who catches it will not come back.
- **example-copy**: "Nobody has used this yet — it went live this week. Here is exactly what you get: upload a month of receipt photos, get back a CSV and a PDF with one row per receipt (vendor, date, amount, tax, Schedule C category) and a monthly summary. [A full example export, with real receipts of mine, is linked here.] If it doesn't save you the hour it's meant to, email me inside 30 days and I'll refund you without asking why."
- **worked-example**: Rewind Ledgerly to launch week, before the 40 customers. The available proof set: the export specification (CSV + PDF, named columns, monthly summary); the lineage (Schedule C expense lines); the time cost ("a month of receipts in [measure it — do not guess]"); the free tier at 5 receipts/month, which *is* the published worked example and costs Ledgerly one model call per visitor; and an unconditional 30-day refund, whose worst case at the $9 Solo price and near-zero COGS is $9 per refund. What is deliberately absent: any count, any star rating, any testimonial, any "trusted by" line. The page is thinner than a competitor's and it is not lying, which is the trade this pattern exists to make.

### P-237 — The fabrication boundary: proof you may not manufacture, at any scale

- **layer**: `trust-proof`
- **mechanism**: Fabricated proof is uniquely dangerous because it is *checkable by the exact person you are selling to*, it survives in screenshots after you fix it, and it converts a product weakness into a character finding. It is also the category of page element most likely to attract a regulator, because it is a factual misstatement made to induce a purchase.
- **works-when**: never. This pattern is a prohibition, listed as a pattern so it carries an ID that can be cited in an audit finding.
- **fails-when**: n/a. The common rationalisations — "it's representative", "we'll have those customers next month", "everyone does it", "it's a placeholder we'll swap out" — all describe a page that is live and false today.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for fabricated proof specifically, and the absence is not evidence of safety. The adjacent regulatory record in this corpus: the FTC's complaint against **JustAnswer LLC and CEO Andrew Kurtzig, filed 2026-01-13 in the Northern District of California**, over disclosure of subscription terms — counts under ROSCA for failure to *"clearly and conspicuously disclose the subscription fee"* and failure to *"obtain express informed consent"*, alleged to have affected "hundreds of thousands of consumers between January 2022 and the present" `[secondary — justanswer.com returned HTTP 403 on every path on 2026-08-12; complaint material obtained via ppc.land, regulatoryoversight.com and allaboutadvertisinglaw.com, all fetched 2026-08-12. Allegations, not adjudicated findings.]` And BetterHelp's **$7.8 million** FTC settlement in 2023 over data sharing, where *"the Federal Trade Commission later found some of BetterHelp's practices to be deceptive"* `[secondary — simplypsychology, healthline, both 2026]`. Both cases concern misdescription rather than invented testimonials; the specific illegality of fabricated reviews and endorsements in the US, EU and UK is `[unsourced in this corpus — no lane fetched the governing rule text; verify against current FTC endorsement/review rules and the EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive before relying on the legal characterisation]`.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` on the regulatory record as it applies here (two enforcement actions, both about misdescription, both reached through secondary carriers); the prohibition itself rests on the mechanism, not on a measured effect.
- **sources**: L14 teardown corpus and its secondary carriers, all fetched 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Treat the following as unshippable regardless of how good the page looks without them: invented or composite testimonials; stock-photo faces attached to quotes; customer counts, ratings or review totals that no query produces; "join N others" where N is aspirational; rotating live-activity widgets with no event stream behind them; a homemade or unlicensed trust seal (the Baymard finding that a fake seal outperformed real ones is a finding about user vulnerability, not a licence — P-224); press or partner logos for relationships that do not exist; award claims without a date and an issuer; "most popular" on a tier that is not modal (P-227); rounding a real rating up into a better one (P-228). If a section of the page cannot be filled honestly, delete the section.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's blind page spec includes a proof band. At 40 customers the band can hold one real counterfactual testimonial (P-234) and nothing else. The correct output of the audit is "proof band: one quote, no counts" — not "proof band: three quotes, 'trusted by hundreds of freelancers', four logos", which is what the section wanted to be.

## Interactions

**P-221 (proof next to the price) versus P-222 (proof subordinate to the CTA).** These pull in opposite directions and the best-documented result in the cluster belongs to the second: WorkZone gained **+34% form submissions at 99% significance** by *desaturating* logos that sat beside its CTA. The decider is visual competition, not distance — proof belongs adjacent to the objection and below the CTA in contrast. If adding proof to a price block makes the button no longer the loudest thing in it, the proof is now costing you.

**P-225 (precise counts) versus P-228 (audit for drift).** Precision raises the cost of being wrong. The moment you print "4,999,000" you have committed to regenerating it; Finch's "500k+" against an actual 738,701 is what happens when a precise-sounding claim is typed once. Never hard-code a number you chose for its precision.

**P-229/P-230 (privacy) versus the product's own architecture, and versus personalisation features generally.** A product that personalises must read the user's data, and every personalisation feature you add narrows the privacy claim you can make. This is a real product trade-off that shows up as copy: Day One can claim incapability, Rosebud can only claim encryption. Decide which side you are on before writing the sentence, and never let marketing pick the stronger claim than engineering can support.

**P-236 (the pre-launch set) versus almost everything above it.** P-221, P-225, P-226, P-227, P-234 and P-235 all require customers. Pre-launch, the page is built almost entirely from P-232, P-233, P-236 and the risk-reversal layer, and it will be visibly thinner than a competitor's. That thinness is the correct outcome, not a gap to fill.

**This layer versus `urgency` (layer 08).** Manufactured scarcity spends trust to buy a click, on a page whose main remaining asset — pre-launch especially — is trust. The calm version observed in this corpus is the Enneagram Institute's *"Test codes expire one year from date of purchase"*: a real constraint stated plainly, with no countdown. Where the brand's whole position is honesty (as it must be for a product using P-236), name the conflict and price it before adding any urgency device.

**This layer versus `checkout` (layer 10), specifically P-250.** Overclaiming proof and hiding a recurring charge are the same failure at different points on the page, and they compound: a buyer who discovers one goes looking for the other. P-237 and P-250 are the same boundary seen from two sides.

**P-227 (badge honesty) versus `tier-architecture` (layer 03).** The tier layer decides which tier to steer toward; this layer decides what you are allowed to call it. When those disagree — you want to steer to the top tier and the modal plan is the bottom one — the tier layer wins on placement and this layer wins on wording: badge the tier you are steering to, label it "Recommended", not "Most popular".

## Where this layer is thin

**Almost every finding here comes from a product that already had customers, so the corpus says nearly nothing about the pre-launch case — which is the case this layer was commissioned to answer.** P-236 is an assembled inference from what customer-bearing products do that does not require customers. It has never been measured against the alternative on a zero-customer page, and it should be labelled a prior in any COLD deliverable that uses it.

**No controlled test of social-proof placement on a pricing page was located anywhere.** The subhead-versus-band question is settled only by prevalence in a 33-page sample. The one well-documented placement result in the corpus (WorkZone) is about desaturating logos beside a B2B demo form and is a single test with no published sample.

**GoodUI's trust-relevant medians are entirely paywalled.** Patterns #63 (Trust Seals, 7 tests), #117 (Company Logos, 5 tests) and #131 (Authority, 4 tests) all render as the literal string `X.X%` without a subscription. What is visible is the test count and the sample sizes, not the direction or the size of the effect. Two to seven tests would in any case be "several anecdotes with a midpoint" rather than a meta-analysis.

**One transcription collision in this layer's own corpus is unresolved.** The 2026-08-12 census recorded the identical token "337K+" for two unrelated metrics at two unrelated companies — Todoist's five-star review count and 16Personalities' tests-taken-today counter. Both are now bracketed as unverified in P-225 and P-226 rather than quoted, because one of them is almost certainly a transcription error and neither page can be re-fetched from this corpus. It is a small defect with a general lesson that belongs in a layer about proof numbers: a census transcribed once and never re-read is itself a proof number that has not been audited.

**The seal research is thirteen years old at its core**, stated-preference rather than behavioural, US-only, and reports a 49% no-preference majority that limits what the ranking can mean.

**The privacy material is copy observation, not conversion measurement.** Every privacy finding in this layer comes from one product neighbourhood (therapy, journaling and decision help) and one observation date, several of the strongest sources returned 403 to automated fetch so their figures arrive through named secondary carriers, and the quote-extraction tool imposed a ~125-character cap that may have clipped longer strings. Nobody in this corpus measured whether a privacy sentence beside an input box changes the fill rate. The single trust-as-a-quantity figure — 83% confident post-settlement — is one secondary-sourced survey with no published sample or method.

**Nothing here addresses B2B reference selling, security questionnaires, SOC 2 as a purchase gate, or the proof requirements of procurement.** The corpus is consumer and prosumer; a founder selling seats into companies should treat this layer as weakly transferable and expect the binding proof constraint to be a compliance artefact rather than a page element.

**A solo founder at low traffic can learn none of this from their own data.** Every effect in this layer is smaller than what 2,000 monthly visitors can detect. These patterns are shipped on mechanism and honesty, not validated, and any deliverable that implies otherwise is dressing priors as evidence.
