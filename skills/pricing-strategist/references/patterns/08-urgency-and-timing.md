# Layer 08 — Urgency and timing

**Layer slug:** `urgency` · **Loaded at:** Phase 6

This layer governs everything on the page that argues *now rather than later*: deadlines, scarcity cues, countdown timers, launch and founder pricing, price-increase announcements, promotional windows and seasonal offers. It is the only layer in this library where **the best-evidenced finding is a cost rather than a benefit**. The conversion side of the scarcity literature is poorly sourced and circulates mostly through unattributed operator posts; the trust side is a controlled study with a stated sample. The thing that goes wrong most often is not a fake countdown — most founders know not to ship one — it is the assumption that an *honest* deadline is free. It is not. Tuncer et al. found that scarcity cues degraded perceived vendor benevolence **even when the constraint was real**, which means every use of this layer spends trust to buy a sale. Direct response, where these tactics were formalised, was a one-shot mail-order business: there was no month eleven, so the bill never arrived. A subscription pays the whole downstream bill, and a pricing-page A/B test structurally cannot see it.

## How to use this layer

**Read P-202 and P-203 first, before you read anything else here, and read them as a pair.** P-202 is the measured trust cost; P-203 is the reason your own experiment will not detect it. Together they set the standard every other pattern in this layer has to clear: not "does it lift conversion" but "is the constraint real, and is the lift worth the benevolence it spends". **P-204 → P-206 are the honesty tests** — apply them to any urgency device before it ships, and delete anything that fails. **P-207 → P-211 are the constructive set**: the four urgency mechanics that are genuinely available to a self-serve product without lying (announced price increases, grandfathering, launch ladders, artefact expiry, and dated promotions). **P-212 → P-217 are the accounting** — how to read a promotion's results, when to run one at all, and how to build a business that does not need manufactured urgency in the first place.

In a **COLD** regime this layer is almost entirely a set of prohibitions. A product with ~2,000 monthly visitors cannot power a test of any urgency device, and — per P-214 — a page whose only asset is trust is the worst possible place to spend trust. The two patterns that pay at COLD are **P-209** (a launch ladder, which generates a real reference price and a real deadline as a by-product of pricing honestly) and **P-217** (a repricing cadence, which removes the pressure that makes founders reach for fake urgency).

## Patterns

### P-202 — Price the benevolence cost before you add any urgency device, because honest cues cost it too

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: Scarcity and urgency cues are increasingly read as attempts at manipulation rather than as information. The buyer's inference is about *you*, not about the offer: a seller who applies pressure is a seller optimising for the seller. Because the inference attaches to the vendor rather than to the cue, it survives the cue being truthful — which is the finding that matters, and the one every operator summary of this literature omits.
- **works-when**: Never as a reason *to* add urgency. This pattern exists to be applied *before* the others: it is the price tag you attach to every device in this layer.
- **fails-when**: Read as "only fake urgency is harmful". The study measured honest cues. Also note the asymmetry in the evidence base itself: the trust cost is measured in a controlled experiment; the conversion benefit it is traded against is mostly unsourced (see `## Folklore`), so you are trading a documented cost for an undocumented gain.
- **observed-effect**: **Tuncer et al. (2023), n=202 participants, three site versions — countdown timer / stock progress bar / control — measured with NASA-TLX, a website-trust benevolence scale, and UEQ-short.** `[secondary — the paper was never fetched in this corpus. It is reached through a Substack summary and an agency blog, so the sample, the instruments and every reported direction below are as those two carriers state them, and neither is independent of the other's framing.]` Countdown timers produced **significantly higher frustration** than no scarcity cues; limited-time cues degraded pragmatic quality, functioning as visual clutter; **both** cue types **negatively affected perceived vendor benevolence**, with users reading them as opportunistic ploys. Stock progress bars did not raise frustration but reduced hedonic appeal. Supporting literature: Kristofferson et al. (2017) on timers triggering annoyance and aggression; Mathur et al. (2019) classifying scarcity cues as dark patterns; Luguri and Strahilevitz (2021) on increased mistrust and impulsive buying.
- **evidence**: `medium` for the trust cost. It was rated `strong` and cannot be: a single study, reached only through two secondary carriers and never read at primary level, is one organisation's finding relayed by others, and this library requires more than one independent organisation before it will say `strong`. The direction is well argued and the asymmetry against the conversion side still holds; the conversion-lift side of the same literature remains poorly sourced.
- **sources**: https://uxpsychology.substack.com/p/the-use-of-scarcity-cues-in-e-commerce `[secondary carrier]` · https://revenueflows.ai/blog/does-product-scarcity-increase-shopify-conversion-rate `[secondary carrier, agency blog]`
- **how-to**: Before adding any urgency element, write two sentences: what the constraint actually is, and what you expect the element to be worth. If you cannot write the first, delete the element (P-205). If you can, add the benevolence cost explicitly to the second — for a subscription, a buyer who feels pressured at purchase is a buyer with a lower bar for cancelling in month three, and that cost lands outside the window any test will measure. Prefer the calmest form of the true constraint: a stated date beats a ticking clock, and a sentence beats a badge.
- **example-copy**: n/a — this pattern governs whether other copy ships at all.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's brand promise is that bookkeeping stops being stressful. A countdown timer on the pricing page contradicts the product's core claim before the buyer has read a feature — it manufactures exactly the feeling the product sells relief from. That is not a general argument against timers; it is the specific version of the benevolence cost for this product, and it is decisive.

### P-203 — Accept that your own A/B test structurally cannot see what urgency costs you

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: An urgency test measures conversion inside its window. The cost it creates — lower benevolence, higher refund propensity, earlier cancellation, less willingness to recommend — lands months later, in a cohort the test is no longer watching. This is not a power problem you can solve with more traffic; it is a horizon problem, and the horizon is longer than almost any published pricing experiment.
- **works-when**: Always, when interpreting an urgency result — yours or anyone's.
- **fails-when**: You substitute a proxy and believe it. Refund rate at 30 days is not benevolence; it is the fastest-moving fraction of the cost.
- **observed-effect**: The clearest published instance of a long-horizon reversal is not about urgency but has the same shape: **MSN Video (Microsoft, 2009)** moved a pre-roll ad to post-roll and measured a **6-week return rate up just over 2%** while ad impressions fell **more than 50%** — net negative, and visible only because they cohorted for two weeks and tracked for six. Almost no published pricing test does this. Two urgency-adjacent counter-metrics were acknowledged and never quantified: **Mojo** demoted the monthly plan and reported **+15–20% yearly take-up with "only a minor increase in cancellations"** — *minor* is not a number (RevenueCat, no sample, duration or significance published). Base rates for context (Microsoft, 2009): only about **one-third** of well-designed experiments improve their key metric, and QualPro's tally across **150,000 ideas over 22 years** found **75% had no impact or actively hurt**.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the structural argument is sound and the MSN Video case demonstrates the mechanism; no one has run the long-horizon experiment on urgency specifically)*
- **sources**: Kohavi et al., "Controlled experiments on the web" (S1 in L09) · https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/paywall-tests-grow-app-revenue/ · L09 winner's-curse analysis
- **how-to**: If you run an urgency test anyway: cohort the exposed users and track them for at least three billing cycles, declare **day-90 cancellation and refund rate as counter-metrics before launch**, and require them to stay flat rather than treating them as commentary. If you cannot power that, you cannot evaluate the tactic — which is itself the finding, and the correct response is to fall back on P-202's prior rather than shipping and hoping. Note that direct response never needed this discipline because it sold once; you are not in that business.
- **example-copy**: n/a (methodological)
- **worked-example**: At ~2,000 monthly visitors and ~40 customers, Ledgerly cannot detect a 10% conversion change on the pricing page, let alone a benevolence effect at day 90. The honest conclusion is that the urgency question is **not empirically available to this product**, so it is decided on mechanism and brand fit — which is what P-202 does.

### P-204 — Use only real deadlines, and put the mechanism next to the date

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: A deadline is credible when the buyer can see what makes it exist. A date with a reason attached ("the price goes up on the 14th because the launch cohort closes") is information; a date with no reason is a claim the buyer has to take on faith from a seller they have just met. Naming the mechanism converts urgency from pressure into scheduling, which is the only form of it that survives P-202 at acceptable cost.
- **works-when**: The constraint exists independently of the marketing — a real price change, a real cohort cap, a real capacity ceiling, a genuine seasonal event. For a self-serve digital product, the honest list is short: an announced price increase (P-207), a founding-cohort cap (P-209), and a genuinely seasonal moment (P-215).
- **fails-when**: The date has no mechanism behind it. "For a limited time" with no end date is the canonical non-claim — it names urgency without naming a deadline, so there is nothing to honour and nothing to check. Also fails when you announce and then do not follow through: a price increase announced and not made is the same lie as a resetting timer, discovered a month later.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for real-deadline copy specifically. The frequently-quoted claim that checkout countdown timers lift purchase completion "up to 9.3% when the deadline is real" is **unattributed at source** and is recorded in `## Folklore`. Live prevalence, 33-page census 2026-08-12: promotional claims carry a footnote marker or an explicit end condition where they appear at all — Slack's *"50% off for 3 months*"* carries the asterisk; Squarespace ships *"Take 20% off any new website plan For a limited time, take 20% off any new website plan. Use code at checkout"* with no date, which is the weak form.
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: https://wisewords.blog/book-summaries/100m-offers-book-summary/ · https://www.gregfaxon.com/blog/100m-offers-summary · 33-page pricing-page census, 2026-08-12 (L17 copy bank)
- **how-to**: Write the deadline as a date, then write the mechanism in the same sentence. If the mechanism sentence is embarrassing to write, the deadline is not real. Put the date in the page's own copy rather than in a widget, so it can be read, screenshotted and held against you — that permanence is the point. Then calendar the follow-through before you publish.
- **example-copy**: "This price goes up on 14 September for new subscribers. If you're in before then, you keep it." · Capacity register (Hormozi): "We only accept 5 new clients per week and we already have the first 3 spots taken."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's only real deadline is its own price ladder (P-209): "Founder pricing — $6/month for the first 50 subscribers, then it goes to $9. 31 are taken." Every element of that sentence is checkable, and the count is a fact Ledgerly can update rather than a bar that fills itself.

### P-205 — Assume a fabricated constraint is detected, and delete it rather than improving it

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: Manufactured scarcity has become a recognised genre. Timers that reset on refresh and "only 2 left" badges that never change are canonical dark patterns, and buyers have been trained on them by a decade of exposure. Detection does not merely neutralise the cue — it re-frames everything else on the page as probably also manipulated, which is why this is the most expensive single element a pricing page can carry.
- **works-when**: Never, as a deliberate choice. Included as a pattern because it is the most-published *losing* move in this layer's territory and because the temptation is strongest at exactly the moment (a soft launch, a bad month) when the damage is least affordable.
- **fails-when**: Always, on a long horizon — and the failure is not the timer, it is the second-order inference about every other claim you make. Note also the regulatory drift: dark-pattern research is increasingly cited in regulatory contexts (Mathur et al. 2019; Luguri and Strahilevitz 2021), and the tail risk on manufactured scarcity is no longer purely reputational.
- **observed-effect**: The trust-cost evidence in P-202 applies here *a fortiori* — it was measured on honest cues. The most instructive live example is **Blinkist** (observed 2026-08-12): its pricing page ran a live banner reading **"20% discount reserved for🔥 10:00"** with a named coupon (`blink20`), against list prices of $174.99 and $99.99 discounted to $139.99 and $79.99. **Whether the "reserved" discount is ever actually absent is unverified — a permanently-running countdown is the standard pattern.** Widely circulated operator claims of 23–41% lower repeat-purchase rates among buyers who detect fake urgency are `[unsourced — presented without attribution; verifying would require the merchant cohort data behind them]`.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the mechanism is well-argued and the dark-pattern classification is peer-reviewed; the quantified damage claims are unsourced)*
- **sources**: https://startupspells.com/p/scarcity-marketing-tactics-fake-urgency-boosts-conversions · https://uxpsychology.substack.com/p/the-use-of-scarcity-cues-in-e-commerce · https://www.blinkist.com/en/pricing (observed 2026-08-12)
- **how-to**: Audit the page for every element implying a constraint you cannot document, and remove each one — do not "make it more believable", because a more believable fabrication is a worse liability, not a better tactic. If you keep a timer, it must survive a refresh, survive an incognito window, and reflect an event on a calendar somewhere. Test it in all three ways yourself before shipping.
- **example-copy**: n/a — this pattern is about removal.
- **worked-example**: The Blinkist banner is the exact device a Ledgerly founder would be tempted to copy, because it is visibly working for a large company. What that company can absorb and a 40-customer product cannot is the second-order inference: Blinkist is an established consumer brand with public store ratings to fall back on, and Ledgerly has one asset, which is that its founder seems honest. The membership count often quoted alongside this comparison is `[unsourced — the "43 million members" figure carried no source and no observation date; it would need Blinkist's own about or press page fetched and dated before it could be printed]`, so the argument here is made on the difference in trust balance rather than on a number.

### P-206 — Put the deadline on the offer, never on the price

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: A deadline on the *price* tells the buyer that the number is arbitrary — if $9 becomes $12 on Friday because of a marketing decision, then $9 was never what the thing costs. A deadline on the *offer* (a bonus, an onboarding session, a migration, a founding-cohort perk) leaves the price's credibility intact, because what expires is something you are genuinely choosing to stop providing. The exception that proves the rule is a real, permanent price increase (P-207), which is a change to the price rather than a deadline on it.
- **works-when**: You have something bundleable with a real marginal cost to you — your time, a limited-capacity onboarding, a hand-migration — that can honestly stop being offered.
- **fails-when**: The "bonus" is a digital good with zero marginal cost that you will obviously keep giving away, which is a fabricated constraint wearing a different hat (P-205). Also fails when the expiring bonus is the actual reason people buy, in which case you have mispriced the core product.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Hormozi's four urgency mechanics — rolling cohorts, rolling seasonal promotions with real start and end dates, pricing/bonus deadlines with advance notice, and exploding offers whose value decays — are doctrine, not measurement. Of the four, only the bonus-deadline and rolling-cohort forms are available to a self-serve product without manufacturing something.
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: https://wisewords.blog/book-summaries/100m-offers-book-summary/ · https://www.gregfaxon.com/blog/100m-offers-summary
- **how-to**: List what you can honestly stop offering. For a solo founder the answer is nearly always the same thing: personal time. Attach the deadline to that, state the capacity in the same sentence, and stop when you said you would. Keep the price itself stable and change it only via P-207.
- **example-copy**: "Everyone who subscribes before 14 September gets a 30-minute setup call with me, where I import your last year of receipts with you. I can do about ten of these a month, which is why this closes."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's founder can do roughly ten import sessions a month. That is a real ceiling with a real cost, and the deadline attached to it is defensible in a way that "$9 until Friday" would not be — the price stays $9 on both sides of the date, so nothing about the number is called into question.

### P-207 — Announce a price increase with a date, and then actually raise it

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: A pre-announced increase is the one urgency device that is fully honest, fully checkable, and generates a genuine reference price as a by-product: after the raise, the old price is a real former price rather than a fabricated anchor. It also converts a decision the buyer was deferring indefinitely into a decision with a date, without anyone inventing a constraint.
- **works-when**: You genuinely intend to raise the price, the new price applies to new subscribers, and you honour whatever you promised existing ones (P-208).
- **fails-when**: You announce and do not raise. That is the same lie as a resetting timer with a longer fuse, and it is discovered by exactly the people who acted on it. It also fails when framed as generosity you are extending — buyers who compare invoices see a price increase regardless of the sentence around it.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for announced-increase copy. The framing literature is **contested**: prospect theory predicts that "the launch discount ends" should sting less than "we're raising your price", but **Gal & Rucker (*JCP* 28:497–516, 2018)** argue the evidence does not support any general tendency for losses to loom larger than gains, with Simonson & Kivetz (2018) and Mrkva et al. (2020) defending only a moderated version. **Treat loss framing as a contingent tool, not a multiplier.** Cadence context: the PricingSaaS 500 averaged **3.6 pricing/packaging changes per company in 2025** — **a B2B software cohort, and the label travels with the number**, since the index tracks enterprise and mid-market vendors rather than $9–$19 consumer subscriptions. It establishes that a price change is a normal event rather than a crisis; it does not establish a cadence for a consumer ladder.
- **evidence**: `anecdote` for the pricing application; the *contestedness* of the framing claim is `medium`-to-`strong`
- **sources**: https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jcpy.1047 · https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jcpy.1046 · L19 price-derivation lane
- **how-to**: Announce the new price, the date it applies from, and who it applies to, in one sentence on the pricing page and in one email. Give at least a month. Do not decorate it — the structure is the persuasion, and any framing flourish invites the comparison that undoes it. Then raise it on the stated date whether or not the pre-raise week went well, because the credibility of your next announcement is worth more than the marginal signups.
- **example-copy**: "Ledgerly goes from $9 to $12 a month on 1 October, for new subscribers. If you're already on $9, you stay on $9 — see below."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly raises Solo from $9 to $12 on 1 October, announced 1 September. Existing subscribers are grandfathered for twelve months (P-208). The by-product is a real reference price: from October, "$12/mo, was $9 at launch" is a truthful anchor, and the founder cohort has a concrete reason to feel they got in early — a feeling that costs nothing and was earned rather than manufactured.

### P-208 — Grandfather existing customers explicitly, for a stated period, and say so in public

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: A price increase creates a fairness question for people who already bought, and fairness violations produce backlash disproportionate to whatever the framing gained. Grandfathering answers the question structurally rather than rhetorically. It also produces a clean natural experiment: the new cohort's conversion and retention can be read against the old one's, at a fixed public price, without ever showing two people different prices at the same moment.
- **works-when**: You can represent two price cohorts in billing (straightforward on Stripe) and you state the grandfather period's length rather than leaving it open.
- **fails-when**: The period is unstated, in which case you have promised "forever" by implication and will break it. Also fails as a substitute for the increase itself: a permanent grandfather on a growing base means an ever-larger share of customers below list, and the arithmetic eventually forces the conversation you deferred.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Common practice reported in the price-derivation lane is to **grandfather for 6–12 months and test new pricing on new customers first**. The fairness constraint is well-established in the anchoring literature: Kahneman/Thaler's fairness work and the partitioned-pricing review (Morwitz 2016) both find perceived unfairness in price framing produces backlash disproportionate to the framing's gain.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the fairness mechanism has academic support; the 6–12 month convention is practice, not measurement)*
- **sources**: L19 price-derivation lane · https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Morwitz-Vicki-Wroe-Alderson-PAPER-JCP-2016.pdf
- **how-to**: State the grandfather period in months, on the pricing page, next to the increase announcement. Give existing customers a dated window in which they can also lock the old rate on an annual plan if they want to extend it — that is a real, honest, deadline-bearing offer that costs you nothing you were not already giving away. Never run a live price A/B test as an alternative to this: cohort pricing and grandfathering give you the same read while showing everyone at a given moment the same public price, which is the property that removes the fairness objection entirely.
- **example-copy**: "If you're already subscribed, you stay at $9 until at least 1 October 2027. Want to keep it longer? Switch to annual before 1 October and you lock $90/year for that year."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's 40 existing customers stay at $9 for twelve months. About a third take the annual lock, which converts a price increase into a cash-collection event and gives the founder a clean comparison: the September cohort at $9 against the October cohort at $12, same page, same copy, different month — the closest thing to a controlled price test this product can run.

### P-209 — Run launch pricing as a public ladder you actually climb

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: A published ladder — "first 50 at $6, then $9, then $12" — does four things at once, none of which requires inventing anything: it creates a real deadline, it generates a real reference price for every later cohort, it gives you a willingness-to-pay reading (how fast does each rung fill), and it lets you start below your intended price without ever discounting. The scarcity is genuine because you set the cap and can prove the count.
- **works-when**: You are pre-launch or early, you intend the price to rise, and you can display and honour the remaining count.
- **fails-when**: The rungs never actually advance, which converts the whole device into a permanent fake discount and is worse than never having run it. Also fails if the cap is large enough that it will never bind — a "first 5,000 customers" ladder on a product with 40 customers is a fabricated constraint (P-205) with an arithmetic disguise.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the ladder as a conversion device. Its evidential value is different and better-grounded: the L19 lane recommends **cohort pricing and grandfathering instead of live price A/B tests**, because a public ladder produces genuine willingness-to-pay readings while showing every customer at a given moment the same public price — which removes the fairness objection that makes live price experiments hazardous. Cautionary precedent for the alternative: **Amazon's 2000 random price test** ended in a public apology, refunds to **6,896 customers averaging $3.10**, and a permanent policy that test purchasers automatically receive the lowest test price.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(as a pricing-research method); `anecdote` as a conversion tactic*
- **sources**: L19 price-derivation lane · https://press.aboutamazon.com/2000/9/amazon-com-issues-statement-regarding-random-price-testing
- **how-to**: Announce the whole ladder up front, including the top rung — hiding the destination makes each raise a surprise. Show the remaining count and update it. Advance on schedule even if a rung fills slowly; a rung that stalls is the single most useful pricing datapoint you will get, and holding the price to "let it fill" destroys the reading and the credibility together. If you ever vary price by anything other than a public, non-personal reason, honour the lowest shown price for everyone who bought during the variation.
- **example-copy**: "Founder pricing: the first 50 subscribers pay $6/month for as long as they stay. After that it's $9, and it'll be $12 by the end of the year. 31 of 50 taken."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly launches the ladder at $6/50, $9/next 150, $12 thereafter. If the first rung fills in three weeks and the second stalls at 60 of 150, that is a willingness-to-pay finding worth more than any survey the founder could run at this scale — and it was produced by a device that is honest, checkable, and generates its own deadline.

### P-210 — Put the expiry on the artefact, not on the buyer

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: An expiry attached to something the buyer *has* — a code, a credit, an access window — is a term of the product. An expiry attached to the buyer's decision is pressure. The two can encode the same constraint and read completely differently, because one is a fact about what was sold and the other is a fact about how hard you are pushing. This is the calmest available form of urgency and the only one that fits a brand whose whole position is honesty.
- **works-when**: There is a real reason the thing cannot sit unredeemed forever — support cost, model changes, unit cost you have already incurred.
- **fails-when**: The expiry has no operational reason, in which case it is a forfeiture mechanism dressed as a term. Also note the interaction with metered products: credits that expire while purchased top-ups do not is a defensible asymmetry (draw the expiring bucket down first, and say so), but silent expiry of anything the buyer paid for reliably produces the worst support thread of your year.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published`. Live instances: **the Enneagram Institute** states *"Test codes expire one year from date of purchase"* (L14, observed 2026-08-12) — a real constraint stated plainly with no countdown, which the trust-and-proof layer flags as the calm exemplar. In metered products, **Midjourney** pairs *"Fast hours don't roll over"* with top-up hours that *"do not expire"* (second-hand via eesel, 2026-06-05); **ElevenLabs and Gamma** cap rollover and expire included credits at period end.
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: L14 decision-neighbourhood teardowns (observed 2026-08-12) · L12 AI-tool teardowns (Midjourney strings quoted second-hand via eesel, 2026-06-05)
- **how-to**: Write the expiry as a property of the thing: "codes expire a year after purchase", "included receipts reset monthly; top-ups never expire". State it at the point of purchase, not in terms. Give the reason if it is a real one. Never send an expiry reminder that reads as a sales email — a factual "your 40 top-up receipts are still here, they don't expire" is a stronger retention message than a countdown.
- **example-copy**: "Test codes expire one year from date of purchase." — the Enneagram Institute, 2026-08-12. Metered form: "Your 100 monthly receipts reset on the 14th. Top-up packs never expire."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's top-up pack ($5 for 50 receipts) never expires, and the monthly allowance resets. Stating both in one sentence on the pricing page removes the buyer's unasked question ("do I lose these?") and creates a mild, honest reason to buy the pack now rather than later — which is as much urgency as this product should ever carry.

### P-211 — Show the discounted price rather than a code, and give every promotion a stated end condition

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: A discount only influences a decision the buyer has not yet made. Requiring a code moves the reveal to the last step, after the decision was already made or abandoned — so the discount pays for itself only among people who were going to buy anyway. Pre-applying it, and rendering the discounted price as the headline number, lets the discount do the persuading it was bought for. The end condition is what stops a promotion becoming a permanent lower price with a decorative strikethrough.
- **works-when**: The promotion is genuine, the saving is large enough to notice, and there is a stated end date or end condition.
- **fails-when**: The "discount" never expires, which resets the reference price and destroys the anchor you were renting. Also fails legally if the struck-through price was never charged: a fabricated reference price is exposed under the EU/UK Omnibus Directive (prior-price substantiation for discount claims) and under FTC guidance in the US. **The specific regulatory text was not verified in this research** — `[unsourced — would need EU Directive 98/6/EC as amended by 2019/2161 Art. 6a and current FTC pricing guidance fetched directly]`.
- **observed-effect**: **GoodUI Pattern #69 "Autodiscounting", 6 tests** across product, pricing, checkout and landing screens (Lovehoney.co.uk, Backstage.com, Mvideo.ru); medians `[paywalled — subscription required]`. One figure is publicly visible: **test #167** — showing the discount earlier in the journey (on product pages rather than only at checkout) reported **"With a 50% Discount: Add to Cart rate +33%, Sales rate +24%"**, with a modest gain at a lower discount level. ⚠️ **Read cautiously: a 50% discount moves sales for obvious reasons, and the test conflates "applied automatically" with "enormous".** Related: **#553 Online.metro-cc.ru** (Sep 2024, revenue — a preset −15% coupon *button* for new users versus an empty coupon field); **#306 Backstage.com** (an emphasis message clarifying that a yearly discount was already active). Prevalence, 33-page census 2026-08-12: promotional anchors carry a footnote marker — Slack's *"50% off for 3 months*"*.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(6 tests, one large published effect confounded with the size of the discount itself)*
- **sources**: https://goodui.org/patterns/69/ · 33-page pricing-page census, 2026-08-12
- **how-to**: Render the post-discount price as the headline number with the genuine pre-discount price struck through beside it, and put the end condition in the same block — a date, not "limited time". Where a code is unavoidable, pre-fill it as a labelled button rather than an empty field. Then diary the end date, and end it.
- **example-copy**: "$6/month — was $9. Founder pricing ends 1 October." Live instance with the honest asterisk convention: "50% off for 3 months*" — Slack, 2026-08-12.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly never ships a coupon field. Its only discount is the launch ladder (P-209), rendered as the current price with the next rung named beside it. The strikethrough is truthful because $9 is the price the next cohort will actually pay, on a date already published.

### P-212 — Never stack urgency on top of a fabricated reference price, and do not assume stacked tactics add

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: Urgency and anchoring are frequently deployed together — a countdown beside a struck-through price beside a "most popular" badge — on the implicit theory that the effects compose. There is no evidence that they do, one published interaction points the other way, and the combination raises the probability that at least one element is checkable and false, which is what converts a persuasive page into a suspicious one.
- **works-when**: Applied as a restraint: pick the single strongest true device and drop the rest.
- **fails-when**: You are optimising each element independently and shipping them all. Each may test neutral-to-positive in isolation and the combination can still be worse, because the inference the buyer draws is about the *page*, not about any element on it.
- **observed-effect**: **There is essentially no evidence on what happens when anchoring, charm pricing, a decoy tier, urgency and a struck-through reference price are combined; the assumption that the effects add is unsupported.** The one published interaction in the price-psychology corpus points against stacking: **Anderson & Simester** found `$9` price endings *weaken* in the presence of a "Sale" cue. On the anchor half specifically, an anchor that is never actually charged is a fabricated reference price with legal exposure (see P-211's caveat), and Thaler's transaction-utility mechanism requires the reference to be *credible* — "a struck-through $499 on a $29 product invites disbelief in both numbers."
- **evidence**: `medium` *(the absence of interaction evidence is well-established; the Anderson & Simester interaction is a single published finding)*
- **sources**: L03 price-psychology lane · https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/thaler-mktsci1985.pdf
- **how-to**: Count the persuasion devices on your pricing page: badges, strikethroughs, countdowns, scarcity counts, "most popular" labels, urgency banners. If there is more than one in the decision zone, remove all but the one whose underlying fact is strongest. Then check that the survivor's fact is documented. Layer 12's rule applies with force here — bundling changes destroys attribution, so stacking also removes your ability to learn which element, if any, was doing anything.
- **example-copy**: n/a — this pattern is about subtraction.
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's decision zone carries exactly one persuasion device: the founder-ladder count. No badge, no countdown, no strikethrough beyond the ladder's own true former price. That is not minimalism as an aesthetic; it is the only configuration in which the founder can later tell what worked.

### P-213 — Judge a promotion by its renewal cohort, not by its signup spike

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: A discount with a deadline reliably produces a signup spike, and the spike is the metric everyone reports. The cost lands at renewal, when the promotional cohort meets the standard price — a moment structurally identical to a price increase, applied to the most price-sensitive customers you have ever acquired. Reading the promotion at signup is reading the numerator of a fraction whose denominator arrives a year later.
- **works-when**: Always, when evaluating any dated discount, launch offer or seasonal promotion.
- **fails-when**: You lack the horizon to see the renewal, in which case the honest report is "we do not know yet", not "it worked".
- **observed-effect**: Discounting is rarer than founders assume: **9 in 10 subscriptions are sold at full price** (Adapty, 16k apps, 2026), and **only 9.3% of apps use promotional offers** overall — Health & Fitness highest at 14%, Gaming lowest at 3.6% (RevenueCat, 115k apps, 2026). ⚠️ **Both are mobile IAP figures**; store promotional-offer types have no direct Stripe analogue. The renewal-cliff mechanism is documented in the annual context: **35% of all annual cancellations occur in month 1** — **RevenueCat's 115k-app cohort, mobile in-app purchase, not web, and the label belongs on the figure every time it is quoted** — before any renewal, and RevenueCat's own framing is that annual retention "can mask a broken product" because the churn is deferred rather than avoided. Retention guidance from the same lane: **never present a discount offer during an active cancel flow**, and avoid stacking discounts.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(prevalence is well-sourced at very large N but mobile-only; the renewal-cliff claim is mechanism plus the month-1 annual cancellation cluster)*
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps · https://adapty.io/blog/high-performing-paywall-2026/ · https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/annual-subscriptions-apps-pros-cons
- **how-to**: Before running a promotion, write down the renewal date of the cohort it will create and diary the review. Report the promotion's result on two lines — signups now, and renewal rate then — and refuse to call it a win on the first line alone. State the renewal price inside the offer sentence at purchase (the risk-reversal layer's P-188), which is the single cheapest thing you can do to make that renewal survivable.
- **example-copy**: "You'll be charged $60 today for your first year. On 14 September 2027 it renews at $90 a year, and we'll email you a month before."
- **worked-example**: If Ledgerly's founder ladder brings 50 subscribers at $6 against a standing price of $12, the honest scorecard has a second line dated twelve months out: how many of the 50 are still paying when their rate goes to $9 (their grandfathered rung), and how many at $12. Until that line has a number in it, the ladder's result is unknown, and the founder should say so.

### P-214 — Do not spend trust on urgency when trust is the only asset the page has

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: Urgency trades credibility for speed. A page with a logo wall, a rating count and 40,000 customers has credibility to trade; a page with none has exactly one asset, and spending it is not a trade but a liquidation. This is why the same device can be roughly free for a large brand and ruinous for a new one — the benevolence cost from P-202 is levied against a much smaller balance.
- **works-when**: Applied as a gate at pre-launch and at low customer counts — which for most products means *by default*.
- **fails-when**: Misread as "urgency is fine once you are big". The cost does not disappear at scale; it becomes affordable, which is a different statement and one you should make deliberately.
- **observed-effect**: `no quantified effect published` for the interaction between brand strength and urgency cost. The supporting structure comes from Layer 09: a product with no customers cannot show a customer count, a rating or a testimonial, and its page is built almost entirely from the pre-launch proof set plus this library's risk-reversal layer — a set whose entire value is that it is honest. The trust-and-proof layer names this conflict explicitly and directs that it be priced before any urgency device is added.
- **evidence**: `medium` *(a composition of P-202's measured benevolence cost with the pre-launch proof constraint; not independently measured)*
- **sources**: L14 decision-neighbourhood teardowns · Layer 09 (P-236, and the layer's stated conflict with `urgency`)
- **how-to**: Count what else the page has: named customers, a rating with a real denominator, press, a substantiated number. If the answer is "nothing yet", ship no urgency device at all, and take the honest constraints (P-209's ladder, P-206's capacity) which *build* credibility rather than consuming it. Revisit once there is something else in the trust column.
- **example-copy**: n/a (gate)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly is COLD with 40 customers and no public proof. Its trust column contains one item: the founder's plainly-written page. Every device in this layer except P-209 and P-206 spends that item, so the correct configuration is a page with a real ladder, a real capacity-bounded onboarding offer, and no other urgency of any kind.

### P-215 — Time the offer to the buyer's calendar, not to yours

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: Consumers act when the underlying need surfaces, and for many products the need is genuinely periodic — tax season, a new year, the start of a project, a trip. A deadline that coincides with the buyer's real moment does not have to manufacture anything, because the urgency already exists in their life and the offer merely arrives on time. A deadline set by your quarter is pressure with a coincidental date.
- **works-when**: The product has an episodic, recurring need with a calendar the buyer already keeps.
- **fails-when**: The product's use is continuous, in which case there is no season and inventing one is a fabricated constraint. Also fails as a retention theory: seasonal timing tells you when to *offer*, not whether the offer is any good.
- **observed-effect**: The nearest measured analogue is reactivation timing rather than promotion timing: **20.1% of churned monthly consumer subscribers reactivate within a year on their own** (up from 13.7% the prior year), against **5.2%** for annual and **28.9%** for high-priced monthly; **Productivity monthly reaches 36.1%** (RevenueCat, 115k apps, 2026 — **mobile IAP**). RevenueCat's framing is the transferable part: *"Users reactivate when the problem comes back, not when your win-back email lands."* The operational corollary from the same lane: **always hold out 10–20% of the targeted cohort**, because with a ~20% organic return rate an un-held-out campaign will report a 20% "success rate" while doing nothing.
- **evidence**: `medium` for the reactivation base rates (very large N, mobile-only); `anecdote` for the timing tactic itself
- **sources**: https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/20-of-your-churned-users-will-come-back-but-are-you-ready · https://revenuecat.github.io/codelabs/monetization-success-formula/reactivation/
- **how-to**: Identify the calendar your buyer already keeps and name it in the offer, so the deadline is theirs rather than yours. Hold out a control cohort on any timed campaign. And separate the two questions: whether the season is real is a fact about the buyer; whether the offer is good is a fact about the offer, and a seasonal spike will flatter a bad one.
- **example-copy**: "Quarterly filing is due on the 15th. If you're doing three months of receipts this weekend, this is the weekend Ledgerly is worth $9."
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's buyer has four real deadlines a year, set by a tax authority rather than by the founder. Those four moments are the only defensible timing in the product's whole calendar — and note that the copy above does not need a discount, a countdown or a badge, because the urgency is already installed in the reader's life.

### P-216 — Read the last-hours sales spike as deadline compliance, not as proof urgency works

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: Sales cluster at the end of any promotion, and the cluster is routinely presented as evidence that urgency creates demand. It is at least equally consistent with a duller explanation: buyers who had already decided complete at the deadline, because a deadline schedules an action rather than causing one. Distinguishing the two requires a counterfactual — the same offer without the deadline — which almost nobody runs, and the difference matters because only one of the two readings justifies extending the tactic to buyers who have *not* decided.
- **works-when**: Interpreting your own promotion's shape, or anyone's published one.
- **fails-when**: You use the spike to justify escalating urgency. If the mechanism is compliance, escalation buys nothing and spends benevolence (P-202); if the mechanism is persuasion, escalation still spends benevolence but at least buys something. You cannot tell which you are in from the spike itself.
- **observed-effect**: The canonical claim is Hormozi's: the biggest sales come in the last four hours of a promotion's final day, **up to 50–60% of volume** — his own anecdote, with no cohort, sample, product or counterfactual. No source in this corpus publishes a with-deadline-versus-without-deadline comparison on the same offer.
- **evidence**: `anecdote`
- **sources**: https://www.gregfaxon.com/blog/100m-offers-summary · https://wisewords.blog/book-summaries/100m-offers-book-summary/
- **how-to**: When a promotion clusters at the deadline, ask what fraction of the closing cohort had already visited the pricing page before the promotion started. A high fraction means you scheduled existing intent — good, cheap, and no argument for more urgency. A low fraction means the deadline recruited new buyers, which is the only reading that supports doing it again, and which you should verify before believing.
- **example-copy**: n/a (analytical)
- **worked-example**: If Ledgerly's founder ladder fills its last eight seats in the final two days, the founder checks how many of those eight had visited in the previous month. If seven had, the ladder did not persuade anybody — it gave seven people who were already interested a reason to stop deferring, which is a perfectly good outcome and a bad reason to add a countdown timer.

### P-217 — Put repricing on a schedule so you never need manufactured urgency

- **layer**: `urgency`
- **mechanism**: Most fake urgency is a symptom. A founder who has not raised prices in eighteen months, whose conversion is flat, and who has no scheduled mechanism for changing anything, reaches for a countdown because it is the only lever that appears to be available. A standing repricing cadence supplies the real levers — an announced increase, a new rung, a repackaged tier — each of which generates genuine, defensible urgency as a by-product, on a date that already existed.
- **works-when**: You can commit to a review interval and actually hold it, and you have the counter-metrics to read the result (renewal, refund, day-90 cancellation).
- **fails-when**: Cadence becomes churn: the same 2026 survey that reports pricing has a short shelf life also reports customers pushing back on credit models precisely because bills became unpredictable. **Frequent repricing and customer trust are in direct tension and no source in this corpus offers a rule for resolving it.** Also fails if reviews happen and nothing changes, which trains you to ignore them.
- **observed-effect**: The **PricingSaaS 500 averaged 3.6 pricing/packaging changes per company in 2025** — **B2B software, and it must be labelled as such at the point of use**; Lovable made roughly one meaningful update per month while scaling to $200M ARR, which is the same cohort at its fastest. Poyar's position is that pricing now has roughly a six-month shelf life, also stated about B2B. **No source in this corpus publishes a repricing cadence for a sub-$30 consumer subscription**, so the quarterly review prescribed below is chosen for being the slowest cadence that still catches a cost-to-serve change, not because a consumer benchmark supports it. Counterweight from the same survey generation: unpredictable billing is a top reported complaint, and **78% of IT leaders reported surprise AI-related charges in the past year** (Zylo 2026 SaaS Management Index, n=218).
- **evidence**: `medium` *(prevalence is well-sourced; the "cadence prevents fake urgency" claim is this library's inference, not a measured finding)*
- **sources**: L10 AI-pricing lane (PricingSaaS 500, 2025) · https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/2025-state-of-b2b-monetization
- **how-to**: Put a quarterly pricing review on the calendar with three fixed inputs: cost-to-serve, the usage distribution's top 5%, and the last quarter's refund and day-90 cancellation rates. Decide the change at the review and announce it with a date (P-207). If the review produces no change three times running, that is a finding about the market, not a reason to ship a timer.
- **example-copy**: n/a (operational)
- **worked-example**: Ledgerly's quarterly review in October finds median usage at 31 receipts against a 100-receipt Solo allowance and a top-5% cohort at 240 — which is a packaging finding (the allowance is not the fence anyone hits) rather than a price one. The resulting change is a repackaged Solo and a dated announcement, and neither the founder nor the page ever needs to invent a reason to buy today.

## Interactions

**P-202 (the trust cost) governs every other pattern in this layer.** It is not one consideration among several; it is the price attached to all of them. Any pattern here read without it becomes a licence, which is exactly how this material is normally transmitted and exactly what makes it dangerous for a subscription business.

**P-202/P-203 versus `experimentation` (Layer 12), specifically P-292.** Layer 12 already requires reading results at the revenue horizon rather than at checkout. Urgency is the case where that rule bites hardest, because the relevant horizon is not the revenue horizon either — it is the *trust* horizon, which is longer still and has no metric. Where P-292 says extend the window, this layer says accept that even the extended window is too short, and fall back on mechanism.

**P-204 (real deadlines) versus P-209 (the launch ladder) versus P-206 (constrain the offer).** These are the three honest deadlines available to a self-serve product, and they are ranked. The ladder is best because the constraint is arithmetic and public. The capacity-bounded offer is next because the constraint is your own time. A bare announced date is last because it depends entirely on your follow-through, which the buyer cannot verify in advance. Prefer in that order.

**P-207 (announce the increase) versus P-208 (grandfather) versus `price-points` (Layer 04).** The increase is a pricing decision; the announcement and the grandfather are urgency and fairness decisions layered on top of it. They must be decided together — announcing an increase without a grandfather policy converts a routine change into a loyalty question, and grandfathering without a stated period converts it into a promise you will break.

**P-211 (show the discount) versus P-212 (do not stack) versus `page-anatomy` (Layer 05), P-129.** P-129 requires anchoring against a price you actually charge; P-211 supplies the one construction that guarantees this (the ladder's own former price); P-212 forbids combining it with anything else in the decision zone. Read as a single instruction: **one true anchor, one true deadline, nothing else.**

**P-213 (judge at renewal) versus `expansion-retention` (Layer 11).** The promotional cohort's renewal is where this layer hands off. Layer 11 owns what happens at that renewal — the reminder, the save flow, the win-back. The handoff rule is the one both layers agree on: **never present a discount inside an active cancel flow**, because that is the moment a promotion stops being an offer and starts being a retention tax on your most price-sensitive customers.

**P-214 (no urgency without a trust balance) versus `trust-and-proof` (Layer 09), P-236.** These are the same constraint from opposite sides. P-236 says a pre-launch page will be visibly thinner than a competitor's and that the thinness is correct. P-214 says the temptation at that moment is to fill the gap with urgency, and that doing so consumes the one asset the thin page has. A pre-launch page should read as honest and sparse, not as urgent and empty.

**P-215 (the buyer's calendar) versus P-216 (deadline compliance).** Both are about reading a spike correctly. A seasonal spike and a deadline spike look identical on a chart and mean different things: the first is demand arriving, the second is decided buyers completing. Attributing a seasonal spike to your promotion is the most common way a founder concludes that urgency works.

## Where this layer is thin

**The conversion side of this layer is essentially unmeasured, and that asymmetry is the layer's most important property.** Every quantified urgency lift located in this research is either unattributed (the 9.3% timer claim, the 23–41% repeat-purchase claim), an operator anecdote with no counterfactual (Hormozi's final-four-hours figure), or confounded with the size of the discount rather than its presentation (GoodUI test #167, where a 50% discount is doing the work). Meanwhile the trust cost has one controlled study. **Anyone reading this layer expecting a list of tactics with effect sizes should understand that the effect sizes do not exist.**

**Tuncer et al. is one study, n=202, on an e-commerce mock-up, and this corpus never read it.** It is the best evidence in the layer and it arrives entirely through two secondary carriers, a Substack summary and an agency blog, which is why P-202 now carries `medium` rather than `strong`. It is also still a single laboratory-style experiment on a simulated retail site, measuring attitudes rather than purchases, with no subscription context and no longitudinal component. The finding this layer leans on hardest — that *honest* cues also cost benevolence — is the part most worth replicating and the part nobody has replicated.

**No one has measured the downstream cost of urgency in a subscription.** The entire argument of P-202 and P-203 is that the cost lands at months three to twelve. **No study in this corpus follows an urgency-exposed cohort that far**, in any category. The MSN Video result is the only long-horizon monetisation test located anywhere in the research and it is about ad placement, not urgency. The transfer is a reasoned one, not a measured one, and it should be labelled as such in any deliverable.

**The regulatory position on reference pricing and promotional claims was not verified.** P-211 and the Folklore table both flag EU/UK Omnibus prior-price substantiation and FTC pricing guidance without having fetched either text. A follow-up needs the current Directive 98/6/EC Art. 6a language as amended by 2019/2161, current FTC guidance on former-price claims, and whatever state-level equivalents apply. **Do not ship a struck-through price to EU or UK buyers on the strength of this layer alone.**

**Launch and founder pricing has no published evidence at all.** P-209 is built entirely on the price-derivation lane's methodological recommendation (use cohort pricing instead of live price A/B tests) plus the Amazon 2000 cautionary precedent. **Nobody has published conversion, retention or willingness-to-pay data from a public price ladder.** How fast rungs fill, whether the visible count matters, whether grandfathered cohorts retain differently — all unmeasured.

**Seasonal offers are the thinnest section here.** P-215 borrows reactivation-timing data from a mobile IAP cohort and applies it, by analogy, to acquisition timing on web. There is no data anywhere in this research on seasonal promotion performance for software subscriptions, no benchmark for how much a genuinely seasonal moment is worth, and no guidance on the products that have no season.

**Price-increase execution is undocumented beyond convention.** The 6–12 month grandfathering window is practice, not measurement. There is no published data on how much notice is enough, what fraction of a grandfathered cohort churns at the transition, whether announcing an increase produces a pre-raise spike large enough to matter, or how any of this differs by price point. For a sub-$30/mo consumer product the whole area is uncharted.

**The mobile-vs-web warning applies to two patterns here.** P-213's discount-prevalence figures (9 in 10 sold at full price; 9.3% using promotional offers) and P-215's reactivation rates are **App Store / Google Play in-app-purchase artifacts** from RevenueCat and Adapty's 2026 cohorts. Store promotional-offer types, store win-back offers and store-mediated cancellation have no direct Stripe analogue; a web product has direct email access to churned users that the store cohorts historically did not, so the web ceiling is plausibly different in both directions and **this data cannot tell you which**.

**Nothing here is testable at COLD traffic.** At ~2,000 monthly visitors, no urgency device can be evaluated on conversion, let alone on the horizon that matters. The layer is therefore applied as mechanism and brand fit, and its practical output for a small product is mostly a list of things not to ship.
