# Confident Expert

## 1. Identity
The voice of someone who has done this a thousand times and can prove it — direct, precise, numbers-forward, allergic to vagueness. It persuades with mechanism and evidence, not enthusiasm. (The YNAB / Basecamp register.) **Use for:** finance, productivity, fitness-with-a-method, developer/pro tools, any audience burned by hype who wants to know *how it works*. **Avoid for:** audiences arriving anxious or ashamed (reads as cold), or apps whose honest evidence base is thin — this tone with no numbers is a bluff.

## 2. Voice attributes
- Makes a claim, then immediately backs it: number, mechanism, or named method.
- Specific over general, always: "9 weeks," never "in no time."
- Declarative sentences; no hedging ("we think," "may help") and no gushing.
- Comfortable telling the visitor what *won't* work — naming the failure mode of alternatives is this tone's trust move.
- Verbs over adjectives: what the app *does*, not what it *is*.
- Respects the reader's intelligence; never oversells a small feature.

## 3. Rhythm rules
Sentences 8–18 words. Periods and the occasional colon-setup ("The result: …"); no exclamation points. Fragments only for emphasis after a proven claim ("Nine weeks. That's the plan."). Two to three sentences per paragraph.

## 4. Vocabulary
**Favor:** exactly, measured, built on, adjusts, the plan, works, proven-by-numbers phrasing ("12,400 runners", "every 30 seconds").
**Banned in this tone:** amazing, magical, delightful, love (as in "you'll love it"), "trust us", superlatives without a cited number ("the best", "#1") — unless the ranking is real and sourced.

## 5. Example rewrites
- Hero headline: "Track your runs and build a running habit." → **"The beginner running plan that actually sticks."**
- Subhead: "Trailhead helps beginners go from couch to consistent runner with guided plans." → **"Trailhead takes new runners from zero to 5K in nine weeks, using run-walk intervals — the method with decades of results behind it."**
- Feature blurb: "Guided audio runs tell you when to run and when to walk." → **"Audio cues call every interval — run, walk, repeat — timed to your current fitness, not a generic schedule."**
- FAQ answer: "Trailhead starts easier than you expect and adjusts when you miss a day." → **"Most running plans fail because they ramp too fast. Trailhead recalculates your next run every time you miss one — so week three never breaks you."**
- CTA: "Download Trailhead on the App Store." → **"Get Trailhead. Start week one today."**

## 6. Anti-patterns
- **Jargon flex** — "periodized mesocycle progression": expertise shown through vocabulary instead of clarity. A real expert makes it sound simple.
- **Unbacked swagger** — "the smartest running app ever built": confidence without a number or mechanism is just hype in a suit.
- **Lecture mode** — three paragraphs of exercise science before the promise. Evidence serves the claim; it never replaces it.
- **Coldness** — precision without any acknowledgment of the reader's situation reads robotic. One line of recognition ("most plans fail because…") keeps it human.

## 7. Readability note
The highest-risk pack for the 8th-grade rule: mechanism talk invites jargon and long clauses. Resolution: every technical term gets swapped for its plain name ("run-walk intervals," not "polarized interval methodology"), and any sentence explaining *how* stays under 20 words or gets split. Numbers are readability's friend — digits read easier than the words around them.
