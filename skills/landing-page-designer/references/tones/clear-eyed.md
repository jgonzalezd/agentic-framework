# Clear-Eyed

## 1. Identity
The voice of someone who tells you the truth you already half-know — calmly, once, and then trusts you to handle it. Direct without being cold, warm without padding. It never soothes and never sells; it names what's real and hands the decision back. (The register of a good mentor's last five minutes with you.) **Use for:** apps that help people make honest calls about emotionally loaded parts of their own life — relationships, habits, identity, money they're avoiding — for problem-aware, growth-driven audiences who distrust both hype and coddling. **Avoid for:** audiences who arrive anxious and need soothing before they can read (sleep, panic, grief), and playful or low-stakes domains where plain truth-telling reads as heavy.

## 2. Voice attributes
- Says the hard thing plainly, exactly once — no euphemism, and no repeating it for drama.
- Second person always; contractions always.
- The reader is the decider. The product shows; the reader judges. Copy never renders a verdict on the reader's behalf.
- Names both directions of a truth (what you give and what you get; what you'd keep and what you'd lose) — one-sided claims read as sales.
- At most one acknowledging clause before the point. Reassurance padding is cut.
- Growth framing stays concrete ("the person you're trying not to be anymore"), never motivational-poster.
- Points at what the reader will see, not at how they should feel about the brand.

## 3. Rhythm rules
Sentences 6–16 words, declarative. A short fragment is allowed to land a hard truth ("You might be."). No exclamation points, ever. Em-dashes for the turn in a sentence; no ellipses. One to three sentences per paragraph.

## 4. Vocabulary
**Favor:** honest, actually, pattern, over time, both, decide, keep, let go, notice, "you already know", plain nouns (people, months, notes, the answer).
**Banned in this tone** (on top of the global list): toxic, vampire, "self-care", "boundaries" (therapy-speak), "it's okay" and "you're allowed" (permission-giving belongs to a different voice), "be honest with yourself" (cornering), any intensifier doing an adjective's job (really, truly, incredibly).

## 5. Example rewrites
- Hero headline: "Track your runs and build a running habit." → **"You don't need motivation. You need a plan that survives week three."**
- Subhead: "Trailhead helps beginners go from couch to consistent runner with guided plans." → **"Trailhead takes you from your first walk-run to a steady habit. It plans around the week you actually have, not the week you meant to have."**
- Feature blurb: "Guided audio runs tell you when to run and when to walk." → **"Audio cues call the run and the walk. Your only job is to keep moving."**
- FAQ answer: "Trailhead starts easier than you expect and adjusts when you miss a day." → **"You didn't quit because you're lazy. You quit because the plan ignored your life. Trailhead rebuilds around every run you miss."**
- CTA: "Download Trailhead on the App Store." → **"Run this week, not someday."**

## 6. Anti-patterns
- **Verdict-grabbing** — "Time to drop the friends holding you back": telling the reader what to decide. This voice lays out evidence and stops; commanding the conclusion breaks the trust that plainness built.
- **Tough-love cosplay** — "Stop lying to yourself": harshness performed for effect. Clear-eyed lands because it's calm and plain, not loud. If a line corners the reader, it's the wrong line.
- **Coach drift** — "show up for your best self": borrowed motivation-speak. One poster phrase and the whole register collapses into every other app.
- **Cold audit** — "optimize your relationship portfolio": when the subject is people and a life, report language reads inhuman. Nouns stay human: people, friends, months, notes.

## 7. Readability note
Plain declaratives keep this pack naturally under 8th grade. The one risk is compressing a hard truth into an abstraction ("the relationship's trajectory") — abstraction is where this voice goes to hide, and it fails the rule in spirit. Resolution: every abstract noun gets swapped for the thing the reader would actually see ("which way it's been heading").
