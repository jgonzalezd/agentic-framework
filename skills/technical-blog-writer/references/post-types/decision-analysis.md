# Decision Analysis

> "Here were the real options, here is the constraint that decided it, here is what would make me reverse it."

---

## Choose this when

There was a genuine fork: at least 2 approaches that a competent engineer could have chosen, and a specific reason one won. The author can name what the losing option would have cost.

This is the post type that most directly demonstrates staff-level judgment, because judgment is visible only where a decision could have gone the other way.

## Do not choose this when

- One option was obviously correct. That is not a decision, it is a step.
- The alternatives were never seriously considered. Reconstructing them after the fact produces strawmen, which this audience recognises instantly.
- The decision has not been lived with. A choice made last week has no evidence behind it yet.

## Required ledger rows

1. At least 2 real alternatives, with evidence they were actually considered: a spike, a branch, a note, a prototype that was deleted, a dated discussion.
2. The deciding constraint, stated concretely.
3. What killed the loser, with the artifact behind it: a measurement, a failed prototype, a cost, a limit that was hit.
4. The reversal condition: what would have to change for the other option to become correct.

## Anatomy

1. **The decision, stated in 1 sentence**, at the top. This post type does not build suspense; the reader needs to know what is being argued before they can evaluate the argument. Mandatory.
2. **The real alternatives.** Each one described as its own advocate would describe it, in enough detail that a reader who prefers it feels represented. Mandatory, and the section where the post earns or loses its credibility.
3. **The constraint that decided it.** Time, scale, team size, an existing system, a compliance requirement, an operational cost. Mandatory.
4. **What killed the loser.** The evidence, not the intuition. If the answer was intuition, say that.
5. **What the choice cost.** Every decision buys something and pays for it. A post that describes only the upside is an advertisement.
6. **What would reverse it.** Mandatory. A decision with no stated reversal condition is a preference.

## The thesis shape

A conditional claim: under these conditions, this option wins, and here is where the condition breaks.

Example: "Below roughly 5 engineers, a shared database between 2 services costs less than the coordination it removes, and the point where that stops being true is the first time 2 people need to change the same table in the same week."

## Failure mode

**The strawman alternative.** The losing option is described in terms only its critics would use, so the decision looks inevitable. The check: read the alternatives section as though you had already chosen the losing option. If it reads as unfair, rewrite it until it does not.

Second failure: **retroactive certainty.** The post implies the author knew all this at the time. If the decision was made on incomplete information, say what was not known then. That sentence is worth more than the rest of the section.

## Length

800 to 1,500 words. The alternatives section needs room; compressing it is what produces strawmen.
