# Audience Pack — Customers

## What they're actually buying

**A better version of themselves.** Not the product. The product is the toll they pay to get there, and every sentence they read is them asking, silently: *is this about me, and does it get me somewhere I want to be?*

This has a hard consequence. The customer does not care that the product exists, who built it, how it works, or how big the market is. They care about a problem they already have, in words they already use. Everything else is friction.

The person you're writing to is usually **problem-aware**: they feel the pain and have half-named it themselves, but they don't know a tool for it exists. That's the default assumption unless the context says otherwise. It means the description opens on the problem, not on the product.

---

## Section skeleton

Draft all six. Cut later if the format demands brevity — but draft them all first, because each one does a job the others can't.

**1. Headline** — the promise, in the person's own frame. One line. It names the shift, not the software.

**2. Sub-headline** — what the thing is, plainly, in one sentence. This is where the product finally gets named and categorized ("a private journal for…", "a planner for…"). It exists to stop the reader wondering *but what IS it*.

**3. The problem** — three to five lines that describe their situation so accurately it's uncomfortable. This is the section that decides everything. If they don't recognize themselves here, nothing below gets read. Mine this from real language; never write it from imagination.

**4. How it works** — three to five capabilities, each rendered as **the benefit it produces**, not the feature that produces it. Format: a short bolded label naming the outcome, then a line or two of what the person actually does and gets. The mechanic can appear — it just can't lead.

**5. The value, in their terms** — the quantified value proposition, translated. Their units are time, energy, stress, money, and avoided regret; never DAU, retention, or engagement. If there's no measured number yet, this is a bracketed placeholder — the *shape* of the claim with the number missing is still useful, and it tells the founder exactly what to go measure.

**6. Call to action** — one action, named as what happens when they take it. Include price and trial terms if they exist; hiding price is a short-term win and a long-term trust cost.

---

## Register

- **Second person.** "You" throughout. The moment it becomes "users", the reader is watching someone else's product.
- **Plain verbs, short sentences.** 8th-grade readability is a floor, not a style preference — it's how people read when they're skimming and unconvinced.
- **Concrete over abstract.** "the friend who's great for a hike and terrible for politics" beats "relationship-specific compatibility mapping."
- **Sentence case. No exclamation marks.** Enthusiasm in punctuation reads as a sales pitch, which is what they're bracing for.
- **Specific beats clever.** A clever line gets admired. A specific line gets recognized. Only recognition converts.

---

## Disqualifiers — instant loss

- Architecture, stack, data model, AI-model names, or anything about how it's built.
- Market size, CAC, LTV, retention, funding, "we're disrupting".
- Framework vocabulary: beachhead, persona, QVP, value proposition, segment, user journey.
- A feature list with no benefit attached — "tracks X, logs Y, syncs Z".
- Anything that makes the reader feel like a demographic rather than a person.
- Invented social proof of any kind: testimonials, star counts, "join 10,000 users". If it isn't real, it isn't there.

---

## Failure modes to watch for

**The feature ladder.** Writing what it does instead of what it's for, one bullet at a time. Test: for each capability, ask "so what?" until you hit something the person would say about their own life. Write that.

**The clever headline.** A line that makes the founder proud and the reader indifferent. Test: would a real person in the target situation say "that's me" — or "that's cute"?

**The uncomfortable mechanic, euphemized.** Some products do something that sounds cold said plainly: ranking people, cutting people off, scoring performance, tracking a habit someone's ashamed of. The instinct is to bury it in soft language. Don't — the reader can feel the gap and stops trusting the page.

The job is to state the honest benefit in the words the person already uses *about themselves* when they're being honest. Someone who wants to stop giving time to a draining friend doesn't think "I should trim my network." They think "I always leave that hangout wrecked and I can't say why." Write the second one. The mechanic stays visible; only the framing changes — from what the software does to what the person gets.

**Writing for the founder's pride.** The description that lists everything built is a description for the person who built it. Cut to what the reader needs to decide.

## Worked example

*Illustrative only — never a template. The fictional product below must never appear in a deliverable.*

**Product:** *Sundial* — an app for night-shift workers to plan sleep around rotating schedules.

**Feature-first (wrong):**
> Sundial uses a circadian-modeling engine to generate optimized sleep windows from your shift roster, with adaptive rescheduling and a local-first sync architecture.

**Benefit-first (right):**

> **Headline:** Sleep like your schedule isn't fighting you.
>
> **Sub-headline:** Sundial turns your shift roster into a sleep plan you can actually follow.
>
> **The problem:** Your shifts rotate, so your sleep never settles. You lie down exhausted at 9am and stare at the ceiling. You give up a day off to catch up and lose the evening anyway. Everyone's advice assumes you work days. By Thursday you're running on four hours and calling it normal.
>
> **How it works:**
> - **Know when to lie down.** Put in your roster once. Sundial tells you the sleep window that fits the shift you're actually working, not the one a day-worker would have.
> - **Stop losing your days off.** It plans the shift back to daylight, so a rest day is a rest day and not a recovery day.
> - **See what's actually costing you.** Log how you slept and Sundial shows which shift patterns wreck you, so you can trade the worst ones.
>
> **The value:** [hours of sleep gained per week — measure with the first 20 beta users before claiming a number.] What you can say today without a number: you stop guessing when to sleep, and you stop burning rest days recovering from the last one.
>
> **CTA:** Add your next two weeks of shifts and see your sleep plan. Free for the first month, then [price — not set].

Note what happened: the circadian engine is still in there — it's just called "Sundial tells you the sleep window that fits the shift you're actually working." And the value section is *empty on purpose*, with the measurement named. That bracket is the honest version, and it doubles as the founder's next task.
