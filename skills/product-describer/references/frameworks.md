# Frameworks

Read this when writing margin notes in synthesized mode, or when running **per-framework mode** (the user explicitly asked for one lens, or for a comparison).

Four sources. They agree on the premise — describe the product by what it does for the listener, never by what it is — and each contributes a distinct piece of the machinery.

---

## Bill Aulet — *Disciplined Entrepreneurship*

The most useful of the four for **structuring** a description, because it names the specific artifacts.

- **Step 7 — High-Level Product Specification.** Describe the product as the customer experiences it: a storyboard, a wireframe, a brochure. Explicitly non-technical. The test is whether the persona can see themselves using it. This is where "never lead with architecture" comes from.
- **Step 8 — Quantified Value Proposition.** The value stated in the customer's own units — time saved, money saved, stress avoided, risk removed — with a number attached. The number is the point; "makes your life better" is not a QVP. When there's no measurement yet, the *shape* of the QVP with a bracketed number still does work: it tells the founder exactly what to go measure.
- **Step 10 — The Core.** The one thing that makes this defensible — the reason a competitor can't simply copy the product. Aulet is strict here: it is not your features, not your UX, not being first. Standards for what qualifies are in `audiences/investors.md`.
- **Beachhead market.** The narrowest homogeneous group who feels the pain most and buys first. A beachhead that sounds too small is usually correct.

**Contributes to the synthesized skeleton:** the product section (customer-experienced, not technical), the value section in both packs, and the Core section for investors.

---

## Eric Ries — *The Lean Startup*

Contributes **posture** more than structure — how to talk about a product that isn't finished, which is the situation most descriptions are written in.

- **Product as hypothesis, not artifact.** Pre-launch, everything stated about the product is a claim awaiting evidence. Describing it as settled fact is the error.
- **Value hypothesis** — will people actually use and keep using this? **Growth hypothesis** — how does it spread? These are separate claims and get separate evidence.
- **MVP** — the smallest thing that produces learning. Describing the MVP honestly ("we built only the logging, to see if people return") is more credible than describing the full vision as if it shipped.
- **Actionable vs vanity metrics.** Downloads, signups, and impressions are vanity. Cohort retention, activation rate, and conversion are actionable. An investor description that cites only vanity metrics reads as evasion.
- **Pivot or persevere.** Naming what would make you change course signals that the founder has a falsifiable model rather than a conviction.

**Contributes to the synthesized skeleton:** the "traction and what's next" section, the honest-stage rule, and the framing of any unmeasured number as a hypothesis with a test attached.

---

## Steve Blank & Bob Dorf — *The Startup Owner's Manual*

Contributes the **investor's mental model**: the product is one block in a business model, not the business.

- **Business Model Canvas** as the frame. Value proposition is one of nine blocks; customer segments, channels, and revenue streams carry equal weight. An investor description that covers only the value proposition has answered one ninth of the question.
- **Earlyvangelists** — early adopters who feel the pain acutely enough to accept an unfinished product. Describing who they are, specifically, is more convincing than describing the total market.
- **Problem/solution fit before product/market fit.** These are different milestones and conflating them is a common stage inflation. Problem/solution fit: people confirm the pain is real and your approach would address it. Product/market fit: people actually use it and keep using it and tell others.
- **A startup is a temporary organization searching for a repeatable, scalable business model** — not a small version of a big company. What you're claiming to an investor is that the search is converging.

**Contributes to the synthesized skeleton:** the market + beachhead section, the business model section, and the discipline of naming which fit milestone has actually been reached.

---

## Steve Blank — Customer Development (*The Four Steps to the Epiphany*)

Contributes the **customer-facing method**.

- **Get out of the building.** Nothing about the customer is known from inside the office. This is the origin of the mined-not-invented rule: a description written from imagination is a guess wearing the costume of a fact.
- **Describe solutions to validated problems, not features.** The unit of description is a problem the customer has confirmed, plus what you do about it.
- **End in a testable question.** A customer description in discovery mode should close with *"does this sound like a problem you have?"* — because its job is to produce learning, not a sale. This is the single most useful move for a pre-launch product and the one founders most often skip.

**Contributes to the synthesized skeleton:** the problem section in the customer pack, the recognition gate in Phase 5, and — for pre-launch products — the option of closing the customer description with the discovery question instead of a CTA.

## Per-framework mode

When the user asks for a specific lens, produce that framework's **customer + investor pair**, with its reasoning visible — that means:

1. A short opening on what this framework says about describing products at all.
2. The customer description, in that framework's terms, with a "why this works in this framework" note after it.
3. The investor description, same treatment.

All global rules still apply — most importantly, **numbers are still sourced or bracketed**. A framework lens changes the structure and vocabulary of a description, never its honesty.

When asked to **compare lenses**, run the pairs and add a closing section on what each framework would tell this specific founder to do next. That's usually the most useful output of the comparison: Aulet says quantify the value, Ries says name the riskiest assumption, Blank says go talk to twenty people. Which of those is the real bottleneck is the answer worth having.
